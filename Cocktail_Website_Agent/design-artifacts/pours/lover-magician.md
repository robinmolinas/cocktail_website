# Pour — Worth Finding · The Charmer (lover-magician)

## Cocktail

- **name:** Worth Finding
- **tagline:** Getting to know you feels like finding something nobody else has found.
- **glassware:** small stemmed cocktail glass (about 150 ml), chilled, no ice
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 30 ml | shochu | Japanese barley or rice shochu, about 25% (25% or stronger); ask for shochu, not soju, which is a different Korean spirit |
| 37.5 ml | London dry gin | any London dry; 45% or stronger keeps it fully in range (40-43% makes it a touch softer) |
| 25 ml | blanc vermouth | the pale, lightly sweet style first made in Chambéry, France (also sold as bianco), not a dry vermouth |
| ½ tsp (2.5 ml) | rich sugar syrup | homemade: 2 parts white sugar to 1 part hot water, by weight |
| 1 | fresh green shiso leaf | torn once, stirred with the drink, strained out, and set on the table beside the glass |

**method**
1. Put a small stemmed cocktail glass in the freezer.
2. If you don't have rich syrup: stir 100 g of white sugar into 50 g of hot water until it's clear, and let it cool (it keeps about a month in the fridge).
3. Tear one green shiso leaf once across and drop it into a mixing glass or a jar.
4. Add the shochu, the gin, the blanc vermouth and ½ teaspoon of the syrup.
5. Fill the mixing glass with ice and stir steadily for about 30 seconds, until the outside is very cold.
6. Strain into the cold glass. Nothing goes on the drink.
7. Lift the leaf out of the strainer and put it on a small plate beside the glass as you serve the drink, before anyone asks what's in it.

**closingLine:** *Put the leaf on the table as you serve it. Then give away the thing you'd have kept for last.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*It starts at a bar upstairs, behind a restaurant. Its shiso hides behind the gin.*

**whoYouAre**
You're easy to talk to and hard to finish. After an evening with you, people have said more than they meant to, and know a little less about you than they'd like. So they come back. Each time you give them a bit more: a story you don't tell at work, a look that says you noticed, a door left slightly open.

Here's the part they miss. You don't keep something back to be chased. You keep it back so there's still a reason to stay. At some point you decided that once someone has seen all of you, the finding is over, and they'll go. So there's always one thing you haven't said yet. And it works: people feel they've found something in you that no one else has. They're right. That's what you hand them: a sense of discovery most people never get from anyone. It's worth a great deal. But behind that last thing, you're on your own.

**yours**

1. In 1994, on a second floor in New York's East Village, behind a door at the back of a Japanese restaurant, Angel's Share opened. Its owner wanted a Tokyo-style bar: small, intimate and formal. There was no small talk, partly because many of the bartenders had little English, and no standing or big groups. If you found the door, you'd found something.

2. Two years in, the people there didn't really know how to run it. Shinichi Ikeda had come over from Japan to play music. He'd been a bartender back home, and one day the owner told him to run the bar. Ikeda said the owner somehow knew. I like to think that's the moment you'd notice: someone saw what he hadn't put forward, and put it to work.

3. The writer who told its story says no New York bar opening in the 1990s mattered more to the cocktail revival's future. Yet it didn't expand or put out a cocktail book, and the movement it helped inspire largely forgot it. Shingo Gokan, who came from Tokyo in 2006, worked there six years before he had a single friend who was a bartender. In his words, they were always outside. The same writer is careful to add that the door was shut from both sides. I recognise you in that. What you hold back stays whole, and it keeps you just outside.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Martini, the renowned classic, built from two things Ikeda brought to that bar: Martinis stirred the way he'd learned them in Japan, and new drinks with Japanese ingredients like shochu and shiso leaf. Joining them in one glass is my idea. Shochu is a clear Japanese spirit with less alcohol than gin, so gin takes the lead, with a pale vermouth. A torn green shiso leaf is stirred with them, then lifted out and set beside the glass as it's served, as a nod to that door at the back. Shiso and juniper share a compound with a cedar-and-pine scent, and in the glass the shiso slips in behind the gin, and what you get is clean, cold and green in a way you can't quite place. The leaf is there to be handed over.

5. So tell one person what you've been saving. Not when they've earned it: before. You're afraid there'll be nothing left to stay for. I don't think that's how it works with you. Being known completely is the one kind of closeness you've always kept from yourself, and it's the kind that doesn't need a reason to stay. They'll stay anyway. Let yourself find out.
