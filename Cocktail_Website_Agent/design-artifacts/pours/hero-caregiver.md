# Pour — Next One's Mine · The Saviour (hero-caregiver)

## Cocktail

- **name:** Next One's Mine
- **tagline:** You'll take anything on for anyone. Except a favour.
- **glassware:** tall glass (highball, about 350 ml), filled with ice
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 45 ml | straight rye whiskey | |
| 45 ml | sweet (Italian red) vermouth | the same measure as the rye: half the weight is the vermouth's |
| 2 dashes | Angostura bitters | |
| 120 ml | soda water, cold | added last |
| 1 strip | lemon peel | |

**method**
1. Fill a tall glass to the top with ice.
2. Measure 45 ml of rye into the glass. Then fill the same jigger to the same line with sweet vermouth and pour that in too.
3. Shake in the Angostura (two dashes) and stir five or six turns with a long spoon, just to chill.
4. Top with the cold soda water, then lift the drink once, gently, with the spoon.
5. Squeeze a strip of lemon peel over the top of the glass so its oils fall on the drink, then drop it in.

**closingLine:** *Let someone get this one. Don't get the next.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*The vermouth in this glass is there because hardly anyone stopped after the first.*

**whoYouAre**
The bill nobody can split, the call nobody wants to make, the mistake that was only half theirs: it's landed on someone near you. You don't rush. You weigh it up, quietly, and then you say it: "Put it on me." And they go home lighter. That's the kindness in you, and it counts for more than you'll ever let anyone say.

The other half is harder. Someone does you a small kindness, a lift, a meal, an evening of their time, and it's paid back within the week, with a little extra. Owing anyone, even briefly, feels like weakness to you, like leaving a door open that someone could walk through. So your books are always in credit. Plenty of people owe you something. You've never asked for any of it back.

**yours**

1. In 1919, months before national Prohibition began, a wartime law had already made selling drink illegal. That year, the Greenwich Village Inn in New York, which a man named Barney Gallant ran, was raided, and he was arrested along with half a dozen of his waiters. Barney agreed to plead guilty on one condition: that his waiters were let go.

2. He was sentenced to thirty days in jail, and he served far less. Yes, his name really was Gallant. You could say it was his place to answer for, and maybe it was simply good business. But I like to think it was more than that: that he looked at his waiters, who'd only been carrying the trays, and decided the cost was his.

3. You'd have made the same decision, and you'd have called it only fair. What I've always wondered is how it felt for those waiters, going home owing someone that much. You've stood where Barney stood more times than you can count. You've never let yourself stand where they did.

4. The cocktail I've made for you comes out of the old saloons, where people at the bar drank in rounds, taking turns to pay, and hardly anyone stopped after the first. A strong drink was too much for that, and one answer was to split the whisky half and half with vermouth, a wine flavoured with herbs: that's where the Manhattan, a renowned classic, and its family come from. So I kept the equal split, the way the Manhattan's early recipes often had it: rye and sweet vermouth, poured to the same line twice, with bitters, then let it run long over ice with soda and a twist of lemon. The rye dry, the vermouth dark and bittersweet, all of it cold and bright. I kept the split for what you won't allow yourself: something else carrying half the weight, so everyone can keep going.

5. So when someone does something for you, don't pay it back. No dinner in return, no bigger present, no "I'll get you back for this". Every time you square it straight away, you take something from them: the feeling you get every time you say "put it on me". Let them keep that. And for once, you get to be the one who's let off.

**closing line (Tomás's, spec v1):** *Let someone get this one. Don't get the next.* Read against the reading: it agrees with the position (y5), not the creed; it's bar-sized where y5 is life-sized, so they don't repeat each other; and it answers the name.
