# Pour — On Their Behalf · The Perfectionist (ruler-creator)

## Cocktail

- **name:** On Their Behalf
- **tagline:** Your standards are a promise to the person who'll never know what you turned down.
- **glassware:** fine stemmed cocktail glass (about 210 ml), chilled, no ice
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 50 ml | bourbon | any bourbon of 45% (90 proof) or stronger |
| 22.5 ml | fresh lemon juice | |
| 15 ml | simple syrup | equal parts sugar and water, stirred until clear |
| 7.5 ml (1½ tsp) | orange curaçao | an accent, not the main sweetener, as in the first printed Daisies |
| 10 ml (2 tsp) | dry vermouth | a little vermouth, for the gin and vermouth Collins remembers his teacher telling him to start liking: it was for the guests, not for him |
| 15 ml (1 tbsp) | soda water, cold | added last |

**method**
1. Chill the glass in the freezer, or fill it with ice water while you mix.
2. Put the bourbon, lemon juice, simple syrup, curaçao and dry vermouth in a shaker. Fill it with ice.
3. Shake hard for about 12 seconds, until the outside of the shaker is almost too cold to hold.
4. Empty the glass. Strain the drink into it, pouring through a small sieve as well so no chips of ice get in.
5. Pour the tablespoon of cold soda water on top. No garnish.

**closingLine:** *Measure the vermouth: it's only two teaspoons. And when you turn something down, add the why.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*Two teaspoons of vermouth went in, for a taste one bartender was told to start liking.*

**whoYouAre**
You notice the thing that's slightly off. The line on the page that sits a millimetre low. The dish that should have come out hot. The word on a menu that doesn't mean what it says. Most people let it pass. You can't, and you'd rather seem cold than let it go out like that.

People take it for pride, or for your taste. It isn't, mostly. At the end of every piece of work there's someone who'll never know what you turned down for them, and they're who you're thinking of. You hardly ever mention it, because it sounds like asking to be thanked. So your no arrives alone, without its reason. What you fear is something careless going out under a name that was meant to stand for something. Around you, good becomes normal. People raise their own bar near you, often without noticing they have, and whoever's at the end gets it as it should be.

**yours**

1. In 1990s London, the hottest bars were pouring fruit "Martinis". People loved those drinks, and plenty still do. A bartender from Camden called Wayne Collins hated them, and wrote an article against them in a magazine called *Class*. His quarrel was with the name. As he told Robert Simonson in 2014, he didn't like that it was called a Martini.

2. Here's the part I love. The way Collins remembers it, he'd ask why anyone would drink gin and vermouth, and his teacher, Lee Chappell, would tell him he'd better start liking it, because it was for the guests, not for him. Chappell, by the way, ran a place in Covent Garden called the Long Island Iced Tea Shop. Nobody in this story is a snob.

3. I can't know what was in Collins's head when he wrote that article, but I like to think it was Chappell's lesson: not his own taste, but a promise made to someone else, about what a name should mean. He didn't stop at no, either. In 2000, as bar manager at High Holborn, he put on a menu of drinks he'd found in old books, ones that weren't on other London menus. That bar, as one London bartender put it, influenced a lot of people, and the training programme he built, Mixxit, is credited with driving up service standards in the trade. That's the shape of what you do: the no comes with a better yes behind it, and people around you start holding the same line.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a whiskey Daisy. Daisies were on his High Holborn menu, and I've made this one the old way: whiskey (bourbon, here), fresh lemon, some sugar, just a touch of orange curaçao, then only a splash of soda. It's an old classic you'll still rarely find on a list. I serve it very cold, in a fine stemmed glass. Like his Sazeracs, it comes in good glassware with no ice in it. And I've added two teaspoons of dry vermouth, as a nod to the gin and vermouth Collins was told to start liking, for the guests. You won't taste it as vermouth. You'll taste a drier, rounder drink because of it.

5. I wouldn't ask you to lower the bar. Please don't, for anyone. But from now on, when you turn something down, add the why, the way Chappell did. A no that comes with its reason stops sounding cold. It sounds like care, because that's what it is. And the reason travels where you can't: people start keeping your standard in rooms you'll never walk into, because at last they understand why.

**closing line (Wren's words, on Tomás's gesture):** *Measure the vermouth: it's only two teaspoons. And when you turn something down, add the why.*
