# Pour — Out of Your Way · The Knight in Shining Armour (lover-hero)

## Cocktail

- **name:** Out of Your Way
- **tagline:** Some people send their love. You bring it.
- **glassware:** stemmed red wine glass (about 250 ml), at room temperature, no ice
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 ml | young red Bordeaux (claret) | "Claret" is the old English name for red Bordeaux. Any dry, medium-bodied red works if it isn't oaky or sweet. The story names no wine, so this one is my choice. |
| 30 ml | straight rye whiskey | any straight rye, 40-50% |
| 7.5 ml | demerara syrup | 1½ teaspoons: equal weights of demerara sugar and warm water, stirred until dissolved, then cooled. Use up to 10 ml if your wine is firm and grippy. |
| 2 dashes | Angostura bitters | |
| 15 ml | water | at room temperature, not from the fridge |
| 1 strip | orange peel | squeezed over the top, then set aside |

**method**
1. Keep everything out of the fridge: the rye, the wine, the water and the glass all stay at room temperature. A cool room is best. No ice, at any point.
2. Pour the syrup, the bitters and the rye into the glass and stir for a few seconds to mix.
3. Add the wine and the water, then stir gently three or four times.
4. Hold the orange peel over the glass, skin side down, and squeeze it so its oils fall on the drink. Set the peel aside, so nothing is in the glass when it's raised.

**closingLine:** *Raise it to someone first. When one's raised to you, you've brought enough.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*This cocktail is made at room temperature. Some things shouldn't arrive cold.*

**whoYouAre**
Someone you love says it's a shame you can't make it. It's three hundred miles away, in the middle of the week. You don't argue. On the night, you're there, on time, in your good coat, and the whole room turns round. That's how you love. Not a card, not a call. You said you'd come, so you come, the whole way, with everyone watching. Anyone you love never has to wonder whether you'll be there, and everyone around them can see how loved they are. Nobody forgets being loved like that.

It was never the distance. It's the second before you see their face, when you don't know yet whether they're glad you came, or whether they'd have been fine without you. You'd never admit it, but somewhere along the way, being needed started to feel like the proof that you're loved. It isn't the only proof. Most of the time, it isn't even the best one.

**yours**

1. In November 1889, a reporter called Nellie Bly set out from New Jersey to go round the world faster than the hero of Jules Verne's novel, who took eighty days. She had promised seventy-five. When her ship reached England, she heard that Verne and his wife had written to ask her to visit them at home in Amiens, in northern France. It was out of her way, and she'd assumed she would have to say no. It could be done, she was told, if she was willing to go without sleep for two nights. She asked only whether she'd miss any connections. If not, she said, "don't think about sleep or rest."

2. It nearly came to nothing. It all hung on a special mail train that night, and when they pulled into Amiens they were locked in their compartment and thought they'd be carried past. Then she stepped onto the platform, and the Vernes were standing there, waiting for her. What she wrote about that moment isn't the miles or the lost sleep: "I wondered if my face was travel-stained, and if my hair was tossed." A second later, she wrote, the welcome had made her forget all about it.

3. That's the second I mean. You'd make the whole journey without a thought and still feel it on the platform. And look at who was waiting. They had only asked her to stop and see them. I like to think they wanted nothing but her company. When it was time to go, they came out into the cold courtyard without hats, though everyone protested, and stood at the gate waving until she was out of sight, the wind blowing their white hair about. Nobody there was worried about anyone's hair.

4. Before she left, there was wine and biscuits on the table, and Verne, against his own rules, took a glass so they could all drink to her success. The glasses clinked, and he managed it in English, for her: "Good luck, Nellie Bly." Her book never says what the wine was, so the wine in the cocktail I've made for you is my choice: a young red Bordeaux, and it's more than half the drink, because the toast was the wine. With it goes straight rye. In the 1880s, American bartenders floated red wine on whiskey sours, so whiskey and red wine already belonged together in her decade. Then a little dark sugar syrup, Angostura bitters, a splash of water, and the scent of orange peel. Ice never comes near it. It's made and served at room temperature, soft, deep and warming: a good red with a spine of rye. I made it warm for the fire Verne's wife lit with her own hands when they arrived, and for the two of them standing bareheaded in the cold courtyard to wave her off.

5. So go and see someone who's doing perfectly well without you. No occasion, nothing to fix, nobody to rescue: just because you'd like to see them. You'll feel that second at the door, the same as always. Let it come. When their face lights up, it won't be for anything you've done. That's what being wanted looks like, and you've had it all along. I hope there's someone on the platform every time.
