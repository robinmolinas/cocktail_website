# Pour — Standing By · The Understanding Spouse (caregiver-lover)

## Cocktail

- **name:** Standing By
- **tagline:** You've seen them at their worst. You're still here.
- **glassware:** small wine glass, chilled, no ice
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 ml | blended scotch whisky | any blended scotch; the letter asks for "Scotch whisky", no brand |
| 7.5 ml | passion fruit syrup | equal weights passion fruit juice (fresh or bottled, unsweetened) and sugar, stirred until clear; takes the place of the crushed sugar |
| 2 dashes | Angostura bitters | |
| 1 | strip of lemon peel | pinched over the glass so its oils land on the drink, then dropped in |

**method**
1. Put a small wine glass in the freezer.
2. Pour the scotch, syrup and bitters into a mixing glass or jar full of ice.
3. Stir for about 30 seconds, until the outside of the glass is very cold.
4. Strain into the cold wine glass. No ice.
5. Hold the lemon peel coloured side down over the glass, pinch it so its oils land on the drink, and drop it in.

**closingLine:** *Set out everything they asked for. Then add the one thing they didn't.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*Mark Twain wrote home for four things to be waiting. Three are in this glass.*

**whoYouAre**
Someone you love walks in, or picks up the phone, and you can hear it in the first word: a bad day. You don't ask yet. You know it isn't about you, you've a fair idea what it is about, and you know they'll get there in their own time. So you stay close, and you wait. You've seen them at their worst more than once. It never changed your mind about them. It completed the picture.

You'd never call that a choice. A choice sounds like keeping score, and keeping score feels selfish. So people call it patience, and now and then, less kindly, letting them off. It isn't either. It's understanding someone all the way down, and it means that around you, nobody has to be at their best to be loved. What it costs you is quieter: the thing that hurt, which you understood your way around instead of saying. And the odd evening when you wonder who understands you the same way.

**yours**

1. In January 1874, Mark Twain was in London, and he wrote home to his wife, Livy. He asked her to have four things waiting in the bathroom for his return: Scotch whisky, a lemon, crushed sugar and a bottle of Angostura bitters. A ship's doctor had recommended a cocktail before breakfast, before dinner and before bed, and he gave it the credit for his digestion. Then he pictured the night he'd get home: the bell at midnight, the bolt, "Who is it?", and him with his drink, "& you standing by". He signed it "I do love & honor you, my darling."

2. Her answer isn't on record. I like to think the four things were there. What is on record, the way he told it years later, is the morning she made him hear himself. He'd kept his swearing from her. One morning, dressing, he swore at a shirt with a button off and threw it out of the window, then another shirt, then his collars and ties. Then he saw the door had been open. She sat up in bed and repeated his last remark back to him, word for word: "I wanted you to hear just how it sounded." She had the words right, he said, but not the tune.

3. That's the part of her I think you'll know. Not the telling-off: the way she did it. No scene, no leaving the room. She understood him, she stood by him, and once, she didn't let understanding stand in for saying it. I like to think she could only do it because she was never going anywhere. People who stay can afford to be honest.

4. Three of his four original cocktail ingredients are here: the Scotch, the Angostura, and the lemon, though only its peel. The crushed sugar is gone, and in its place is a little passion fruit syrup. It's the only sweetener in the glass and the sharpest thing too, and you couldn't mistake it for agreement if you tried. Less, and the drink is thin; more, and it's all you taste. It's served in a wine glass, the way he took his. The sweetness he asked for is still there. It just comes with something he didn't ask to hear, but that you choose to say.

5. There's something you've been understanding your way around: a remark, a habit, a thing that hurt, that you explained for them before they had to. Next time it happens, don't explain it. Say it back to them, gently, just as it sounded. You're not going anywhere. That's exactly why you're the only one who can say it.
