# Pour — Who's In? · The Charismatic Leader (ruler-jester)

## Cocktail

- **name:** Who's In?
- **tagline:** With you it feels like a party. It's also going somewhere.
- **glassware:** a big glass (about 400 ml, a 14-oz highball or double old-fashioned), chilled in the freezer, packed with crushed ice and a straw
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 ml | vodka | any unflavoured vodka from 37.5% to 45% |
| 20 ml | fresh lime juice | |
| 15 ml (1 tbsp) | raspberry and toasted cumin syrup | homemade: fresh raspberries, the same weight of sugar and a little toasted cumin, blended cold and sieved. Keeps a week in the fridge |
| 6 | fresh mint leaves | they stay in the glass |
| one of | 3 or 4 more mint leaves, or ½ tsp (2.5 ml) more syrup, or 1 dash Angostura bitters | your call, after you've tasted it through the straw |
| to fill | crushed ice | lots of it: more ice than drink |
| garnish | a mint sprig and two fresh raspberries | on top of the ice |

**method**
1. Make the syrup ahead. Warm half a teaspoon of cumin seeds in a dry frying pan, shaking it, until they smell nutty, then crush them lightly with the back of a spoon. Blend them cold with 100 g fresh raspberries and 100 g white sugar until the sugar has gone. Leave it for 30 minutes, then press it through a fine sieve to take out all the seeds. Keep it in the fridge.
2. Put the glass in the freezer for a few minutes.
3. Put the vodka, lime juice, syrup and mint leaves into a shaker with just a few pieces of crushed ice. Shake for about five seconds, only enough to mix it. The ice in the glass does the rest.
4. Pour everything, leaves and all, into the cold glass. Fill it four-fifths with crushed ice and stir up and down with a long spoon for a few seconds. Then pack in more ice, piled up above the rim.
5. Put a straw in and taste it. Then make one call: a few more mint leaves pushed down if you want it fresher, half a teaspoon more syrup if it's sharp, or a dash of Angostura if you want it deeper. Stir once more.
6. Top with the mint sprig and two raspberries.

**closingLine:** *Taste it through the straw and make one call. Then pass the glass to whoever hasn't made a call yet.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*A bartending school went bust. Its party bar didn't. This drink comes in its big glass.*

**whoYouAre**
The last train's cancelled, and there are twelve of you on a cold platform. Someone sighs. You make a joke, it lands, and somewhere in the middle of it there's a plan, and everyone hears it: the café that's still open, the walk across town, a bet on who gets home first. Years later, it's the night everyone brings up.

Nobody calls it being in charge, because to them it feels like being asked along. You like it like that. Being obeyed bores you, and it would turn you into the boss. You seldom admit how much you enjoy having your hand on the wheel, and that when you make that first joke, you don't yet know it'll work. What you dread is the night it gets away from you, or the night the fun runs out and you're just someone telling people where to go. Around you, a plan falling apart is where the good part begins. People go further than they ever would on their own, and they come back glad they went.

**yours**

1. Six hundred pounds bought you three weeks at the London Academy of Bartenders. It opened in 1996, and it never had enough money. One of its founders, Alex Turner, said they were always one student short of being able to run the next course, so the founders worked bar jobs at night and taught in the mornings. Eventually the school went bust.

2. The name didn't. Douglas Ankrah and Richard Hargroves had a bar called LAB as well, meant, by one account, as a living advertisement for the school. The school didn't survive. The bar did, and it was never a quiet advert. Its owners wanted a party bar, loud and busy every night. Ankrah said other bars were "very, very passively boring". Jamie Terrell, whom Ankrah brought in, remembered what they were after in all that noise: "the best drinks in the world". The two of them wrote five cocktail menus together, with two hundred drinks on each.

3. It could have gone quiet. Early in 1999 it was slow, and hardly anyone noticed the place until good press turned it. After that, one bartender said everybody in the trade used to be there, to hire, to get hired, or to meet the drinks firms' reps, and it helped make bartenders into names people knew. Someone who saw it remembered the bartenders sipping a drink through a straw, then adding "a bit more mint, a little more sugar, a dash of bitters". My favourite detail: in a room that loud, someone was still tasting. I like to think you'd have got on with them. When a plan falls through, you don't mourn it. You throw the party, and you mean the party to get somewhere.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a vodka Daiquiri, built like a Mojito, in the kind of big glass LAB served so many of its drinks in, with crushed ice heaped above the rim. I chose raspberries for it, blended cold into a syrup, so the bold red comes from the fruit alone, with a pinch of toasted cumin in the syrup, a turn you won't see coming that works out, and lime and mint leaves that stay in: bright and generous. Then it's over to you. When you make it, taste it through the straw and make one of those three calls: a few more mint leaves, a little more syrup, or a dash of bitters. That step is there as a nod to those bartenders tasting in all that noise, and the drink is built so that whichever call you make, it holds.

5. Before you go, a thought, and I mean it kindly. Somewhere near you is someone who never makes the first joke, because you always do. When the evening next falls apart, wait a moment. Leave it to them, and follow wherever it goes. You won't lose the wheel. What you'll get is something you almost never have: the fun of not knowing where you're going while someone else steers.
