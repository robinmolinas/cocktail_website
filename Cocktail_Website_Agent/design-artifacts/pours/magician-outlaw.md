## Cocktail

- **name:** No Accident
- **tagline:** You never break a rule by accident.
- **glassware:** champagne flute
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note (shown) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | white sugar cube | where the truth hides |
| 3 dashes | Angostura bitters | |
| 3 dashes | Peychaud's bitters | |
| 15 ml | bourbon | |
| 15 ml | orange liqueur (triple sec) | |
| 100 ml | brut champagne, very cold | |
| 1 strip | orange peel | |

**method**
1. Chill a champagne flute.
2. Soak the sugar cube with both bitters and drop it into the flute.
3. Add the bourbon and the orange liqueur.
4. Top slowly with the champagne. Do not stir.
5. Squeeze the orange peel over the top so its oils fall on the drink, then drop it in.

**closingLine:** *Don't stir it. It tells you the truth at the end.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*The story was a trick. The drink never was.*

**whoYouAre**

**yours** (authored base; the live tailoring keeps these paragraphs, anchors and order)

1. In 1995, a bartender at an old hotel in Louisville, Kentucky, introduced a cocktail he said he'd found in the hotel's past: its signature drink from before Prohibition, born when a bartender spilled champagne into a Manhattan by accident. It was a perfect story. Newspapers repeated it. Respected cocktail books printed it. For twenty years, people who knew a great deal about drinks raised their glasses to a piece of history.
2. There was no history. In 2016 he admitted he had invented the drink himself, and the story with it. No accident, no century-old recipe. And here's the part you'll like: nothing about the drink changed. It tasted just as good the day after the confession as the day before, and it's still poured at that hotel.
3. So why do it? He later said that back then he was a nobody, with no name in the bar world, and he wanted to put the hotel on the map. That's the reason he gave. But I like to think there was more to it. He knew a good drink with no past is easy to walk past, and a drink with a legend gets ordered, loved and passed on. So he played a trick with a gift inside: he gave people a great drink, a great story to enjoy it with, and in the end the truth as well. It showed how powerful a good story is, and how quickly people will rally behind one. All it takes is a little magic to set it going.
4. That's your kind of magic: never a trick for its own sake. So this is his drink, played your way. Bourbon, orange liqueur and two kinds of bitters, poured over a sugar cube and topped with champagne, and never stirred. It changes as it goes. The top is bright and dry. The sugar at the bottom dissolves slowly, sending up a thin line of bubbles, so the last sips are sweeter, darker and more bitter than the first. The truth is at the bottom of the glass, which is exactly where you would hide it. And the story it was first sold with was about an accident. You know better: the best tricks only look like accidents.
5. Here's what I'd ask of you. The next time you feel like pulling the rug out from under a room, make it a trick with a gift inside, and leave people with something better than what they walked in with. Then stay long enough to show them how it was done. The trick is what they'll remember tonight. The gift is what they'll keep.

### Why the Seelbach (and not the Stinger)

Designed from scratch, no image. Two candidates:
- **Seelbach (chosen).** The drink *is* the trick: a hoax that fooled experts for about twenty years, then a confession. It matches the Trickster's "con **and** reveal" and "disruption with a point". The fake origin being an *accident* answers the fear of unintended results.
- **Stinger (runner-up).** One drink under many names (the Bartholdi, the Judge, the Brant, the Ice Trust), and a millionaire credited with inventing it when he was ten. Kept as a fact card: *Oxford Companion*, STINGER (pdf 1920–1921).

### Legends and inferences (not usable as fact)

- "Born in 1917 when champagne was spilled into a Manhattan": **the hoax itself**. Only ever quoted *as* the invented story.
- "Discussed as a modern classic": Oxford lists it among candidates, not as a settled classic. The reading says only that it's still poured at the hotel.
- The *Cocktail Codex* calls the Stinger's origin "untraceable" (p. 13); Oxford traced it to 1890. Oxford wins.
