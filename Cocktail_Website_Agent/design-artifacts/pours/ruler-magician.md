# Pour — The Long View · The Legend (ruler-magician)

## Cocktail

- **name:** The Long View
- **tagline:** The worse it gets, the clearer you are.
- **glassware:** stemmed sour glass (about 240 ml), chilled, no ice
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 ml | straight rye whiskey | 43% or stronger; 50% is ideal |
| 15 ml (½ oz) | Virgin Islands (St. Croix) rum, lightly aged | a style; Duffy asked only for enough to flavour it, and half an ounce is Wondrich's measure. Any lightly aged rum of about 40%, not spiced |
| 60 ml | sharpened orange juice | fresh orange juice with a little citric and malic acid stirred in (see step 1); in winter, try blood oranges |
| 1½ tsp (6 g) | caster sugar | stirred into the juice first |
| 1 strip | orange peel | squeezed over the top |

**method**
1. Sharpen the orange. Squeeze about 4 oranges for 250 ml of juice, on the day you'll serve it, and strain it: many navel oranges turn bitter if the juice sits. Weigh in 3.4 g of citric acid and 2.1 g of malic acid, and stir until both have dissolved. If you only have citric acid, use 5.5 g. Keep it in the fridge until the first drink.
2. Chill the glass in the freezer, or fill it with ice water while you mix.
3. Put 60 ml of the sharpened juice and the sugar in a shaker, and stir until the sugar has dissolved.
4. Add the rye and the rum, fill the shaker with ice, and shake hard for about 12 seconds, until the outside is frosty.
5. Empty the glass. Strain the drink into it, through a small sieve as well so no chips of ice get in.
6. Hold a strip of orange peel over the drink, skin side down, and squeeze it so its oils fall on the top. Then lay it on the rim.

**closingLine:** *Sharpen the orange before anyone's thirsty. Then say it early, while nobody else is worried.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*Rye and orange, after a bartender whose book came out as the ban ended.*

**whoYouAre**
On an ordinary day you can seem like too much. You take the long view when the rest of the room is busy with the week, and you mention what could go wrong before anyone else is worried. People nod, and talk about something else. Then the worst day comes, the one where everyone else is lost for words, and they all look at you. You've had the words for a while.

You don't say how much the quiet stretches sting. Seeing it coming isn't much comfort while nobody's listening. And part of you, a part you'd rather not mention, steadies in a crisis, because at last you make sense. The storm doesn't frighten you. Being the one in charge when it wins does, and being remembered for that. So you work out what you'd say long before anyone asks, and on the day people have nothing left to stand on, you hand them a sentence that holds. People get through their worst days because of how you talk them through it.

**yours**

1. In the 1890s, Patrick Duffy was head bartender of New York's Ashland House. Someone at the time described his waxed moustache and the carnation in his lapel. Then he left the trade. Years later he was running rooming houses in Brooklyn.

2. In December 1933 he told a reporter how his book came about. He'd been watching drink coming back for three years, and with not much to do but look after his two houses, he'd started gathering the recipes. The book was on sale by the time the ban ended, and it holds the detail I love most. The mixtures people had got used to in the dry years went in, because people asked for them, but the wilder ones got a little circle: he wouldn't want it said he'd recommended them. The ones he thought belonged to the best tradition got a star. A cocktail historian puts it among the few books that did most to bring the craft back.

3. Maybe the circles were only good manners. I like to think they were more than that: a man who knew the day had come, cared what would be said about it afterwards, and made sure nobody could say he'd let it slide. You'd have drawn those circles too. You know a moment like that gets remembered, and you want to be remembered for what you stood behind.

4. The cocktail I've made for you starts from his Herald Punch, the one drink of his that caught on, which he dedicated to a newspaper, the *Herald*, in 1901: rye, orange juice, a spoon of sugar and just enough rum to flavour it, shaken well and strained into a sour glass. The same historian calls it a corker. Orange on its own is too gentle to hold a sour up, so I sharpen the juice, about halfway to lime, with a little of the acids that make lemons and apples tart. You still taste orange, bright and fresh, with the rye's dry spice through the middle and the rum warm underneath. It just holds. I did that as a nod to the work he did before anyone needed it.

5. So I'll take a side. The quiet years aren't the wait. They're where the words come from. Next time you see something coming and nobody's listening, mention it anyway, once, plainly, and early. You don't need everything to go wrong to be worth hearing. And if the day does come, you'll find people already listening.

### Image brief

- **Glass and drink:** a stemmed sour glass (about 240 ml), chilled, with a light frost on the bowl. The drink is a hazy, opaque orange-amber (fresh orange juice shaken with amber rye and a little gold rum), with a thin, fine pale foam on top from the shake. A strip of orange peel lies across the rim. No ice in the glass. Show the ordinary orange version, not blood orange.
- **Props (3-5):** a few oranges, one halved, cut side up, on a small wooden board. A small pocket kitchen scale with a little white heap of acid crystals on its pan (the spark). An old loose-leaf recipe binder, open, its lines too small to read, with a small printed star beside one entry and a small open circle beside another (his book's marks, F7-F8). A folded broadsheet newspaper with no legible text (the drink was dedicated to a newspaper). An unlabelled bottle of amber whiskey behind and to one side.
- **One impossible detail:** the drops of oil from the squeezed peel hang above the glass, perfectly still in the light, as if the moment were being held before it happens.
- **Setting and palette:** a quiet, orderly bar top or writing desk in strong side light, with dramatic contrast but a mid-tone background, never dark behind the glass. Deep charcoal (Pantone 433 C) for the binder's cover and a felt mat under it, with warm orange, amber and the scale's steel. Archival textures: paper, cloth, worn wood.
- **Must not appear:** a microphone, radio, cigar, flag, uniform, map, crown or podium (the archetype and Churchill traps). Any legible text, label, date or headline. A second glass, a punch bowl or a ladle (it's called a punch, but it's one glass). Lemon or lime, ice in the glass, a cherry, smoke, people. Not a carnation (it's the man's lapel, not the guest's).

### Names considered

*The Long View* (all three, round 5: Tomás's pick; Wren moved from *The Quiet Years*, Hester from *Days Early*), over *Seen Coming* and *Before Anyone Asked*. No crossing: the voices held their picks until round 5.

**My final name: *The Long View*.** (Round 5, one name each.)

1. **The Long View** (my vote): it praises how the person thinks, years ahead, which is the true limb of the ruled story (Duffy watching drink come back "these last three years", *Eagle*, 1 Dec 1933). It's easy to say across a bar ("a Long View, please"), and it shares no word with the tagline, the epigraph or the closing line. The registry has no "Long" and no "View". whoYouAre says "you take the long view", so the name recognises what the reading names, and leaves the turn to y5 and the closing line. Hester could take it.
2. **The Quiet Years** (Wren's): strong, but it's the turn of y5 ("the quiet years aren't the wait"), so the title would give the position away before the reading reaches it. That's my case, and it's for Robin if we stay split.
3. **Seen Coming** (Wren's could-take): close to mine, but passive.
4. **Days Early** (Hester's): weakened by A11 (the book was announced for Repeal day itself), and it shares "early" with the closing line.
