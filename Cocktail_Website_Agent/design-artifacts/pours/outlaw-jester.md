# Pour — With the Bite In · The Subverter (outlaw-jester)

## Cocktail

- **name:** With the Bite In
- **tagline:** Said straight, they'd stop you. So you make them laugh.
- **glassware:** a tall glass, filled with ice
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 ml | Angostura 1919 aged rum (recommended), or any column-distilled aged rum, not spiced, about 40% | from Trinidad, made by the same house as the bitters |
| 15 ml | fresh lime juice | about half a lime; keep the squeezed half |
| 120 ml | Coca-Cola, recommended (or any full-sugar cola) | fridge-cold, freshly opened |
| 2 dashes | Angostura bitters | on top, last |
| 1 | squeezed lime half | dropped into the glass, not muddled |

**method**
1. Fill a tall glass with ice and pour in the rum. Stir for three seconds.
2. Squeeze in the lime, then drop the squeezed half into the glass.
3. Pour the cola gently down the side, and stir once.
4. Shake two dashes of bitters onto the top, so the first thing you smell is the island.

**closingLine:** *Squeeze the lime and drop it in. If they sing it back wrong, write a new one.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*A song made rum and Coca-Cola famous. The song was never really about the drink.*

**whoYouAre**
There's a meeting, or a family dinner, or a rule nobody's supposed to question, and everyone's nodding along. Then you say something, lightly, and the table laughs, the person at the head of it too, a beat late. Nobody can quite tell you off. It was only funny. That was the idea.

Nobody notices that you chose it. The joke isn't a reflex, and it never punches down. It's aimed at whoever's at the head of the table, because that's the one place a plain sentence would be stopped. So you keep quiet about how much of it you mean: the moment they know, they stop laughing and start stopping you. A telling-off you can live with; it means it landed. What you dread is hearing your line from somebody else, everyone laughing, with the point quietly taken out.

And what you give is more than a laugh. When a room laughs at whatever's in charge, it sees how ordinary the thing really is, and fears it less. That's how you hand people their nerve back, and it's a real gift, whatever you call it.

**yours**

1. In wartime Trinidad, with an American base on the island, a calypso singer called Lord Invader set new words to an old tune by Lionel Belasco. Where he sang, songs had to get past the police commissioner's board of censors. His got through. It was called "Rum and Coca-Cola", it laughed at what the soldiers' money was doing to the island, and soldiers and Trinidadians alike sang it.

2. In 1943 an American comedian, Morey Amsterdam, heard it while entertaining the troops and took it home. The Andrews Sisters made it a hit, and the first 200,000 records named Amsterdam as the writer. A New York court later found that Lord Invader had written the words. The money reached him seven years late, and even then he was often short.

3. While the case went on, he recorded "Yankee Dollar", a follow-up that turned the joke on himself: the man who'd sung the hit, still without the dollars. Years later came "Crisis in Arkansas", which laughed at the black bow tie of the governor who had kept nine Black students out of a Little Rock school. I like to think he'd worked out what you know: someone can take a line, but never the place it came from.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is rum and Coca-Cola. It's older than the song, and simple, but made properly it's a classic worth asking for by name, long and cold over plenty of ice. The rum I'd pick is from Trinidad, from the same house as the Angostura bitters that go on last, so the island reaches you before anything else. And the lime, squeezed and dropped into the glass, to cut through the cola's sweetness. I doubled the lime in the recipe I started from, as a nod to the song: sweet enough for everyone to sing, and still sharp.

5. One day you'll hear a line of yours from someone else, to a big laugh, with the point gone. Don't spend the evening proving it was yours. Come up with another, and aim it a little higher than the last. A joke can be borrowed; the person it came from can't, and every room that laughed with you is a little braver for it.
