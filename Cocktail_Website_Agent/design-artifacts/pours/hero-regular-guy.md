# Pour — Anyone Would Have · The Firefighter (hero-regular-guy)

## Cocktail

- **name:** Anyone Would Have
- **tagline:** When it counts, you've already done it a hundred times.
- **glassware:** large rocks glass (about 400 ml), one large ice cube
- **contains:** `["nuts"]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 30 ml (1 oz) | London dry gin (any, 40–47%) | one of each: that's the whole recipe |
| 30 ml (1 oz) | Cherry Heering (recommended), or another red cherry liqueur | the red cherry brandy Singapore drank then; it's what turns the drink pink |
| 30 ml (1 oz) | Bénédictine (recommended), or another sweet herbal liqueur | a French liqueur of herbs and spices, finished with honey |
| 30 ml (1 oz) | fresh lime juice (about one lime) | |
| 90 ml (3 oz) | cold soda water | |
| 3 dashes | Angostura bitters | on top, last |
| 1 | large ice cube | |

**method**
1. Pour the gin, Cherry Heering, Bénédictine and lime juice straight into a large rocks glass: 30 ml of each.
2. Add one large ice cube and stir for about 20 seconds, until the outside of the glass feels cold.
3. Top up with the cold soda water and give it one gentle stir.
4. Shake three dashes of Angostura bitters onto the top. No garnish.

**closingLine:** *One of each, so anyone can learn it. Someone beside you already has.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*Gin, cherry, herbs and lime. One day, nobody behind the bar could make it.*

**whoYouAre**
The lights go out in the middle of a party, and you already know where the fuse box is. The car won't start on the way to the airport, and you've got the number saved. Afterwards, you shrug: anyone would have done the same. But you know that isn't quite true. You found the fuse box the week you moved in. You drove the route the day before. You don't trust yourself to be at your best on the day, so you don't leave anything to the day.

What you'd never say is why. It isn't pride. You're afraid of being the one who wasn't enough when someone was counting on you. So you practise, quietly, for things that may never happen, and when it goes well you hand the credit to everyone else. Being singled out has always felt a bit like being left out. But everyone near you gets through a bad day more easily because of a hundred ordinary ones you never mention. That's how you care for people, and it deserves more than a shrug.

**yours**

1. At a Mexican restaurant in the Pacific Northwest, Marco Dionysos worked his way up from clearing tables to the bar. (He chose that surname himself, after the Greek god of wine: the same god this place is named after.) There he mostly made frozen drinks, and liked it. Then one day a guest ordered a Singapore Sling. Marco asked two senior bartenders, and neither of them knew the recipe. As his story is told, that didn't sit right with him. He went to a bookshop in Portland, bought his first cocktail book, and kept going back.

2. His books ran into the hundreds, though he said he learned more from watching other people work. He moved to San Francisco and tended bar there for years. He once described a moment on a busy Friday night, with sixteen drinks going at once, a lot of it muscle memory, and the part he loved: "I got this." One writer says he could have become the city's star bartender, but "didn't seize the mantle or seem to want it". Asked what he'd like to be known for, Marco said "making consistently excellent cocktails". Asked about his mentors, he said: "Probably dozens."

3. I like to think he never quite forgot that guest at the bar, waiting for a drink nobody there could make. Nobody had done anything wrong. Someone had simply asked, and nobody was ready. I think that's the moment you practise against: not failing, but being the one who wasn't enough when it mattered. So you put in the hours nobody sees, and when the day comes and goes fine, you shrug. Like Marco, you'd rather be known for getting it right every time.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is that Singapore Sling, the older way, before a hotel sweetened it in the 1970s. The earliest recipe on record is a bar order from 1913, at a club in Singapore: one cherry brandy, one Bénédictine (a sweet herbal liqueur), one gin, one lime juice, then ice, soda water and some bitters. I kept it at one measure of each, so it's the kind of recipe you'll never need a book for. It's built straight in the glass over one big ice cube and stirred, with no shaker and nothing to show: a deep, rosy pink-red, a little cloudy with lime, the bitters just starting to bleed in from the top. I made it properly, for the guest who asked for it that day, and for every day you've got ready so nobody's left waiting.

5. You've spent years getting ready for other people's hard days. What you forget is that the others have been getting ready too, and some of it was for yours. Being enough was never meant to be your job alone. So before the next day like that, practise as much as you like. Then look at who's around you, and trust their hours the way they've always trusted yours. You were never the only one who was ready.

**closing line** (Tomás's, my rewrite, in his draft v1.2): *One of each, so anyone can learn it. Someone beside you already has.* It agrees with y5 (trust the others' hours), not the creed. His "The people beside you already have" is a 4-gram *Making the Calls* uses. If the name becomes *One of Each*, the line should open differently: *Anyone can learn it. Someone beside you already has.*
