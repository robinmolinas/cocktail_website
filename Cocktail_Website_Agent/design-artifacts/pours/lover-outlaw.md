# Pour — Accomplices · The Bad Influence (lover-outlaw)

## Cocktail

- **name:** Accomplices
- **tagline:** Around you, people drop the act and like who they find underneath.
- **glassware:** coupe (about 180 ml), chilled
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 ml | straight rye whiskey | any straight rye; proved from 40% to 50% |
| 30 ml | beetroot sweet vermouth | sweet vermouth steeped with raw beetroot (below); the change I made |
| 7.5 ml | orange curaçao, dry style | Employees Only's Manhattan adds curaçao; the measure is mine |
| 2 dashes | Angostura bitters | |
| 1 strip | orange peel | |

**Beetroot sweet vermouth (makes enough for eight):** 50 g peeled raw beetroot, thinly sliced, in 250 ml sweet vermouth. Cover and leave at room temperature for about 12 hours, then strain and keep it in the fridge.

**method**
1. Peel and slice the beetroot with your bare hands. Your fingers will go red. Leave them.
2. Steep the slices in the sweet vermouth for about 12 hours, covered, then strain. It keeps in the fridge.
3. Put a coupe in the freezer.
4. Pour the rye, the beetroot vermouth, the curaçao and the bitters into a mixing glass or a jar.
5. Fill it with ice and stir for about 30 seconds, until the outside of the glass is very cold.
6. Strain into the cold coupe.
7. Squeeze the orange peel over the top so its oils fall on the drink, then drop it in.

**closingLine:** *Slice the beetroot yourself and leave the red on. Then ask someone to something dull, and keep whoever comes.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*A New York bar put curaçao in its Manhattan. This one adds beetroot. It stains.*

**whoYouAre**
Two hours into a polite evening, you lean over and whisper, "Coming?" Twenty minutes later the two of you are sitting on a kerb with chips, laughing too loudly. Then they tell you something they've never told anyone. You weren't getting them into trouble. You were getting through to them.

People call you a bad influence, and you let them, because it's half true and it makes a better story. The rest of it: polite feels lonely to you. It keeps everyone at arm's length, you included. You don't want an audience. You want accomplices. Under every "come on" there's a "please come", and you'd rather raise the stakes than say that plainly. What you dread is being kept for the noise: loved as the wild one and never really known, or made sensible and quietly dropped. That's your gift: around you, people drop the act. They say the honest thing, the funny thing, and they like who they are when they're with you.

**yours**

1. Early in 2004, Jason Kosmas and Dushan Zaric left their jobs at Schiller's, in New York, taking its bar manager with them. Two more colleagues joined them. The owner, Kosmas said, wasn't happy with them. That autumn the five opened Employees Only. They broke the scene's rules together: they poured by eye while other serious bars measured, and they served food.

2. Some of the other cocktail people, as Kosmas felt it, looked at them as if they couldn't really care about their drinks "because people are having too much fun there". Zaric was plain about it. They weren't the best mixologists, he said, and they "chose to play a different card in order to provide experience".

3. And from opening day, whoever was still there when the bar closed was given homemade chicken soup, free. The food was partly business: Zaric had seen hungry customers leave a bar he'd worked at before. Partly it was their European background, where food and drink belong together. And the staff stayed. Very few ever left. One reason Zaric gave: they felt "they had a hand in creating this". I like to think that's why your people stay too. Not for the noise, but because with you, they got to help make it. The dare is what they talk about. What they remember is that you wanted them there.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a rye Manhattan, a classic the whole world knows, with curaçao (an orange liqueur), which Employees Only added to theirs. My change is the vermouth. It's steeped for about twelve hours with raw beetroot that you peel and slice yourself, and your fingers will go red. I put it there as a nod to that hand in it: you can't make this drink without it marking you. Stirred over ice, it goes into a coupe, a glass they used too. It tastes rich and smooth, earthy and sweet beneath the rye, with orange oil on top.

5. You don't have to keep raising the stakes to keep people. Some time soon, plan something with nothing to dare in it: a Sunday food shop, soup on the stove, a film you've both seen twice. See who's glad to be there. Those are your accomplices, and they always were. They'll still be around when nothing's happening, and you get to be known for more than the noise.

### Resonance

**The "this is me" line:** *You don't want an audience. You want accomplices.* Its runner-up is whoYouAre ¶1's last line, *You weren't getting them into trouble. You were getting through to them.*

**As the guest (a Bad Influence).** The tagline makes me grin. It's what I'd say in my own defence, and nobody has ever said it for me. The epigraph, read cold: a bar that put something odd in its Manhattan, and a drink that stains. I want to know why. ¶1 is me, the "shall we go?" and the kerb. And then the last line catches me, because the point was never the leaving. ¶2 names what I'd never admit: polite feels lonely, and "please come" hides under "come on". The fear is exact, and it has two sides, which is how it actually feels. Then it gives me something back: people like who they are with me. The story is a gang that left together and got written off as the fun bar. The soup makes me soften, and "a hand in creating this" is the real reason my people stay. y5 doesn't tame me. It asks for one ordinary plan, as a test I can win, and ends on being known.
