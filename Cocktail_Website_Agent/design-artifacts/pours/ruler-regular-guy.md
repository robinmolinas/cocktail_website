# Pour — One Table · The People's Representative (ruler-regular-guy)

## Cocktail

- **name:** One Table
- **tagline:** Everyone wants you on their side. You keep ending up on both.
- **glassware:** large rocks glass (about 300 ml), one large ice cube
- **contains:** `["nuts"]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 30 ml | straight rye whiskey | 40% or stronger; a 50% bottled-in-bond rye is what the numbers were run on |
| 30 ml | cognac | any VS or VSOP cognac, 40% or stronger |
| 30 ml | sweet vermouth steeped with cardamom | made the night before (see Method); start from any Italian-style sweet vermouth, as the 1937 recipe asks |
| 1 tsp (5 ml) | Bénédictine D.O.M. | recommended, as the old recipe names it; or a honeyed French herbal liqueur. Bénédictine contains nutmeg: check any substitute's label for nuts |
| 2 dashes | Peychaud's bitters | recommended, as the old recipe names it; or any New Orleans-style aromatic bitters |
| 2 dashes | Angostura bitters | or any aromatic bitters |
| 1 | strip of lemon peel | squeezed over the top, then dropped in |

**method**
1. The night before, put about 3 g of whole green cardamom pods (roughly 15) into 250 ml of sweet vermouth in a clean jar. Leave it at room temperature for about 12 hours, then strain it back into its bottle and keep it in the fridge. It's enough for eight drinks.
2. Pour the rye and the cognac into a mixing glass or jar, just as they come from the bottle.
3. Add the cardamom vermouth, the Bénédictine and both bitters. Fill the mixing glass with ice.
4. Stir for about 30 seconds, until the outside of the glass feels very cold.
5. Put one large ice cube in the rocks glass and strain the drink over it.
6. Squeeze the lemon peel over the top so its oils fall on the drink, then drop it in.

**closingLine:** *Put the cardamom in the vermouth, never in either spirit. Next time you cross over, bring all of yourself.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*A city gave up brandy for whiskey. Years later, one glass poured both.*

**whoYouAre**
A family stops speaking over a seating plan. A team splits down the middle over who did the work. Two friends each want you to say the other one's wrong. Everyone waits to see which table you'll sit at. You go and sit with the people who expected you not to, and you have whatever they're having.

You don't do it with a speech. You've never won anyone round with an argument, and you don't try. You just turn up on their ground and mean it. From outside it can look like sitting on the fence. It isn't. You have a side, and you know your own mind. The hardest question you get doesn't come from the other side. It comes from your own: whose side are you on? What you fear is being made to choose, and the day a row stops being a row and becomes the way things are. Here's what all that is worth: because of you, nobody has to pick a table. Both halves end up in one room, and neither had to give itself up to get there.

**yours**

1. Before the American Civil War, New Orleans was a brandy town. Its juleps were made with brandy, and that was how the city drank. Then the city changed. By 1885, a bartender was telling a New Orleans newspaper that brandy drinking there was over, and that whiskey had taken its place. The city drank it in whiskey cocktails and in the new Manhattan.

2. By the 1930s, the Hotel Monteleone's head bartender was Walter Bergeron. He'd worked that bar before Prohibition and returned when it ended. The drink he created there became the hotel's signature. It's a Manhattan at heart, but where a Manhattan has whiskey, his has two spirits: rye whiskey and French cognac. They go in at equal measures, level with the sweet vermouth, plus two different bitters and a touch of a herbal liqueur from France, Bénédictine. Its name is the old quarter's: Vieux Carré, "old square".

3. He said he made it to honour the old quarter. Why he put those two spirits side by side, he didn't say. But I like to think it's the city's whole story in a single drink: the brandy it drank first and the whiskey it turned to, together in one glass, neither poured a drop less than the other. That's what you do. When people split, you don't rule on which half was right. You bring both in, at the same weight, and you call it by a name everyone already shares.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is the Vieux Carré, a renowned New Orleans classic, with the proportions of its first printed recipe: rye, cognac and sweet vermouth in three equal measures, none cut back for another. I didn't change either spirit. I steeped the vermouth overnight with green cardamom, as a nod to the ground the two sides share: of the three measures, the vermouth is the one that belongs to neither, and cardamom goes with both the grain behind the rye and the grape behind the cognac. It's stirred, rich and deep, with the rye's spice and the cognac's softer fruit each as clear as the other, and the cardamom warm in the vermouth between them.

5. One thing, though. When you go over to the other side, you tend to leave a little of yourself at the door, so they feel safe. Your own side notices, and that's where the question comes from. Don't pour less of yourself. It was never your quietness they trusted. It's that you came as you are and stayed. And when your own side asks again, you'll have your answer: both, and still me.

**closing line (Tomás, r4):** *Put the cardamom in the vermouth, never in either spirit. Next time you cross over, bring all of yourself.*

### Open items

- **The link is ours:** the city's move from brandy to whiskey (by 1885, *Imbibe!*) and Bergeron's 1930s drink are about fifty years apart and no page links them; the reading signposts it. Bergeron's own side is never claimed; his stated reason was to honour the old quarter, and why he put rye and cognac side by side is not on record.
- **Hotel legend, dossier only:** the Monteleone's story that the drink was "an homage to the different ethnic groups of New Orleans" (relayed by Seidman, Pearce 2017) is brand-tier with no 1930s source and sits on the race trap; kept out of guest text. Guidebooks' "1938" is wrong against the 1937 printing, so "the 1930s". Always "the Monteleone's bar", never the Carousel (built 1949; Bergeron died 1947).
- **Our choices, said as ours:** the cardamom steep (about 3 g per 250 ml sweet vermouth, 12 hours, scaled from the *Codex*'s cardamom St-Germain), rocks over one large cube (Oxford allows up; up would put it in *Go On*'s glass), lemon peel, no cherry, and Oxford's modern 5 ml Bénédictine and 2 dashes against Arthur's ½ tsp and 1 dash. The equal thirds are 1937's; the jigger size behind Arthur's ⅓ is Tomás's assumption.
- **Edge:** a very tart sweet vermouth (0.7% acid, a guess) puts initial acid OUT and finished acid at the edge; the recipe says any Italian-style sweet vermouth. The drink holds through about 20 ml of melt and drops under the floor at 30 ml.
- **Contains nuts** (nutmeg in Bénédictine), as the plan's veto floor allows; not veto-free.
