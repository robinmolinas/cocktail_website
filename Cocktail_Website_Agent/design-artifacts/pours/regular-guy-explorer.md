# Pour — Loose on Top · The Entrepreneur (regular-guy-explorer)

## Cocktail

- **name:** Loose on Top
- **tagline:** You never wanted to be the boss. You just couldn't bear having one.
- **glassware:** cocktail glass (about 200 ml), chilled, no salt
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 ml | Sauza 100% agave blanco tequila (recommended; sold in the US as Sauza Signature Blue) | The brand Café Pacifico's Margaritas were made with, per one of its barmen; this is Sauza's 100% agave blanco, not Pacifico's exact bottle. Or any blanco tequila of 38-40%. |
| 22.5 ml | Giffard Triple Sec, 25% (recommended) | The brand Pacifico used, per one of its barmen. Giffard makes it at 25% and at 40% (Curaçao Triple Sec), and both balance. Or any French triple sec. |
| 22.5 ml | fresh lime juice | Squeezed the same day. |
| 7.5 ml | simple syrup | Equal parts sugar and water, stirred until clear. |
| 1 strip | grapefruit peel | The loose top: oils squeezed over the drink, then rested on the rim. |

**method**
1. Put the cocktail glass in the freezer for ten minutes, or fill it with ice and water while you work.
2. Measure the tequila, triple sec, lime juice and syrup into a shaker. Measure them exactly: this is the tight part.
3. Fill the shaker with ice and shake hard for about twelve seconds, until the outside is too cold to hold comfortably.
4. Empty the glass and strain the drink into it. No salt.
5. Now the loose part. Cut a wide strip of grapefruit peel with as little white as you can, hold it skin-side down over the glass and squeeze it so its oils fall on the drink, then rest it on the rim. That's my top. If someone else is making it, the top is theirs: anything that goes on the drink rather than into it.

**closingLine:** *Measure the bottom exactly. Then hand the top to someone else, and join the people you made room for.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*One restaurant owner chose the brands in this drink. Whoever's pouring chooses the top.*

**whoYouAre**
You started something of your own. Maybe a Sunday football team, a stall at the weekend market, a supper that turned into a monthly thing, or a whole business. When people ask why, they expect a big reason. The real one is smaller, and you rarely say it: you wanted to live your way, and pay your own way doing it.

There's something else you keep quiet. Somewhere along the line, other people started treating it as theirs. They turn up early, bring friends and save each other seats. And part of you worries about ending up in the corner, keeping the whole thing running for everyone else to belong to. It should be said, because you won't say it: the freedom you went out for always turns out to have room for other people in it.

**yours**

1. Tomas Estes taught English in Los Angeles and wanted to live in Amsterdam, a place where, he said, he could learn a lot about himself. He couldn't see how a teacher could manage it. A Mexican restaurant could. "My main goal was to support myself," he said, though one writer saw it differently: the restaurant was his way to freedom.

2. He opened Café Pacifico there in 1976, with little money, on a street people told him was too dangerous to visit. A second Pacifico opened in London, around a long bar. Young bartenders with few places to go for a proper cocktail found it, and one of its barmen spent ten years making Margaritas for the ones just off their shifts. At another of his London places, one bartender said he felt at home.

3. His partner Phil Bayly remembered his rule for the staff: "Tight on bottom, loose on top." Get the work right, then do it your way. I like to think that's the whole of him: he left to be free, then gave the same freedom away. In 2017 he said he was handing his shares in the London restaurant to the partners who worked there, keeping a minority share because he wanted to stay "part of the community". He added that he should probably have got out of the way five years sooner.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Margarita, a renowned classic. I made it with the two brands one of his London barmen said Estes chose, Sauza for the tequila and Giffard for the triple sec, shaken hard with fresh lime and served ice-cold, with no salt. The bottom is measured to the drop, as a nod to the tight half of his rule. The top is the loose half: I squeeze the oils of a grapefruit peel over it, and when someone else makes it, the top is theirs to choose. It only ever goes on the drink, never into it, so whatever they pick, the drink underneath still holds.

5. Don't leave it as late as he did. Whatever you've started, the team, the stall, the thing with your name on the door, hand someone the piece you've kept hold of and let them do it their way. It won't be how you'd do it. It'll be theirs, and that means it's working. Then turn up the way everyone else does: early, with a friend, for no reason except that you like it there. You went out on your own to be free. You don't have to stay on your own to keep it.
