# Pour — Before It Had a Name · The Leading Edge (outlaw-ruler)

## Cocktail

- **name:** Before It Had a Name
- **tagline:** Your rebellions have a plan, and a future people can join.
- **glassware:** a well-chilled coupe
- **contains:** `["nuts"]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 45 ml (1½ oz) | Old Potrero Straight Rye whiskey (48.5%) | recommended; the Toasted Barrel bottling (51.2%) works too; or any straight rye of 40-51% |
| 22.5 ml (¾ oz) | Cointreau | or any orange liqueur of the triple sec style, about 40% |
| 22.5 ml (¾ oz) | fresh lemon juice | squeezed the same day |
| 15 ml (1 tbsp) | loosened quince paste | plain membrillo (quince, sugar and lemon, with no nuts in the ingredients) mashed with an equal weight of just-boiled water; check the pack for a nut warning |
| 1 strip | orange peel | squeezed over the top |

**method**
1. Loosen the quince: put 30 g of quince paste (a slice about the size of a matchbox) in a cup with 30 ml of just-boiled water and mash it with a fork until no lumps are left. Let it cool. That's enough for three drinks, and it keeps in the fridge for a few days.
2. Put the coupe in the freezer.
3. Pour the loosened quince, the lemon juice, the Cointreau and the rye into a shaker.
4. Fill the shaker with ice and shake hard for about 12 seconds, until the outside is frosty.
5. Strain it into the cold coupe through the shaker's strainer and a small fine sieve held underneath, which catches any bits of quince.
6. Hold the orange peel over the drink, skin side down, and squeeze it so its oils fall on the top. Then lay it on the rim.

**closingLine:** *Mash the quince until no lumps are left. Then pick what you'll steer yourself, and let the rest run.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*Old Potrero began as a brewer's next idea, once other brewers had followed his lead.*

**whoYouAre**
You catch it before anyone has a word for it: a sound, a look, an idea a few people keep circling. And you're the one who gives it a shape, a name, a place, a first version people can point at and join.

People see the rebel. They don't see the plan, and you prefer it that way, because wanting to steer sounds like the very thing you were all against. So you steer quietly. What worries you is what it turns into afterwards: run by people who never got it, smoothed out for everyone, your hands nowhere near it.

And here's the good of it: most new things die young, talked out of it or copied badly. The ones you start tend to grow up, because someone was steering while they were fragile.

**yours**

1. In 1965, when factory beer ruled America, Fritz Maytag, from the washing-machine family, bought a failing San Francisco brewery called Anchor. Its beer, he admitted later, wasn't good: a lot of the time it was sort of sour. He brought in a microscope and, in his words, spent his whole nest egg on the brewery. It took ten years to make money.

2. What he'd started caught on: a San Francisco paper later called his beers the first drops of a tsunami. Others opened the breweries that followed. His own, he kept small. And in 1993 he set up stills in a corner of the brewery to make rye whiskey, a secret from all but a few of his staff. One drinks writer calls it an early, "perhaps too early", shot across the bows of American whisky.

3. When he sold, in 2010, the man who'd steered it for forty-five years said he'd do whatever the new boss said, to help them do well. Thirteen years later, under other owners, the brewery went quiet. A new owner has promised it back, though in the autumn of 2026 it still wasn't pouring. That's your fear, come true long after he'd let go. But look at what didn't stop: the small breweries everywhere, and a wave of new small-distillery ryes that the same book says began with his. I like to think that's why he could offer to take orders. He'd never needed the whole wave. Only to start it, and keep one part small enough to steer himself.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a rye Sidecar, after the renowned classic of brandy, orange liqueur and lemon, shaken hard over ice, then strained twice, the second time through a fine sieve, into a well-chilled coupe. The rye is Old Potrero, the name his first rye was sold under, though today's bottles are aged longer than that first one, and there's more of it than anything else. Where some bartenders add a little syrup, I add quince paste instead, loosened with hot water. Quince is a fruit hardly anyone eats raw, so hard and mouth-puckering it takes real work, and then it's wonderful, with a strong perfume. I chose it as a nod to a sour beer he took on, and put right.

5. So let me steer, just once. When something you started takes off, don't try to hold on to all of it. Decide what you'll keep steering yourself, and do it properly. Let the rest run in other people's hands. That isn't it getting away from you. It's how you'll know it was real. And it leaves you free for the next thing nobody has a name for yet.
