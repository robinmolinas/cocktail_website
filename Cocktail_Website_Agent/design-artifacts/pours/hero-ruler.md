# Pour — Dressed for It · The Warrior (hero-ruler)

## Cocktail

- **name:** Dressed for It
- **tagline:** Everything's gone wrong, and you still set the table at seven.
- **glassware:** small stemmed wine glass (about 200 ml), chilled, fine sugar on the outside of the rim, lined inside with a collar of lemon peel, no ice
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 ml | cognac steeped with black tea | 2 ordinary black-tea bags in 150 ml cognac (any VS or VSOP, 40% or stronger), 20 minutes, then lift the bags out; enough for two drinks |
| 5 ml (1 teaspoon) | rich simple syrup | 2 parts white sugar to 1 part water, by weight, warmed until clear (100 g sugar and 50 g water make about 110 ml, enough for about 20 drinks; it keeps about two weeks in the fridge) |
| 5 ml (1 teaspoon) | dry orange curaçao | or a triple sec |
| 2.5 ml (½ teaspoon) | fresh lemon juice | measure it: a full teaspoon makes it too sharp for a stirred drink |
| 2 dashes | Angostura bitters | |
| half a lemon | lemon peel, pared in one long piece | the collar |
| a saucer | fine (caster) sugar | for the outside of the rim |

**method**
1. Put the two tea bags in the cognac in a jar and leave them 20 minutes. Taste from 15 minutes on and lift them out, without squeezing, when the tea shows as a dry edge behind the brandy. Left too long, black tea turns harsh.
2. Chill a small stemmed wine glass (step 3 does it).
3. About an hour ahead, run a cut lemon around the outside of the rim, roll the outside of the rim in fine sugar so none falls into the glass, and put the glass in the fridge: the sugar sets and won't run.
4. Pare half a lemon in one long strip, the way you'd peel an apple, yellow only. Curl it and fit it inside the glass so it lines the bowl like a collar.
5. In a mixing glass, put the tea cognac, syrup, curaçao, lemon juice and bitters. Fill with ice and stir for about 20 to 30 seconds, until the outside of the mixing glass is very cold.
6. Strain into the glass, inside the collar. No ice.

**closingLine:** *Fit the peel inside the glass, then take your hand away: it stays.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*The Crusta is named for its sugar crust. Its 1862 recipe ends with two words: "Then smile."*

**whoYouAre**
The week everything goes wrong, you still put a proper dinner on the table. You still turn up on Monday ironed and on time, and you'd quite like everyone else to as well. People roll their eyes. Someone always asks whether this is really the moment.

It is, and you know why, even if you seldom explain it. In a crisis, people watch whoever's in front to see how serious it is, and a rule that's still being kept tells them it isn't over. You rarely admit that the rules hold you up too, or that some nights you've agreed with every person who told you it was a terrible idea. What you guard against is wobbling in front of them. But look at what your rules give people: in their worst week, someone still expects something of them, so they still count. That's respect, and most people never think to offer it.

**yours**

1. After Hurricane Katrina, Neal Bodenheimer, then tending bar in New York, felt he should be back in New Orleans. Many people from the city who lived elsewhere felt that if they didn't go home, the city might not survive. So he came home. His idea was to be one of the first there with a cocktail bar standing on its own, not inside a hotel or a restaurant. He bought a building in Freret, a lonely neighbourhood far from the French Quarter, and his partner, Matt Kohnke, loved it. At least ten to twenty people tried to talk him out of it: a terrible idea, they said. Years later he admitted that at first they'd been a little nervous too, and that they couldn't afford to be anywhere else.

2. Cure opened in 2009. He handed the drinks over to his bartenders. What he did give the bar was a dress code: no shorts, no flip-flops, no baseball caps. It gave the place a reputation it never quite lived down. For the first nine months to a year, he said, they spent their time persuading people that they should drink cocktails, and that the bar wasn't just pretentious.

3. Then comes my favourite part. Years later, talking about Cure's first days, he didn't say the critics had been wrong. He said craft cocktails had partly earned that label, and that they really had to work on their service. So they did. Cure did well from the start, and later he and his partners opened more bars. I like to think the dress code was never really about shorts. In what he called, in a way, a decaying city, where people started over, it said: this place still expects something of you. That's you. And the rest is you at your best: he granted the part of the criticism that was fair, worked on it, and kept going.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Brandy Crusta, a New Orleans classic from the mid-1800s. It's famous for how it's dressed: its name means the crust the sugar makes around the rim, and a long lemon peel lines the glass like a collar. Inside go cognac, a teaspoon apiece of dry curaçao (orange liqueur) and syrup, Angostura, and lemon, just half a teaspoon, all stirred until icy and strained into a little wineglass from the fridge, no ice in it: deep amber and rich. My main change is in the cognac. It sits for twenty minutes with ordinary black tea, so the firm grip at the end comes from the tea, not from more lemon. I did that as a nod to you: firm, without ever raising your voice. By 1908, one New York newspaperman was already sneering at sugar rims as old-fashioned. I kept the crust anyway.

5. Next time you're keeping a standard and someone calls it pretentious, or too much, don't hear it as all or nothing. Find the part they're right about, admit it plainly, and fix that part. You'll worry it looks like wobbling. It doesn't. That's how the rest of what you stand for holds, and people will trust your rules more for it.
