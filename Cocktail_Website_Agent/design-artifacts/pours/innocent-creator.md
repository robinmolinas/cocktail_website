# Pour — The Way It Felt · The Naive Artist (innocent-creator)

## Cocktail

- **name:** The Way It Felt
- **tagline:** Your creations get the feeling right before the rules get a say.
- **glassware:** tall glass (about 400 ml), filled with the frozen Kalimotxo cubes
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 100 ml | young Spanish red wine, unoaked (a young Rioja or Garnacha, the kind poured by the glass) | fridge-cold; never a grand bottle |
| 100 ml | Coca-Cola, recommended (or any full-sugar cola) | fridge-cold, freshly opened: the fizz comes from this pour |
| about 8 cubes (a glassful) | cubes frozen from the same half and half | made first, below; they melt into more of the drink, not water |

**method**
1. **The cubes (at least 6 hours ahead).** Stir 150 ml red wine and 150 ml cola together in a jug until the fizz settles, so the tray doesn't foam over. Pour into an ice-cube tray (a silicone one is easiest: the cubes come out soft) and freeze on the coldest setting. They freeze softer than water ice, a bit like an ice lolly.
2. Put the wine and a fresh bottle of cola in the fridge, so both are cold.
3. Fill a tall glass to the top with the cubes, straight from the freezer.
4. Pour in the wine, then the cola, slowly: it foams up on the cubes.
5. Stir once, gently, and serve it straight away.

**closingLine:** *Stir once, over its own ice. How it's usually done can wait until they've tasted it.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*Half red wine, half cola. There's a right bottle to mix, apparently.*

**whoYouAre**
What you've made, you hand over straight away: a cake iced the colour the day felt, a card you drew instead of buying one, a birthday song with one verse too many. It's obviously lovely. It hasn't crossed your mind that there's a proper way to do it.

Then someone explains, kindly, how it's usually done. You nod. You still don't see what was wrong with it. The day you'd dread is the one when the explanation starts to make sense. Because what you make rarely looks like the thing. It looks like how the thing felt, and the people it's for can always tell. That's rarer than getting it right, and it's worth more.

**yours**

1. In the Basque Country, people pour red wine and cola into a tall glass of ice, half and half, and call it a Kalimotxo. Spaniards had probably been mixing the two since at least the 1920s. The name, the story goes, came at a festival in Algorta in 1972, after a friend's nickname, and within about ten years it had caught on through the Basque Country and Navarre. In much of the former Yugoslavia it's bambus; in Hungary, vadász.

2. People who know wine have their doubts. A writer in the *Guardian* once remembered a tapas bar in Granada where his partner started pouring cola into a glass of the good local red. The barman saw it and came down the bar, pointing: the house wine from the barrel is the one you mix, and this bottle, you don't.

3. In Bilbao, they take it seriously. The most trusted account of its history was put together by an artists' collective there called Funky Project. The *Oxford Companion to Spirits and Cocktails* admits that doesn't sound much like an authority, then takes them at their word anyway, for that very reason.

4. I like to think you'd have done what the Basques did: looked at the wine and the cola, and not seen the problem. So the cocktail I've made for you is a Kalimotxo, the way they pour it: red wine and Coca-Cola, half and half, stirred. My red is a young Spanish one, the kind poured by the glass. It's a Basque classic, dark, cold and fizzy, a little like sangria. I haven't corrected a thing. The one change is the ice. It's the same drink, frozen into cubes first, so as it melts it turns into more of itself, never into water, so nothing waters it down. The fizz comes from the fresh pour. That's for you: something you make should still be what you made, right to the last of it.

5. So when someone next explains the proper way, listen. Some of it will be useful. Then make the next thing the way it looked to you, and hand it straight to whoever you made it for, before anyone else sees it. They'll see what you meant. And you'll get to enjoy making it again, the way you did before anyone explained.
