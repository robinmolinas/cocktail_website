# Pour — On the Record · The Lone Voice (sage-outlaw)

## Cocktail

- **name:** On the Record
- **tagline:** You don't like being the only one. You like pretending even less.
- **glassware:** small stemmed port glass (about 150 ml), chilled, no ice
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 20 ml | ruby port | a young, non-vintage ruby |
| 40 ml | 10-year-old tawny port | any tawny with "10 years old" on the label |
| 25 ml | cold strong black tea | plain black tea (not Earl Grey or any flavoured tea), no sugar, no milk: 2 tea bags in 150 ml just-boiled water for 8 minutes, then cooled in the fridge |
| 1 strip | orange peel | |

**method**
1. Make the tea: steep 2 bags of plain black tea in 150 ml of just-boiled water for 8 minutes, twice as long as for a cup, so it comes out strong and a little drying. Lift the bags out, let it cool, and keep it in the fridge (it lasts two days).
2. Put the glass in the freezer for 10 minutes.
3. In a mixing glass or a jar, pour the ruby first, then the tawny, then the cold tea.
4. Fill with ice and stir for about 15 seconds, until the outside of the glass is cold.
5. Strain into the chilled glass, with no ice.
6. Squeeze the orange peel over the top so its oils fall on the drink, then set it aside.

**closingLine:** *Ruby first, then twice the tawny. Make the second pour the bigger one.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*Port goes in twice here: once in short, then again at greater length.*

**whoYouAre**
You looked into it properly: the figures behind the plan everyone's excited about, the story the family has told for years, the way things have always been done at work. And it doesn't hold. So you said so, plainly, with what you'd found. The room went quiet, and not the good kind.

Here's what you don't show. You don't enjoy standing there on your own, not at all. Part of you hopes you're wrong, because being wrong would make life simpler. So you check it again, usually late, and it's still there. Then you say it again, with more of it. What you can't live with is the comfortable version becoming the official one, with nobody having said a word.

And often, later, someone finds you, a little quietly, to say they'd wondered the same. Because you said it first, they get to say it second. That's a gift, though it rarely feels like one at the time.

**yours**

1. In 1831 a young man born in Hull, Joseph James Forrester, went out to Oporto, in Portugal, to join his uncle, a partner in a port house there. He became a merchant and wine shipper, and over the years he came to believe the trade was being badly run: port mishandled in the making, and the Douro Wine Company, which held a monopoly dating from 1756, taxed exports heavily and limited how much could be shipped. He blamed both for the slump the port trade was in.

2. In 1844 he set it out in a pamphlet, without his name on it. It ran through eight printings fast, so plenty of people wanted a copy. He was easily found out anyway, and bitterly attacked by those with a stake in the trade as it was. I like to think he'd hoped the pages would be judged on their own.

3. What he did next is why I thought of you. He didn't go quiet. By the next year he had more on the same subject in print. And in 1852 he gave evidence to a committee of the House of Commons, under his own name this time, setting out at greater length every abuse his pamphlet had summed up. He wasn't on his own: the people of the wine country backed him warmly. He had a stake too: he shipped port himself, and what that committee was weighing was Britain's duty on wine. And not everyone since agrees he was right about all of it. But what stays with me is the order of things. First the attack, then more of the argument, not less. Most people only ever see the first time you say something. They don't see you checking it again, and coming back with more.

4. So the cocktail I've made for you is port, twice. It's stirred and served like a Martini, with port where the gin would be: first a short measure of young ruby, fresh and fruity, then twice as much tawny, a blend of wines from several years. The same kind of wine, said again at greater length, as a nod to the way he kept saying it. Port tends to be sweet, and rather than add anything to make it go down easier, I dried it with strong, cold black tea. On the flavour chart I work from, port and tea sit in the same family of aromas. Orange oil goes on top, and it's served with no ice, in a small, chilled port glass.

5. Let me leave you with this. When a room goes cold on you, it's tempting to read it as an answer. On bad nights it feels like proof you're wrong. On good ones, like proof you're right. It's neither. Forrester was attacked by the people with a stake and backed by the people of the wine country, and neither of those settled whether he was right. So the next time it goes quiet, let it, and go back to what you found. If it's still there, say it again, with more of it. And if one day it isn't, you'll be the one who says so, out loud. That's exactly why people can trust what you tell them.

**Conflicts** (fact card)

- **C1, the title.** DNB: *A Word or two on Port Wine*; Barr (FTS): *A Word or Two on Port Wine!* (London, 1844); Oxford pdf 707: "A Word or Two about Port". Two against one: if a title is told, use *A Word or Two on Port Wine*; better, leave it out.
- **C2, whom he accused.** Ludington, *The Politics of Wine in Britain* (2013, FTS highlight): Forrester "charged his fellow port shippers with adulterating" (cut off). The DNB frames it as abuses plus the monopoly. So the accused may well have included his own trade. The DNB's attackers stay "the persons interested" (people with a stake); the reading never names them as shippers, and never says his trade stood with him either.
- **C3, whether he was right.** Barr (FTS highlight): his account of adulterated port in the 1840s "has generally been written off by wine" (cut off, the end of the sentence unread). *Edinburgh Review* 1867 (FTS): a writer "goes out of his way to disparage" him after his death, and disputes his treacle claim for the present day. Oxford pdf 707 sides with him on elderberries. Sources disagree: no "he was right", no "the port was full of elderberries" as fact.
- **C4, his own stake.** Simpson, *Creating Wine* (2011, FTS highlights, three separate fragments): "the port-wine shipper J. J. Forrester, believed that if duties were cut sufficiently" (cut off); a different fragment, "“everyone” to be able to afford to drink it" (subject and "it" unread); and "Forrester, as many others in the debate, was not a disinter-" (cut off; almost certainly "disinterested"). Safe level: he shipped port and was not a disinterested party in the 1852 debate on Britain's wine import duties. **Struck (mine, r2):** "he wanted duties cut far enough for everyone to afford port", which merged two cut-off fragments. Never tell him as a pure, selfless lone truth-teller.
