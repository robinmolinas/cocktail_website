# Pour — Work It Out · The Initiate (hero-innocent)

## Cocktail

- **name:** Work It Out
- **tagline:** You know there's more in you, and keep working until it shows.
- **glassware:** tall highball glass (about 400 ml), filled with ice
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 ml | vodka | any vodka, 37.5% or stronger |
| 90 ml | plain beef broth, made at home | beef shin and bones, onion, carrot and water; no salt, no stock cube; made the day before; made with beef: not for vegetarians |
| 5 ml | fresh orange juice | one of the two orange touches |
| 5 ml | medium dry sherry | an amontillado style works |
| 1 small pinch | fine salt | the broth has none of its own |
| 1 strip | orange peel | the other orange touch |

**method**
1. The day before, make the broth: put about 1 kg of beef shin on the bone, one onion cut in half and one carrot in a large pan with 2 litres of cold water. Bring it slowly to a bare simmer and keep it there for about 3 hours, skimming off the grey foam as it rises. Strain through a fine sieve, add no salt, let it cool and keep it in the fridge (it keeps 3 days, or freeze it). Lift off the fat once it's cold.
2. If the broth has set to a soft jelly in the fridge, that's a good broth. Warm it just until it pours again, then let it cool to room temperature.
3. Fill a tall glass to the top with ice.
4. Put the vodka, 90 ml of broth, the orange juice, the sherry and a small pinch of salt in a shaker with plenty of ice.
5. Shake hard for about ten seconds, then strain into the glass.
6. Squeeze a strip of orange peel over the drink so its oils fall on it, then lay the peel on the ice.

**closingLine:** *Squeeze the orange peel over it last. When nobody will say what's wrong, read every menu you're handed.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*Vodka, beef broth, and an orange someone found on a Chinese menu.*

**whoYouAre**
Someone you look up to tries something you've worked hard on and says, "Not quite." Nothing else. You smile, say you'll have another go, and that night you're still turning it over, long after they've forgotten they said it. You'd never admit how much one sentence from the right person can move you. Or why: you're sure there's more in you than anyone has seen so far, and you don't have the proof yet.

What you dread is the day it's for real: being asked for it properly, in front of the very person you wanted to impress, and having nothing to show. So you listen to people who know more as if every word might be the key. It's also your gift to them. People love teaching you, because you take a hard word home and come back with something. But part of you is still waiting for them to hand you the answer. Some answers nobody can hand you.

**yours**

1. In the mid-eighties, a legendary New York restaurateur named Joe Baum was opening a new restaurant, Aurora, and he tried out a bartender called Dale DeGroff to run its cocktail bar. DeGroff had spent six years behind a hotel bar in Los Angeles, learning spirits and cocktails on his own; the way one book tells it, so he wouldn't lose a good job he knew was beyond his qualifications. Perhaps his regulars noticed. The wider world didn't.

2. Baum rarely explained himself. He mostly told DeGroff whether he was going the right way or the wrong way. "Joe never told me anything," DeGroff said. One day Baum tasted his beef-broth drink and said: "There's something wrong with it. Fix it." DeGroff couldn't work out what. Baum also wanted fresh juice behind the bar, never a bottled sour mix, and when DeGroff asked what they'd do if it got busy, Baum told him that if it was more than he could manage, someone else would get the job.

3. The answer turned up somewhere else entirely: at a Chinese restaurant, where DeGroff saw beef with orange on the menu. He took the lemon and lime out of his drink and put orange in, and Baum tasted it and said OK. Baum never pointed him to the orange. I like to think he couldn't have: he could taste that something was wrong, but the answer had to be DeGroff's, and it stayed his. More than thirty years later, the orange is still in the recipe DeGroff gives for it. You treat the people you learn from as if they're holding the answer and deciding when to let you have it. The answers that make you who you are come from somewhere nobody pointed you, and they're yours for good.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is built on the Bullshot, as DeGroff prints it: vodka shaken with beef broth. It was once everywhere, from advertising men to film stars, and it's half-forgotten now, which is a shame, because one drinks historian calls it surprisingly good when it's made with care. So I make the broth at home the day before, slowly and plainly, then shake it cold with the vodka, a splash of medium dry sherry, a little salt and a little fresh orange juice, and squeeze an orange peel over it at the end. I left out his hot sauce and pepper, and there's no lemon or lime anywhere in it. I kept both touches of orange, because the orange is the answer he found for himself. It comes out a warm amber, savoury and deep, and the orange lifts it.

5. So if someone whose opinion matters to you says "not quite" and stops there, don't hold out for an explanation. Take it home, and let the answer come from wherever it turns up: a conversation on a train, a shop window, something you read for another reason. When you bring it back, it'll be yours, and so will everything you build on it. Nobody is going to tell you when you're good enough. You'll know it the day your answer is one they hadn't thought of.
