# Pour — Overnight · The Makeover Artist (creator-magician)

## Cocktail

- **name:** Overnight
- **tagline:** You change everything except the part that makes it theirs.
- **glassware:** a small tumbler (rocks glass), chilled, no ice
- **contains:** `["dairy"]`

**recipe**

Makes about eight drinks of 125 ml.

| amount (batch) | item | note (shown) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 480 ml | aged rum, steeped with 15 g loose black tea | the milk softens the tea's harsh edge and leaves its flavour |
| 360 ml | tamarind water (below) | the star; sour tamarind, never sweet |
| 80 ml | fresh lime juice | |
| 60 ml | demerara syrup (equal weights demerara sugar and hot water) | |
| 240 ml | cold whole milk | it curdles, and the curds are strained out |

**Tamarind water:** 150 g block of sour, seedless tamarind pulp (from a supermarket's world-food aisle or an Asian grocer; the packet should not say "sweet"). Break it up into a bowl, pour on 375 ml just-boiled water, leave 30 minutes, mash it with a fork, and press it through a sieve with the back of a spoon. Keep 360 ml.

**method**
1. Put the tea in the rum in a jar, shake it, and leave it 20 to 30 minutes, until it's as dark as strong tea. Strain out the leaves.
2. Make the tamarind water.
3. Mix the tea rum, tamarind water, lime and demerara syrup in a jug. Taste it. It should be too sour to enjoy: the milk softens it. If it isn't, add lime a little at a time until it is.
4. Pour the milk into a large bowl. Pour the punch into the milk slowly, stirring gently as you go (the punch into the milk, never the milk into the punch). Within a minute or so the milk breaks into soft curds, like scrambled egg in cloudy liquid. It looks ruined. That's right.
5. Stop stirring. Leave it an hour.
6. Set a sieve lined with a paper coffee filter over a clean jug, and pour everything in, curds and all. The first cup runs cloudy: pour it back over the curds. Once the curds have settled on the filter, they do the cleaning. Leave it to drip in the fridge overnight. Don't press it.
7. Bottle what came through and keep it in the fridge; it's at its silkiest in the first week. Throw away the curds.
8. Pour it cold from the bottle into a chilled small tumbler, about 125 ml. No ice.

**closingLine:** *Leave it overnight, and don't fix what you find.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*Last night, this drink looked like a mistake.*

**whoYouAre**
Show you almost anything and you see what it could be: the chair someone put out on the pavement, a friend's flat, the haircut they've had since school. Once you start on it, you'll change nearly everything, except the one thing that makes it theirs. You find that part first, and build the rest around it. So the words you're hoping for at the end aren't "I'd never have recognised it." They're "That's so you."

What nobody sees is the middle. There's always a point where it looks worse than when you started: the room stripped to the walls, the paint half on, a friend at the mirror not sure what you've done. You're the only one who isn't worried. You've been through enough middles to know the mess is part of how it works. What worries you comes later, once you've gone: that it all slides back, or that it turns out in some way you didn't mean. What stays is how you looked at them: as if the best of them was worth moving everything else for. Most people have never been looked at like that.

**yours**

1. In 2012, Dave Arnold was asked to make Arnold Palmers, iced tea and lemonade, with alcohol in them, for a film crew. He made several. Everyone agreed they weren't very good. Afterwards he tried something that sounds like a way to make it worse: he poured his tea-flavoured vodka into milk. The milk curdled, which looks exactly like a mistake. But the curds do the work. They catch the harsh, drying edge of the tea and carry it off with them, and what comes through is smooth. The tea flavour, he wrote, was still very strong. It went on his bar's menu. It had also come out silky, which he hadn't planned at all. He called that an "unintended side benefit".

2. He wasn't the first to curdle a drink on purpose. Milk punch is on record from 1688, and by 1763 Benjamin Franklin was writing the curdled kind into a letter: pour boiling milk into the punch, leave it to stand for two hours, then strain it. The part where it looks ruined is right there in the recipe, written as a wait. And made that way, it kept. Bottles of it were found in Charles Dickens's cellar after he died.

3. Here's what I see in it, and it's only my reading. Nobody turned that tea into something else. The harsh part went, and what was left tasted more like itself. And the part that looked like failure was the part doing the work. You've known that for a long time. It's how you can stand in the middle of a mess and not flinch.

4. The one I'd make you starts with tamarind: a brown, sticky pulp, about the least glamorous thing in a kitchen. It's most of the sour in the drink, with a little lime, and together they break the milk. With them go aged rum steeped with black tea, and a little brown sugar. It's made a day ahead: all of it stirred into milk, left to curdle, and strained back through its own curds. What comes through is smooth, softer than what went in, and it still tastes of tamarind. It's poured cold, from the bottle. Whoever drinks it never sees the middle.

5. Next time something you've changed comes out differently from the picture in your head, don't fix it straight away. Give it a night. Look at it the way you'd look at that chair on the pavement. Arnold was only trying to get rid of a harsh edge, and he got silk. Some of your best work will be the part you didn't mean.

### Legends and inferences

**Dossier only** (Hester, `historian-anchors.md`)

- **1688 is milk punch in general**, not the curdled kind (audit v1 X3). Never put 1688 next to "curdled".
- **Not "invented"** for Franklin, Rockett or Arnold. Franklin *wrote a recipe down*. Behn only "possibly" (never state). First record 1688; earliest written recipe 1711.
- **The milk's direction.** "Liquor into milk" is Arnold's modern rule (LI p. 271). The old recipes put hot milk into the punch (Franklin; Oxford's Rockett adaptation). Never call our method Rockett's or Franklin's.
- **"Back through its own curds"** is Oxford's modern adaptation, not verified as 1711 wording: say it of *our* method, not of history. Franklin's word on record is "till it is clear", but that's the dossier only: **"clear" is not the reveal word** (Wren; Half a Rim).
- **"Keeps for years"**: not on the page. Oxford: indefinite storage without refrigeration, Dickens's bottles (undated). Never claim it for our punch.
- **"Rough brandy"** (Arnold's guess about Franklin): inference, out.
- **Royal warrant**: out (Oxford contradicts itself: Whisson vs Hudson; "mid-eighteenth century" vs Victoria).
- **Arnold Palmer** = iced tea and lemonade (LI p. 263); his versions were "alcoholic", spirit unnamed: "with alcohol in it", not "a spirit". He made "several", not "a batch".
- **Arnold's quotes**: "weren't very good" and "unintended side benefit" are his, under 15 words. "The tea flavor was still very strong" is his too.
- **Tamarind**: "reputed/known as", never "is the most acidic and sweetest". Colour ("amber") is Tomás's expectation, not a fact.
- **"The milk leaves the sour alone"**: Tomás's inference from LI p. 265; p. 266 says proteins also strip some colour and flavour. Signpost it or keep it out.
- **Siblings**: no "it was always there" (As It Was); no "rescued a bad drink" as the whole story (Half a Rim), since Arnold's is about what stays, not what's refused; "leave it once it breaks" sits close to No Accident's "Don't stir it". Say "let it rest".
- Franklin's recipient and date (James Bowdoin, 11 Oct 1763): Founders Online listing only, page not fetched. Say "a 1763 letter".
