# Pour — Worth the Trade · The Good Wizard (magician-hero)

## Cocktail

- **name:** Worth the Trade
- **tagline:** Everyone remembers that you saved it. You remember what you had to change.
- **glassware:** old-fashioned glass (rocks glass, about 300 ml), one large ice cube
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 45 ml | VS cognac | made mostly from Ugni Blanc, the grape that replaced Folle Blanche once the vines were saved |
| 15 ml | young Armagnac made from Folle Blanche | look for "Folle Blanche" on the label, young or unaged, 40% or stronger. The grape that made way in Cognac and is still grown in Armagnac. If you can't find one, any young Armagnac gives the same balance, but it may hold little or none of the Folle Blanche |
| 10 ml (2 teaspoons) | gooseberry syrup, homemade (below) | |
| | *for the syrup:* 200 g green gooseberries (fresh, or frozen and thawed), 100 g white sugar | blend cold until smooth, press through a fine sieve; keeps about a week in a closed jar in the fridge. Not cape gooseberries (physalis): a different fruit |

**method**
1. Make the syrup ahead: blend the gooseberries and sugar until smooth, with no heat. Press the purée through a fine sieve with the back of a spoon and keep the liquid.
2. Put the large cube in the old-fashioned glass to chill it.
3. Pour the cognac, then the Folle Blanche Armagnac, then the syrup, into a mixing glass or a jar. Fill it with ice cubes and stir for about 20 seconds, until the outside feels cold.
4. Strain it over the large cube. No garnish: the Folle Blanche's flower is the scent on top.

**closingLine:** *Keep the quarter of Folle Blanche, however small it looks. Once it's safe, find a place again for what you had to give up.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*Cognac was replanted on a rootstock from Texas. The grape that couldn't follow is here too.*

**whoYouAre**
Something is going under: a friend's business losing money every month, a plan for a big day that has come apart, a team about to lose the thing it built. Everyone else is afraid of making it worse, so nobody touches it. You're the one who says, quietly, "this is what has to change." Then you see it through, step by step, until it stands again.

Afterwards, while the others are relieved, a part of you isn't, quite. Something had to give so the rest could hold: the old name over the door, the bigger room, the side of the project someone loved most. It worked, and you still think about that part. What you fear is what it might do that you didn't mean. It's why people trust you with the change: you weigh what it will take before anyone else thinks to ask, then you make the call anyway, and carry it yourself. Because of you, things that would have been lost altogether are still here.

**yours**

1. In the 1870s, the vineyards around Cognac were the biggest vineyard region in the world. Then a louse called phylloxera arrived, and by 1893 six hectares in every seven were gone.

2. The way out took over a decade to find: a rootstock, found in Texas, that could grow in Cognac's deep chalk, with the French vines grafted on top. The vineyard was saved by being changed. But one grape couldn't make the change. Folle Blanche, the region's floral grape, didn't suit grafting: its bunches grew so tight that they rotted. So Cognac planted Ugni Blanc, with far less scent, and made fine cognac from it all the same. The save was real, and much bigger than what gave way.

3. I like to think whoever chose the new roots knew what they were giving up, and chose it anyway, because the alternative was nothing at all. That's the call you make. Better still, Folle Blanche never disappeared. It made way in Cognac, but further south, in Armagnac, it's still grown, and its brandy comes out floral and fruity, at its best while it's young.

4. The cocktail I've made for you starts with cognac, from the grape that came through, and gives the other one its place back, as a nod to the part you had to let go: a smaller pour of young Armagnac made from Folle Blanche, a quarter of the brandy. In Gascony one of its names means lip-stinger, which hints at how sharp it is. Its brandy keeps the flowers but very little of the sharpness, because spirits carry almost no acid. So I've put some back with a syrup of green gooseberries, a fruit that can stand in for grapes in cooking. It's built like a great Old-Fashioned, and it comes out bright and fragrant: two brandies, with a green, sharp edge.

5. Once you've pulled something through and it's safe again, look for whatever had to make way. More often than you'd think, it hasn't gone anywhere, and now there's room. Bring a little of it back in, and let yourself enjoy the whole of what you saved.
