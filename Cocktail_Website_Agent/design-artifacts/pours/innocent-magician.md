# Pour — Let's Try It · The Dreamer (innocent-magician)

## Cocktail

- **name:** Let's Try It
- **tagline:** You notice the little signs that make a big hope feel possible.
- **glassware:** a tall glass (highball, about 300 ml), filled with ice
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 45 ml | Lark Classic Cask Tasmanian single malt whisky (43%), recommended | or any Tasmanian single malt, 40-46%; not cask strength. If you can't find a Tasmanian one, any single malt of 40-46% aged at least partly in wine, sherry or port casks |
| 110 ml | cold still water | from the fridge; not sparkling |
| 10 ml (2 teaspoons) | Glenfarclas single malt Scotch whisky, recommended | or any Scotch single malt; added last |
| to fill | ice | ordinary cubes |

**method**
1. Fill a tall glass with ice.
2. Pour in the Lark, then the cold water, and stir a few turns, until the outside of the glass feels cold.
3. Add the two teaspoons of Glenfarclas last, over the top, and give it one more turn.

**closingLine:** *The Glenfarclas goes in last. Then find out what's really in the way.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*Tasmania's new whisky began on a fishing trip: good water, good barley, and nobody making any.*

**whoYouAre**
Little things add up for you. The friend who lights up whenever baking comes up. The job advert you know would suit someone perfectly. The two people at a party who keep ending up talking to each other. Put enough of them together and you can see how something could turn out, well ahead of everyone else. To you, that isn't wishing. It's where the signs point.

But you learned early to keep it quiet. "Don't get your hopes up" was nearly always said kindly, by people who loved you. So now every hope comes with a little "probably not" in front of it, said first, to beat everyone else to it. What you dread is the look afterwards, and worse, someone telling you that you got their hopes up.

And still you don't stop. When a person close to you puts a hope down, you quietly pick it up and keep it for them until they're ready to have it back. Most people just tell them to be realistic.

**yours**

1. Tasmania, the island south of mainland Australia, owes its whisky today to a fishing trip. Bill Lark and his father-in-law were sitting up in the highlands, talking over what the island already had: some of the best water in the world, as they saw it, good barley, and a climate that seemed fine for whisky. And nobody was making any. Then Bill's wife, Lyn, arrived, heard the idea and said, more or less, that they should try it. Neither Bill nor Lyn had ever distilled.

2. What stood in the way was an old rule. Since 1901, Australian law had asked for stills so big that only large industrial firms could distil, and by then even the big whisky makers had stopped: Australians had turned to cheaper imports and away from their own brands. I like to think a whole country had been told not to get its hopes up about its whisky. In 1992 Bill persuaded the government to rewrite the rule, and, as he tells it, the politicians helped at once. Lyn and Bill were the first to get a licence.

3. Within two weeks of the licence, the phone rang at ten at night. It was John Grant, of the Glenfarclas distillery in Scotland, offering to help them make good whisky. His reason, as Bill remembers it: if somebody tastes whisky for the first time from the Larks' distillery, it should be a good first time. As others on the island started distilling, Bill decided he should help them too.

4. This is where you come in. The idea that day wasn't a wish. They'd looked at what was in front of them and added it up. The "no", when it came, wasn't a person at all, just a rule that had stood since 1901. And once the door was open, Bill held it for the ones coming after him, the way a distillery in Scotland had held it for him. That's what you do with other people's hopes. You keep them going until they can carry them again, and some of them come true because you did.

5. The cocktail I've made for you is a whisky highball, one of the great simple drinks, made still: a Tasmanian single malt, Lark's own if you can find it, with cold water over ice, for the water and barley they talked about that day. The last thing in is two teaspoons of Glenfarclas, the Scotch from the distillery whose call came at ten at night. It's there for the help that turns up before there's anything to show for it, and for the hopes you keep for other people.

6. Whenever you can see how something could go, for yourself or for someone close, drop the "probably not". Say what you've added up, plainly, and then find out what's really in the way. It's often smaller than the hope: an old rule, a form, one person nobody has thought to ask. And sometimes the first answer is a yes.
