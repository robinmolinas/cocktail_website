# Pour — Cold Water First · The Entertainer (magician-regular-guy)

## Cocktail

- **name:** Cold Water First
- **tagline:** You practise alone so that strangers can laugh together.
- **glassware:** a small heatproof glass tumbler (about 300 ml), warmed with hot water first
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 30 ml | Caol Ila 12 Islay single malt Scotch whisky (43%), recommended | from the distillery whose malt Thomas kept in barrels in his cellar, the historian David Wondrich writes; or any Islay single malt at ordinary bottling strength (40–46%) |
| 30 ml | Irish single pot still whiskey (40–46%) | his recipe from the following year mixed Scotch and Irish (half and half, as Wondrich reads it) |
| 14 g | demerara sugar | a slightly heaped tablespoon |
| 1 | piece of lemon peel, pared in one go, no white pith | one long piece, as Wondrich advises |
| 150 ml | water, just off the boil | about two-thirds of a cup |

**method**
1. Fill the tumbler with hot water to warm it, then pour that water away.
2. Put the sugar and the lemon peel in the glass. Press the peel into the sugar with the back of a spoon a few times, so the sugar pulls out the oil in the peel.
3. Pour in the water, just off the boil, and stir until the sugar has disappeared.
4. Add both whiskies and stir once.
5. Cover the glass with a saucer, so the lemon and the smoke stay in the glass instead of drifting into the room.
6. Lift the saucer off just before you drink it, and smell it before the first sip.

**closingLine:** *Lift the saucer yourself. Then practise the next trick with someone.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*People came to watch the fire. This is what was underneath it.*

**whoYouAre**
A pack of cards on a kitchen table, or at a bus stop while the bus is late, and for one minute the ordinary world goes strange. Grown-ups turn back into children. Whoever happened to be standing there gets it.

People think you do it for the gasp. The gasp is lovely, but what you really practise for is the second after, when two people who've never met look at each other and laugh. You're the only one there who knows how it's done, so you don't get the wonder. You get their faces, and that's what you came for. What you'd rarely say is how much it matters, and how much you dread the silence where that laugh should be, in front of people who owe you nothing. That's what the hours are against, and what they buy is a minute in which you turn a bus stop full of strangers into a room. Very few people can do that with a pack of cards.

**yours**

1. The Blue Blazer is one of the drinks Jerry Thomas is remembered for: hot whisky set alight and poured back and forth between two mugs in a long arc of blue fire. In his 1862 book he gave almost no instructions for the throw. What he did give was a warning to beginners not to scald themselves, and one piece of advice: to get good at it, you'd have to practise for some time with cold water.

2. By the 1880s the smart bars had tired of it. In 1883 a Chicago bartender sniffed that it was a drink for the country, where people cared more for watching the blaze than for the drink. I'd take that as the compliment he didn't mean. It nearly vanished by 1900 and came back about a hundred years later.

3. I like to think every bartender who threw one started the way the book said: at an empty bar before opening, pouring cold water from mug to mug, and missing, with nobody to impress. That's the bit of you strangers never get: the cold water. For the throw itself, it's nearly all the advice Thomas left.

4. The cocktail I've made for you starts from what was in the mugs while the crowd watched the fire. Take away the fire and the pouring, says David Wondrich, the historian, and the Blazer is "merely a Scotch Whisky Skin": whisky, lemon peel and boiling water. Thomas's recipe from the following year mixes Scotch and Irish whisky in one mug, so I've kept both. One is a gently smoky Scotch from the island of Islay, ideally Caol Ila, the distillery whose malt Thomas kept in his cellar, Wondrich writes. The other is an Irish whiskey made the old way, in a pot still, round and rich. We don't know why he mixed them. My guess is that two whiskies that rarely share a glass get on better than anyone expects, and I kept both as a nod to what you do at bus stops. Demerara sugar and one long ribbon of lemon peel go into a warm glass, then the boiling water, then the whiskies. His recipe ends by covering the glass, most likely to put the flame out. I've kept the cover and given it a new job: a saucer keeps the lemon and the smoke in while it steeps. The fire went into the smoke. And the saucer coming off is the one reveal that's just for you.

5. So when you're learning the next one, don't wait until it's perfect. Let one person watch you practise, spills and all. You'll find they laugh just as much, and you'll be in the laugh instead of watching it from behind the cards.
