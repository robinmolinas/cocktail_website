# Pour — Up Close · The Magnetic Leader (ruler-lover)

## Cocktail

- **name:** Up Close
- **tagline:** You know exactly what your attention does. And you mean all of it.
- **glassware:** stemmed cocktail glass (about 180 ml), chilled, no ice
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 small square (about 2.5 cm, 15 g) | fresh ripe pineapple | cut from near the base, where it's sweetest; no bigger, or the drink turns sharp |
| ½ tsp (2.5 ml) | rich sugar syrup | 2 parts sugar to 1 part water, stirred until clear |
| 60 ml | straight rye whiskey | any straight rye, 40% or stronger; the numbers were run on 50% |
| 20 ml | sweet vermouth | a red Italian-style vermouth |
| 2 dashes | Angostura bitters | or any aromatic bitters |
| 1 | strip of lemon peel | squeezed over the top, then dropped in |

**method**
1. Put the cocktail glass in the freezer, or fill it with ice and water while you mix.
2. Put the pineapple and the syrup in a mixing glass or jar. Press the pineapple with a muddler or the end of a rolling pin until its juice runs.
3. Add the rye, the vermouth and the bitters. Fill the mixing glass with ice.
4. Stir for about 30 seconds, until the outside of the glass feels very cold.
5. Empty the cocktail glass. Strain the drink into it through a small tea strainer, so no pineapple gets through.
6. Squeeze the lemon peel over the top so its oils fall on the drink, then drop it in.

**closingLine:** *Strain the pineapple out. It's still there at the rim. So are you, even when you're not trying.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*A Monte Carlo barman kept his Manhattans plain. This one borrows from a regular's cocktail.*

**whoYouAre**
Someone comes back after a year away, and you ask about the interview they were dreading. You remember their sister's name. You stay with them a second longer than they expect. They go home feeling remembered.

You know exactly what your attention does, and you give it on purpose, because a lot holds together when you do. People who watch from further off call it charm, and sometimes mean it as a warning. They've got it backwards: it works because you mean it. You hardly mention what it costs, or the question underneath: if I stopped, would they still come? The fear is the evening it doesn't land, when someone looks past you and everything quietly drifts. And this is what your attention gives: people leave feeling they were the one who mattered, and sure enough to offer something of their own. On an ordinary evening, nobody expects to be given that.

**yours**

1. In about 1885, a sea captain's son from near Naples was made head bartender of a small bar facing the casino in Monte Carlo. Ciro Capozzi had actually tended bar in America, and where other bars poured inventions called things like Flip Flaps, he served far simpler drinks, the Manhattan among them.

2. His own bar opened in 1888, just off the casino square, and among its regulars was the Prince of Wales, who would show him cocktails he had learned. He liked American drinks already, so Ciro never had to win him over. He kept coming back, with what he knew. I like to think Ciro treasured that: the most watched man in Monte Carlo, at ease enough to teach the barman.

3. People look for the trick in you, and there isn't one. The plainness is the point: someone paying real attention, and meaning it. And the best of it was never that people are charmed. It's what they bring you once they feel seen. Anyone can be charming for an evening. You remember, you return to it, and so people offer you their best.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Manhattan, the renowned classic Ciro was pouring in Monte Carlo. Mine is rye, sweet vermouth and bitters, stirred until ice-cold, then brightened with lemon peel. To it I've added a small square of fresh pineapple, from a cocktail of that regular's own, as a London paper had it in 1887. I like to think his own was among the ones he showed Ciro. Back then a pineapple was hugely expensive, and his drink showed it off. In this glass it goes quiet: pressed with a touch of syrup, then strained out again, as a nod to what a regular brings to the bar. It still looks like a plain Manhattan. You only find the pineapple up close.

5. Soon, for once, don't be "on". Let someone else carry the conversation, and let them show you something they've learned. Then you'll see whether they'd still come, and I hope the answer surprises you, in the best way.
