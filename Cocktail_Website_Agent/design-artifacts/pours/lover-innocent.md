# Pour — Wide Open · The Pure Heart (lover-innocent)

## Cocktail

- **name:** Wide Open
- **tagline:** You've been hurt. You still love like you haven't.
- **glassware:** coupe (about 180 ml), chilled
- **contains:** `["egg-white"]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 45 ml (1½ oz) | Japanese blended whisky | Nikka From the Barrel recommended, or any Japanese blended whisky of 45% or stronger |
| 22.5 ml (¾ oz) | demerara syrup | one part demerara sugar to one part hot water, stirred until it dissolves, then cooled |
| 1 | whole egg, large | the freshest you can get, clean and uncracked, kept cold; left overnight in its shell beside dried culinary lavender (see Method) |

**method**
1. The night before, line a small lidded box with kitchen paper and scatter a tablespoon of dried culinary lavender over it. Lay the eggs on top, whole, close the lid, and put the box in the fridge. Use the freshest eggs you can get, clean and uncracked, and keep them cold until you use them: the alcohol doesn't make a raw egg safe. If the drink is for anyone pregnant, elderly or unwell, use pasteurised eggs, though the lavender may come through more faintly. The shell lets the scent through to the white.
2. Chill the coupe in the freezer.
3. Crack one egg into the shaker, yolk and white together. Add the whisky and the demerara syrup.
4. Close the shaker and shake hard with no ice for about fifteen seconds, until the egg is light and fully mixed in.
5. Fill it with ice and shake hard again, a little longer than you would for most drinks, until the tin is ice-cold.
6. Strain it into the coupe through the shaker's strainer and a small sieve held over the glass. Leave the top bare: no nutmeg.

**closingLine:** *The egg goes in whole. And when you want to reply, reply now: that's what they love you for.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*A Japanese whisky, a whole egg, and a love story that began in Scotland.*

**whoYouAre**
You say it when you feel it. "I missed you", at the door, instead of "long time no see". You answer a message the minute it arrives, because you were glad to get it, and you can't see why you'd hide that. When you love someone, they know. Usually within the week.

Most people call it innocence. They assume nobody's ever hurt you. Somebody has, more than once, and every time you knew exactly what you were risking. You've heard all the advice: be careful, make them wait, don't show your hand so soon. You decided long ago that being hurt costs less than being careful. The bruises stay private, because showing them only brings the advice back. The thing you're quietly afraid of is the evening you notice you've waited three hours to reply, on purpose. And next to you, nobody has to pretend to care less than they do. They say it back. Love you don't have to earn is the rarest kind, and you give it to everyone you love.

**yours**

1. A young Japanese chemist, Masataka Taketsuru, was lodging with a doctor's widow in Scotland. His firm in Osaka had sent him to learn how whisky is made. In January 1920 he married her daughter, Rita Cowan, and she went to Japan with him.

2. When they got there, the firm that had sent him was in the hands of administrators, and its plans for a whisky distillery were on hold. The work he'd been sent to learn had nowhere to go. We don't know how Rita took that. What she did is plainer: she taught English and piano. He found work making whisky for another firm, and later the two of them moved north together, to Yoichi in Hokkaido, where he started his own company. Today it's called Nikka, one of Japan's biggest whisky makers. They lived in Hokkaido for the rest of their lives.

3. I like to think she knew exactly what she was risking, and said yes anyway. And when the plan fell through, she didn't take any of it back. That's the part I recognise in you: not the yes, which anyone can admire, but the staying open when it didn't go to plan. In 2014, Japanese television made a morning drama of their story, and told it as what it was, a love story.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Flip, a renowned classic: spirit, sugar and a whole egg, white and yolk, the yolk for richness and the white for froth, shaken hard, then served cold. The whisky is Nikka, from Masataka's company, the one that began when they moved north. My one addition happens the night before. The eggs sit in a closed box on a bed of dried lavender, and the shell, whole and uncracked, lets the scent through to the white. I like to think that's you: nothing broken, and still open. It's silky and rich, with just enough demerara sugar, a light amber-beige, with a faint breath of lavender, in a chilled coupe.

5. My advice is the opposite of everyone else's: don't learn the lesson. When it goes wrong again, and sometimes it will, someone will tell you to be more careful. Hear them out. Then love the next person exactly as you would have before, and if you ever catch yourself waiting three hours to reply, on purpose, reply now. Not because it's safe. Because that's how everyone near you learns to say it too. And I hope the next one says it back before you've finished saying it.
