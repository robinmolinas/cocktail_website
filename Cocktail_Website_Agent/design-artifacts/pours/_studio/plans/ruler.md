## ruler-caregiver · The Caring Leader
- **Wren's test:** the story's person has real power and chooses not to use force or volume; faces a moment of heat (an angry crowd, a panicking crew, a fight at the bar) and holds a line, said calmly; it costs them (patience, being taken for weak, a price of their own); nobody in their care is hurt, and the people around them do their best because of the calm. Out: rescues, rationing and water, hunger or health, a calm that's only a quality of the drink.
- **Lead: Max Bilgray (1885–1958) and Bilgray's Tropic Bar, Colón, Panama.** Oxford Companion, BILGRAY'S TROPICO (pdf 267–269), primary (Berry and Wondrich, citing *Time*, 1956, and Ernie Pyle, 1940). On Balboa Street, "Bottle Alley", in a city known for "raunchy, boozy fun", Bilgray kept "an honest saloon that scorned chromium, jukeboxes and B-girls" (the hostesses paid to hustle drinks): brass, dark wood, "softly whirling fans". Pyle found him "an unassuming, soft-spoken man (though … immensely profane)". When customers wanted louder, he opened a separate garden cabaret in 1938 rather than change the bar. The place "never regained" its name after he sold it in 1955. **Passes because:** the authority is quiet by choice, in the loudest street of its day; the line held is a calm one (no hustling the customer, no noise machines), kept for the people in the room. **Partly:** the page gives no single moment of heat; the heat is the street, not an incident. The room may want Wren to rule on that. **Guards:** "man-to-man talk" (*Time*) is gendered: never in guest text. The Aimee Semple McPherson episode and the Hallelujah Cocktail stay out (religion, and a jab, not calm). His pre-Prohibition flight (he saw a colleague jailed) is a date at most (Outlaw's ground). Military patrons (Eisenhower, Halsey) stay out. "Soft-spoken" is Pyle's, quoted with its "profane" half or not at all. Motive for scorning B-girls isn't on the page: "I like to think…".
- **Backup: Ben Reed and Ben Pundole at the Met Bar, London (late 1990s).** *A Proper Drink*, pp. 97–98, reliable secondary; Oxford, REED, BEN (pdf 1629), primary. The tiny, roped-off bar in the Metropolitan Hotel drew Oasis, Blur, Kate Moss and Damien Hirst, "whom Reed banned from the bar several times"; Pundole, "all of twenty-two", "handled celebrities with ease". **Passes partly:** a line held against the most famous, more than once, in the heat of the hottest room in town; calm is Simonson's word for Pundole's manner, not Reed's. Thin: two sentences. **Guards:** the Met Bar shares a chapter with three other Ruler rows (LAB, Collins, Downey): no names cross. Never name the drunk celebrities as drunk. The bar's fruit-and-vodka "Martinis" aren't a reason to make one.
- **Wren:** accept. On Hester's flag: there's no single moment of heat, and I'll let that go, because the page gives something I'd rather have. The heat is a standing one, the loudest street of its day, and he answers it calmly twice. He keeps the jukeboxes and the drink-hustling hostesses out. Then, when some customers find it "too staid" (that's the cost, being taken for dull, on pdf 267), he opens a separate garden for the noise in 1938 instead of changing the bar. That's a line held without raising his voice, which is this guest's one true thing. And "soft-spoken … (though … immensely profane)" stops the saint reading: the calm is work, not temperament. Keep both halves, signposted ("I like to think the swearing was what the calm cost him"). Guards to add: never "charismatic" (Oxford's word for him on pdf 269, and a sibling's name). The reading shows the sound he kept out (the fans, no jukebox), not the brass and wood, because the Reformer's row needs the old saloon furniture. Nothing may claim anyone was kept safe or did their best because of him (not on the page): signpost it or leave it out. Don't promote the Met Bar backup. Its fruit "Martinis" are the drinks Collins scorned and LAB built on (Proper pp. 98–101), so it would be a third row from that chapter. If the room overturns Bilgray, Hester needs a new backup.
- **Drink direction (Tomás):** Old-Fashioned · aged Panamanian rum (a style, no brand) · no named bottle · heavy rocks glass, one large cube. From the story because the room he kept sounds like a stirred drink: no shaker, no rattle of small ice, nothing that hustles, just the fans. That the single cube is quieter is my craft call, not a source. The rum is the place's (Colón), not the page's, so the reading never says he poured it. ## ruler-creator · The Perfectionist
- **Wren's test:** a person whose taste set the rule for others (a menu, a house style, a list of what's allowed) and who refused things to protect it; it costs them warmth or being liked; the standard was care for whoever would drink it, not vanity, and other people's work got better under it. Out: redoing others' work, revising their own classic, taking away, a vision handed on, quality alone.
- **Lead: Wayne Collins, who wouldn't call a fruit drink a Martini.** *A Proper Drink*, pp. 101–103, reliable secondary (Collins on the record); Oxford, COLLINS, WAYNE (pdf 557), primary. In late-1990s London, when the fruit "Martini" ruled, Collins "hated" it and wrote an article in *Class* bashing it: "I didn't like that it was called a Martini." His teacher, Lee Chappell, had drilled into him that a drink is "not about you, is it? It's about the guests." As bar manager at High Holborn (2000) he chose drinks he "found in books that were not on lists around London" (Manhattans, Daisies, Punches) and stirred Sazeracs in fine glassware "in a time when most London drinkers had never been served a cocktail without ice". The bar "influenced a lot of people" (Conigliaro). Oxford credits his Mixxit programme with "driving up service standards" across the UK trade. **Passes because:** his taste became a rule for others (a word that had to mean something; a menu; a training standard), he refused what the room loved, and the stated reason is the guest, on the record. The cost on the page is the public stand itself: an article against the drink everyone was ordering. Anything about it costing him warmth is "I like to think" (Wren). The yes for the position is on p. 102: the drinks "not on lists around London". **Guards (step 4, Wren's fixes folded in):** High Holborn closed within a year because the restaurant failed; Proper pp. 102–103 says the bar did better than the restaurant: never tie the closing to his standard, and don't use it as his cost. Chappell's line is his teacher's, quoted as such. His "I wanted to do something different from LAB. LAB was a party bar. This was for serious drinkers" and his "Muddled up, crushed ice … bang!" (p. 101) stay out of guest text (they name the Charismatic Leader's lead, and "serious drinkers" reads as snobbery). His White Negroni (p. 106) stays out (Hero considered it). No "editor"/"curator"/"classicist" labels for the guest. The Martini goes to the Diva's row (Wren): this row's drink comes from his other stirred classics, Tomás's call, minding the Sazerac's closeness to *Beside the First*. Living person: date him ("as he told Simonson, by 2016").
- **Backup: John Gertsen and Drink, Boston: no menu, no bottles on the backbar.** *A Proper Drink*, p. 188 (and p. 192 for who he is), reliable secondary. Gertsen "recalibrated patrons' habits by taking their go-to choices away": "I don't want bottles behind me … I want it to be about the drink. I want it to be about the experience." **Passes partly:** taste as the house rule, refusal, care for the guest on the record; the cost (the "grumpy and arrogant" reputation) is said of the era's bars in the same chapter, not of Drink by name, so don't attach it to him. **Guards:** the Somerville house and the Jack Rose Society on p. 192 are Lover's backup: never in this row. His "I took that drink to the bank" quote is in Magician's Red Hook backup: stays out. Not "the Scolds" framing (the chapter's title) for the guest.
- **Wren:** accept, with one claim fixed. The cost isn't on the page as Hester wrote it. High Holborn closed within a year because the restaurant failed, and Proper pp. 102–103 says the bar did better than the restaurant, so never tie the closing to his standard. The cost that is on the page is the public stand itself: an article bashing the drink everyone loved. Anything about it costing him warmth is "I like to think". Keep out of guest text his "I wanted to do something different from LAB. LAB was a party bar. This was for serious drinkers" (it names the Charismatic Leader's lead, and "serious drinkers" reads as snobbery to the guest), and his "Muddled up, crushed ice … bang!" about LAB (p. 101). The yes for the position is on p. 102: drinks he found in old books that were "not on lists around London". Tomás, a flag: the Martini runs through three Ruler rows (Collins's schooling, Chalker's "two best drinks", Ciro's Manhattan and Martini). Only one may pour it, and Chalker has the strongest claim. This row's drink comes from Collins's other stirred classics (p. 102 names Manhattans, Daisies and Punches, and his Sazeracs), your call, minding the Sazerac's closeness to *Beside the First*.

## ruler-regular-guy · The People's Representative
- **Wren's test:** someone with standing crosses to the other side's symbol, drink or ground to hold a divided group together; faces distrust from the other side and suspicion from their own; it costs them their own side's easy approval; both sides end up in the same room, drinking the same thing, and the person loses nothing of who they are. Out: charity, a limp compromise, war.
- **Lead (step 4, after Wren's review: replaces Simon Ford and Victor Morris, both free again): the Piña Colada, claimed by two San Juan bars.** Oxford, PIÑA COLADA (pdf 1516–1517, Wondrich), primary. Two bars in one city each say it's theirs: the Caribe Hilton, for its bartender Ramón "Monchito" Marrero Pérez in 1954, and the Barrachina restaurant, for Ramón Porta Mingot in 1963. Oxford weighs both and leaves it open: Barrachina appears in the press as "a new restaurant in 1965", and none of the Caribe Hilton's recorded 1950s recipes put Coco López and pineapple together, which "does not invalidate Perez's claim" but questions its timing. Whoever made it, the drink "hit big in 1968", and in 1978 Puerto Rico declared it the island's national drink: both bars' claim, one glass everyone drinks. **Passes partly:** Wren's own shape, a drink two bars claim, with both sides in the end drinking the same thing. **Fails** her crossing: nobody on the page goes over to the other bar, and no distrust between the two is shown, so the reading would have to make the guest the one who crosses ("I like to think…"). Wren rules, or the room hunts. **Guards:** Innocent holds Porta Mingot as a backup and guards that his Barrachina claim is "never" told as fact: here it's one of two claims, Oxford's doubts included, and nothing else about him (his football-team cocktails stay Innocent's). Puerto Rico's postwar development programme (behind Coco López and the Caribe Hilton) is politics: out. The 1979 song and "health-conscious" line stay out. Not tiki (Oxford says it isn't).
- **Backup: the Vieux Carré, the French Quarter's drink with the American whiskey in it (New Orleans, 1930s).** Oxford, VIEUX CARRÉ (pdf 2102), primary; *Imbibe!*, pdf 182, primary. The camps: before the Civil War "New Orleans was a Brandy Julep town", the Creole city of cognac, and after it the city "became much more Americanized" and whiskey "displaced" brandy (a bartender in 1885: "brandy drinking in New Orleans… a thing of the past"). The drink: at the Monteleone, head bartender Walter Bergeron put straight rye and cognac in equal parts in one glass, with Bénédictine and two bitters, and named it for the old square, the French Quarter; first printed in 1937. **Passes partly:** the two sides' spirits end up in one glass under the older side's name. **Fails:** the pairing of the two spirits as a peace between camps is our reading, not on either page, and Bergeron's motive isn't recorded: signposted only. **Guards:** Bergeron "had worked at the bar before Prohibition and returned upon Repeal": the date only, and only if Duffy (Legend) isn't the one telling Repeal. The Sazerac, on the Imbibe page, stays out (*Beside the First* and two other pours). Peychaud's with rye leans toward the Sazerac: Tomás's flag. Hero's Warrior holds the Crusta and Santini (the other Creole–American crossing in *Imbibe!*, pdf 233), not used here.

## ruler-explorer · The Reformer

## ruler-sage · The Judge
- **Wren's test:** someone who settled a dispute or protected ordinary drinkers by checking what was real (a rule against adulterated spirits, a disputed origin settled on the evidence, an honest measure); what must hold: both sides heard, a decision made and stood by, and the rule protecting the ordinary drinker rather than the powerful; cost: being unpopular with someone. - **Lead: the 1908 commission that settled what "whisky" may mean (Britain).** Oxford, WHISKY, GRAIN (pdf 2149, Wren's anchor, read this round) and ADULTERATION (pdf 68–70), primary. Grain whisky came out of continuous stills in the 1830s, "lightly flavored but pure whisky that could be sold very cheaply to urban industrial workers"; the pot-still malt makers said it wasn't whisky at all. The 1908 Royal Commission on Whiskey and Other Potable Spirits "settled (legally, anyway) the heated debate between the pot-still malt whisky makers and the continuous-still operators about whether this new distillate was whisky at all. Legally, it was—as long as you called it 'grain whisky,' and not just plain 'whisky.'" ADULTERATION sums up the rule: definitions and labelling "without outlawing blending or preventing column-still spirit from being labeled as whisky". Witnesses from the trade testified in person (Booth's gin, pdf 317; a Demerara rum distiller, pdf 1697). **Passes because:** both sides heard; a decision made against the older, grander side's wish; and the name had to tell the truth, which protects whoever buys the cheaper bottle.
