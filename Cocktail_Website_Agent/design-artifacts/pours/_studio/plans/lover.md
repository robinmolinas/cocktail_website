## lover-caregiver · The Geisha
- **Wren's test:** a real person whose gift was anticipating what others would enjoy and making it look easy while the work stayed hidden. It must show the preparation nobody saw, a moment where the care had nowhere to land, and that they kept setting the table. Out: health, "best host" quality-only stories, servility, secret recipes, Japan as costume, anything built on how someone takes a drink.
- **Lead: Orsamus Willard of the City Hotel (1792–1876).** Oxford Companion, WILLARD, ORSAMUS (pdf 2192–2194), primary; context in CITY HOTEL. The most famous bartender in America in his day. Guests checked in at his bar, and he was desk clerk and bartender in one. Oxford credits him with a photographic memory and says he would do anything possible to accommodate a guest. On the page: a guest sailed to England, stayed ten years and came back, and Willard greeted him by name, recalled his old room number and remarked how quickly ten years roll away. He almost never left the hotel. He retired rich in 1836 and bought a farm, and the farmhouse had number plates on every room door. In 1843 the hotel had failed, and he came out of retirement to restore it, staying five more years. After 1848 "little is heard from him", and the city's sporting crowd assumed he was dead. He is buried near his old farm, beside his wife and descendants (Oxford). **Passes because:** the memory is the unseen preparation (ten years of it, for one guest). The failed hotel is the table with nobody at it, and his return is him setting it again. The numbered doors on his own farmhouse show he never stopped expecting guests. **Guards:** the Virginia gentleman teaching him the julep is "probably impossible to corroborate", so it's a legend, and this row keeps the julep out altogether (it's McMillian's, below). His ice-drink pioneering stays out (*Half a Rim*, and Tudor's row). His exact words on the ten years are Oxford's paraphrase of an old account, so no quotation marks. Remembering a room number isn't remembering how someone takes a drink, but the room must keep it that way. "The first celebrity bartender" is Oxford's sentence, so cite it as Oxford's or leave it out. Don't turn "assumed he was no more" into loneliness: the grave beside his family is on the page.
- **Backup: Tom Bullock (1872–1964) and *The Ideal Bartender* (1917).** Oxford Companion, BULLOCK, TOM (pdf 372–373), primary. An African American bartender at the Pendennis and Kenton clubs in Louisville and then the St. Louis Country Club. He wrote the first known cocktail book by a Black author, just before Prohibition took away the trade he had documented. As the law took force he seems to have found work as a waiter on the Pennsylvania Railroad. Oxford says the book may have helped preserve drinks that would otherwise have been lost. **Passes because:** the table was taken away by law, and he kept serving, on trains. The book is care set out for guests he would never meet. **Guards:** "seems to have found work" and "it is believed" are Oxford's hedges, so keep them. Jim Crow is the context Oxford gives. Say it plainly and never make it a lesson. The foreword by George Herbert Walker is a validation story, not ours. The preparation-nobody-saw limb is weaker here than in the lead.
- **Wren:** **accept.** I read it on the page (WILLARD, pdf 2192–2194): the guest welcomed by name after ten years, "would do anything possible to accommodate a guest's desires", almost never left the hotel, number plates on every room door of his own farmhouse, and the 1843 return to the failed hotel. All four limbs pass. Conditions for the room: the unseen preparation rests on what he *did* (never left the hotel, kept every guest in mind), never on "photographic memory", which is a gift, not effort, and turns the reading into praise. His return is setting the table again, never a rescue (that's the Knight's ground). The line I'll fight for is the numbered doors: a farmhouse made ready for guests is care waiting for someone to receive it. Tell it as "I like to think", because the page gives no motive. His entry names Cato Alexander as his rival, and Cato is now the Pure Heart's story, so neither reading names the other. Backup (Bullock) stands, weak on the preparation limb as Hester says.

## lover-creator · The Muse
- **Wren's test:** a real person told as the inspiration for a drink, a book or a bar who was also, on the record, a maker in their own right, and whose own part was overlooked. It must show both halves: the inspiration and the making. Grant the legend before correcting it. The cost is that the name that went on it wasn't theirs, and it must hold that the work survives and can be traced to them today. Out: known only for looks; contested attributions; "the woman behind the man".
- **Lead: Gerald Murphy (1888–1964), the "juice of a few flowers", and the painting *Cocktail* (1927).** Web, secondary, fetched: Kathryn Price, "The 'Juice of a Few Flowers': Gerald and Sara Murphy's Life of Beautiful Things", *Gastronomica* 7:2 (2007). On that page: Murphy called his drinks "just the juice of a few flowers". His recipe was gin (Booth's House of Lords), grapefruit juice, fresh lime, fresh mint and ice. Philip Barry said that mixing drinks he looked like a priest saying Mass. Fitzgerald modelled Dick and Nicole Diver in *Tender Is the Night* on the Murphys (and on himself and Zelda). Murphy exhibited at the Salon des Indépendants from 1923 and painted only a few years. Seven of his fourteen paintings survive, among them *Cocktail* (1927: a shaker, a glass with a cherry, a cut lemon, a corkscrew and a cigar box) at the Whitney Museum of American Art. **Passes because:** the world knows him as the model for someone else's hero. The name on the book is Fitzgerald's. But he made things too: paintings, one of them of the very ritual people remember him for. It survives in a museum under his own name. Legend first ("the man in the novel"), then the correction ("the man who painted"). **Guards:** only facts from the fetched *Gastronomica* page count. Seen only in search results and **not verified**: the dedication "To Sara and Gerald, Many Fêtes"; painting years 1921–1929 and "never painted again"; Barry reusing the "few flowers" line in *Holiday*. Verify them before any anchor, or leave them out. The Whitney location is from *Gastronomica*, and the Whitney's own page (whitney.org/collection/works/10120) was seen in search but not fetched. The Murphys' sons' deaths are out. So is speculation about Gerald's private life. The Diver model is shared with the Fitzgeralds, so say "partly", as the source does. "Living well is the best revenge" is Tomkins's 1962 title, not verified here.
- **Backup: Margie Samuels (d. 1985) and Maker's Mark.** Web, secondary, fetched: WhiskyCast, "Margie Samuels To Join Kentucky Bourbon Hall Of Fame" (11 September 2014). She named the whisky, created the red wax, designed the bottle, labels and typeface, and in 1967 opened the bourbon industry's first visitors' centre with her son Leslie. She had a chemistry degree (Louisville, 1933) and grew up around her father's distillery. Her son said she "never really considered herself part of the business". In 2014 she became the first woman inducted into the Hall of Fame for her work with a distillery ("long overdue", said the KDA president). Oxford, MAKER'S MARK (pdf 1214), primary, credits the founding to T. William Samuels and never mentions her. That silence is the story. **Passes because:** she made everything you see on the bottle, and the history books gave the name to her husband. The wax is on every bottle today. **Guards:** the "pewter makers' marks" origin of the name wasn't on the fetched page, so it isn't verified. The inspiration half is thin, because she's overlooked as a maker rather than told as a muse. The gender trap is live. The reading is about credit, never about her being a wife. Oxford calls the burned-family-recipe story "probably apocryphal", so keep it out.
- **Wren:** **accept.** Passes every limb: known as the model (partly, as the source says) for someone else's hero, a maker in his own right, the name on the book wasn't his, and *Cocktail* survives under his own name. The legend-then-correction is built in. A man as the muse also defuses "the woman behind the man". Conditions: fetched *Gastronomica* facts only; the reading stays on Murphy and never becomes a Fitzgerald story; the drink comes out of the painted ritual, so the making and the drink are one thing. Backup (Samuels) stands; the gender trap is live there.
- **Drink direction (Tomás):** Daiquiri (a gin sour) · London dry gin · Booth's House of Lords is the gin in Murphy's recipe (*Gastronomica*), but I don't know that it is still sold under that name (my knowledge, unsourced), so the room names it only if Hester finds a current bottling on a page, and otherwise writes the style · a nick-and-nora, chilled. Murphy's own "juice of a few flowers": gin, grapefruit juice, fresh lime and fresh mint, shaken with ice. From the story because it is the drink in the ritual he painted, so the making and the drink are one thing, as Wren asks, and no bridge is needed. Guards: the page gives no sweetener, and grapefruit and lime alone will read OUT on sugar, so the room adds one and says so in Checks (not honey: that's the Gold Rush's). **Grapefruit is this row's** (Hester's note): the Jasmine keeps its "grapefruit juice" tease, with no grapefruit in its glass. Mint is in this glass and in the Romantic's julep: here it's shaken and strained out, a taste, never a julep. The glass isn't a coupe or a cocktail glass because a gin sour already exists in both (*Not Only the Way*, *As It Was*); if the painting's glass turns out to matter, the room owns the repeat in one clause. The cherry in the painting stays in the painting: it isn't in his recipe. Veto-free.

## lover-regular-guy · The Rough Diamond

## lover-explorer · The Pleasure Seeker
- **Wren's test:** a real person who chased pleasure with courage and generosity, whose story has an *after*: the quiet once the feast was over. The pleasures were shared, not hoarded. The after was faced, and what lasted was company or memory, not regret or punishment. Out: ruin, debt or disease as the ending, conquest, "died alone" pity, praising "one more". Paper is allowed only as writing to remember after the fact (the Romantic's paper is letters written now).
- **Lead: Crosby Gaige (1882–1949).** Oxford Companion, GAIGE, CROSBY (pdf 861), primary. He was the son of a small-town postmaster (Skunk Hollow, New York) and made millions as a Broadway producer: about 150 plays over twenty-five years. His real passions were fine wine and cooking. After the 1929 crash he turned them into his living: a monthly "Cellar and Pantry" column, a daily NBC radio programme (*Kitchen Cavalcade*, 1937), cookbooks, and two cocktail books. His 1941 guide is witty and full of friends' contributions. It includes his own drinks "Let's Slide Downstairs" and "Madame, Can You Walk?", and it helped spread the Bloody Mary. **Passes because:** the crash is the music stopping, and he met the after by giving his pleasures away on the air and on the page, to people he'd never meet. What lasted was the company in the book. **Guards:** the crash is the turn, not a ruin story. Oxford doesn't say he lost everything, only that he looked to these interests for a living, so don't inflate it. Don't name Beebe (he wrote Gaige's foreword, and Beebe is this row's backup). Oxford's source title "Tells How to Live a Life" is a 1940s newspaper piece I haven't read. The drink names are cheeky, not a licence to praise getting drunk.
- **Backup: Lucius Beebe (1902–1966) and Charles Clegg in Virginia City.** Oxford Companion, BEEBE, LUCIUS (pdf 243), primary: gourmand and bon vivant, sent down from Harvard and Yale (he kept a roulette wheel and a bar in his room), coined "café society" in the *Herald Tribune*, and wrote the *Stork Club Bar Book* (1946). The after, from the web, secondary: Wikipedia, "Charles Clegg" (fetched; cites *Time*, 22 April 1966). They met in 1940 and were partners until Beebe's death, 26 years. They moved to Virginia City, Nevada, in about 1950 and revived the *Territorial Enterprise*. A search summary says they bought it in 1952 and it became the biggest weekly west of the Mississippi, but I couldn't load the Online Nevada Encyclopedia page to check, so that's **unverified**. **Passes because:** café society's loudest diner ended in a quiet mining town with the one person who stayed, printing a paper together. **Guards:** Clegg's suicide in 1979 is never mentioned. Neither is the "lavender scare" framing Wikipedia gives the move. Be careful with Wikipedia (it's secondary, cited only in part). Don't name Gaige. Beebe's excess is the "before", never praised.
- **Wren:** **accept.** The crash is the music stopping, and he met the after by giving his pleasures away on the air and on the page, with friends' drinks in the book as the company that lasted. Conditions: don't inflate the crash (Hester's guard); the after is a new table, never a rescue from ruin. Lean on the radio more than the page, and never use "he wrote it all down" (Casanova's frame on the sheet). "Madame, Can You Walk?" stays out of the reading: it's a joke about being too drunk to walk. **For Tomás:** Gaige helped spread the Bloody Mary, but tomato is *The Other Berry*'s (the Surrealist), so don't go there without owning it. Backup (Beebe and Clegg) stands with its guards.

## lover-sage · The Romantic
- **Wren's test:** a real exchange of minds (a correspondence, a friendship or a dedication kept up across distance or years) where beauty or understanding was *shared*, not just admired. It must show someone answered at the same depth. It must cost the risk of being too much, or of silence, and it must hold that the exchange lasted or survives. Out: unrequited obsession (the Obsessed's), tasting and quality judgement (the Connoisseur's), death as the romance, Byron, brothers (*A Brother's Care*), "put it into words" (the Connoisseur's).
- **Lead (thin on "exchange"): Chris McMillian and the "Ode to the Mint Julep".** Oxford Companion, McMILLIAN, CHRIS (pdf 1273), primary; context in ICE MALLET (pdf 1066). He's a fourth-generation New Orleans bartender who researched antebellum bars and early drinks and shared what he found freely with bartenders and guests, before cocktail history was a popular pursuit. At the Ritz-Carlton's Library Bar he revived the Mint Julep, pounding the ice in a canvas bag with a big mallet, in rhythm, while reciting Soule Smith's nineteenth-century "Ode to the Mint Julep". He says that making drinks is 10 percent of what he does. **Passes because:** a man answers a long-dead writer's ode by learning it by heart and giving it, aloud, to strangers across a bar. He takes the risk of being too much every time. The exchange survives because he keeps saying it. **Guards and why it's thin:** the "someone answered him at the same depth" limb is Smith-to-McMillian across a century, not two living minds, so Wren may judge it short of her test. The text of the Ode is not in the library. Soule Smith's dates and the Ode's date aren't verified ("late nineteenth-century" is Oxford's). Quote none of it until it's found. This row reserves the family's Mint Julep. McMillian is alive, so date any present tense.
- **Backup (thin): Aeneas MacDonald's *Whisky* (1930).** Oxford Companion, MacDONALD, AENEAS (pdf 1205), primary. It was the pseudonym of George Malcolm Thomson, a publisher at the heart of the Scottish literary renaissance. Oxford calls the book by turns hard-eyed and lyrical, one of the earliest paeans to single malt, and one of the finest books on the subject. It has been reprinted several times, and in 2016 Ian Buxton edited an annotated edition (Birlinn). **Passes because:** it's a long, lyrical love letter to a drink, sent under another name, answered at length 86 years later by a reader who annotated it. **Guards:** why he used a pseudonym isn't on the page, so don't invent a motive. The "exchange of minds" limb is thin in the same way as the lead. I have not read Buxton's edition. I looked for a true two-way correspondence in the library and found none I could use: the ones I found belonged to Explorer (Regan's book and Grimes's review) or to the registry (Twain and Livy), or were brothers (Keats, which collides with *A Brother's Care*). **If Wren rejects both, the room needs a web hunt for a real correspondence with a drink in it,** and I'll say so in round 2.
