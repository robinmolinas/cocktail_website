## regular-guy-caregiver · The Good Samaritan
- **Wren's test:** the story's person helps a **stranger** with no claim on them (not their job, not their customer). Others were there or passed by, or the stranger was plainly alone. It **costs** something small and real, and they seek no credit. It **held**: the help helped, and ideally someone followed or the habit spread. **Out:** emergencies and rescues, warming arriving travellers (Foynes), official hospitality (Kir), philanthropists and galas, anything medical.
- **Lead: the *caffè sospeso*, the suspended coffee of Naples.** Sylvia Poggioli, "EU Embraces 'Suspended Coffee': Pay It Forward With A Cup Of Joe", NPR, 24 April 2013 (fetched 2026-10-01), secondary (established press). On the page: a tradition that began "some one-hundred years ago" in Naples, where a customer pays in advance for a coffee for someone who can't afford one. The barista kept a log, and anyone who put their head round the door and asked "Is there anything suspended?" was served free. Poggioli: "donors and recipients never meet each other, the donor doesn't show off and the recipient doesn't have to show gratitude." Luciano De Crescenzo, on the record: someone who'd had "a break of good luck" paid for two cups, not one, "allowing someone less fortunate who entered later" to have one free; "a cup of coffee … offered to the rest of humankind." The custom faded in the postwar boom and survived for decades mainly at Christmas. It came back during the eurozone crisis (Naples declared 10 December "Suspended Coffee Day"), spread to more than 150 cafés in Bulgaria, and a young man in Barcelona, Gonzalo Sapina, started a Spanish network in a few weeks. **Passes because** the whole of it is Wren's test: a stranger with no claim, a small cost, no credit on purpose, and a habit that others picked up, down to one named person who followed. **Guards:** the origin is only "about a century ago" (no year; the web's origin legends stay out) · it's a custom of many ordinary people, not one hero, so the reading never invents a first giver · never the Bulgarian self-immolations on the same page · "charity" is Poggioli's word; ours is plainer · the drink must stay clear of the registry's Irish Coffee (*Everybody's*), Jester's Café Brûlot and Magician's Spanish Coffee · one gap for Wren: the Samaritan crosses the road and is seen, while the sospeso giver is never seen. The row rests on "no credit sought", not on the crossing.
- **Second source (round 2, Wren's condition 1):** *Euronews* (Italian edition), "Dal caffè al tampone sospeso, la solidarietà di Napoli", 19 November 2020 (fetched 2026-10-01), secondary (established press), independent of NPR: whoever has a coffee at the bar "prima di andare via ne pagherà un altro, il sospeso, per qualcuno che non se lo può permettere", and the custom is still alive in the city's working neighbourhoods. *ANSA*, 23 September 2023 (fetched), confirms the practice in passing: paying for one or more coffees "a favore di persone indigenti". **What this corroborates:** the core (a cup paid ahead, by a stranger, for someone who can't afford it, still done today). **What it doesn't:** the "Is there anything suspended?" ask and the fading and return rest on NPR alone, so they stay out of the reading or are said as "one reporter wrote". **Conflict:** *Euronews* dates the custom to the end of the Second World War; NPR says it began about a century before 2013 and *faded* in the postwar boom. No date or era goes in the reading.
- **Backup: Sue Fedroff's fifteen minutes.** *A Proper Drink* pp. 151–152 (Simonson), secondary (interview; the words are Phil Ward's). Fedroff co-owned Flatiron Lounge with Julie Reiner. Ward, a rough-edged applicant nobody would have picked, hoped for the interviewer on her side: "I hope I get that lady. She's so nice." Reiner "admits she probably wouldn't have hired Ward." Ward: "Sue would talk to a homeless man for fifteen minutes about what his interests were outside panhandling. Jules would look at him for one second and [brushes hands]: done with that." The book calls Ward "Reiner's most important hire", and it "almost didn't happen". **Passes because** it has the crossing the lead lacks: a stranger on the street with no claim on her, someone beside her who'd have moved on, a small cost (fifteen minutes) given with no credit sought, and the same habit let a stranger in the door who mattered. **Half-fails:** the homeless man's own outcome isn't on the page; it's a habit described by a third person, not one scene. **Owned overlap:** Flatiron is Explorer's backup ground (Reiner) and Phil Ward is Hero's lead person, on this same page. This row tells Fedroff's habit and names Ward in one clause at most; if Hero's Ward row runs, Wren decides whether both can. I had listed Flatiron as ground this family leaves alone; this is the one owned exception. **Guards:** the staff nicknames ("Creator of False Hopes", "Destroyer of Dreams") are teasing; never make Reiner the villain · Ward's "go home and shoot myself" on the same page, never · carry the channel: it's Ward's account.
- **Wren:** **accept, with conditions; strike the backup.** The sospeso carries the half of my test that matters most for this guest: a stranger with no claim, a small cost, no credit on purpose, and a habit that spread (Sapina is the one named person who followed). It misses the crossing: the giver is never seen and nobody walks past. So my line "in front of everyone" is retired for this row, and the one true thing reads as noticing the one who's left out, even one who hasn't come in yet (De Crescenzo's "someone less fortunate who entered later"). Three conditions. (1) One NPR piece is too thin to anchor a whole reading: the room adds a second, independent source for the custom's core (the cup paid ahead, the "anything suspended?" ask, the fading and the return) before any line rests on it. If nothing corroborates, the row goes back to the room. (2) The gift is never "the recipient doesn't have to show gratitude": making help easy to take is *Till Spring*'s (the Healer). This row's gift is the stranger and the ripple. (3) The drink must not read as a warm cup handed to arrivals (*Everybody's*). **Boldt is out as a backup, not just weak:** it's a legend with no primary behind it, the couple were his own hotel's would-be guests, and its moral is kindness repaid by a millionaire, which flatters this guest instead of recognising them. Hester: a new backup, same test.
- **Drink direction (Tomás):** Old-Fashioned (shaken hard and served up, the way an Italian bar shakes an iced espresso) · brandy (style) with a fresh espresso · no named bottle · cocktail glass, chilled. From the story because the coffee *is* the story: the cup paid ahead becomes the glass's core flavour, and nothing bridges to Naples (no Strega, no limoncello, no Neapolitan liqueur). Cold and shaken so it can never read as a warm cup handed to an arrival (Wren's condition 3, *Everybody's*). No cream, no flame, no coffee liqueur and no vodka, so it's none of Irish Coffee, Café Brûlot, Spanish Coffee, Jester's Chocolate Martini or the Espresso Martini (Bradsell, Explorer's person). Coffee with cognac is a pairing the *Codex* prints in an Old-Fashioned riff. The two-cup gesture stays out of the glass: pouring a second glass for someone is *In Your Own Hand*'s, and keeping a cup back is *Four Shares*'. "Espresso" is my reading of a Naples bar's coffee; the sources say coffee.

## regular-guy-creator · The Hidden Talent
- **Wren's test:** the story's person was **ordinary in their world's eyes** (a job, a place or a background nobody linked to this gift) and had a **real, documented** gift. It was **unseen, doubted or kept private** at first, and there's a sourced moment it's **seen**. Best of all, their own people see it, or they bring it back home. It **costs** the risk of seeming to get above themselves, or years in private. **Out:** rags-to-riches fame as the point, genius myths, a gift for drinking, and Coleman, Breaux, Ensslin and Bermejo.
- **Lead: Tom Nichol, from the grain plant to Tanqueray.** Oxford, NICHOL, TOM (pdf 1388, Jack Robertiello), primary. On the page: born in 1955 in Tullibody, Scotland, near the Cambus Distillery where his father worked. He joined Cambus in 1973 and was a supervisor by 1979. When Cambus closed in 1993 he moved to Cameronbridge in Fife, first producing liquid carbon dioxide, then grain whisky and neutral grain spirit from 1996. In 2005 he joined the white-spirits team, and within a year he was Tanqueray's master distiller (to 2015). He's credited with "developing, recreating, or maintaining" the Tanqueray range: the Rangpur (2007), the Malacca revival (2013), an Old Tom (2014) and the Bloomsbury (2015). He was "a passionate, even irascible advocate for the idea that large-scale distilling does not automatically mean compromised quality." **Passes because** the gift came out of the least glamorous corner of the trade (a grain plant, carbon dioxide) after thirty-odd years, and the moment it's seen is on the page. He never left his people: he stayed in the big distilleries his father worked in and argued for their kind of work. That's Wren's "bring it back home". **Half-fails:** the page never says anyone doubted him; "unseen" is my reading of thirty years without a title. **Guards:** "irascible" is Oxford's word, so never soften him into "quiet" · No. Ten dates from 2000, before his time: never credit him with creating it ("maintaining" covers it) · "credited with" is Oxford's hedge, so keep it · his Old Tom sits near *Instead* (Ransom Old Tom): owned in one clause if the glass uses Old Tom · never "genius".
- **Backup: Erik Lorincz and Marian Beke, cleaners who became London's best.** *A Proper Drink* pp. 217–218, 220 (Simonson), secondary (from interviews). Lorincz came to London once visas weren't needed, worked at the Attica Club, then the Purple Bar at the Sanderson, where Simon Difford "was dumbfounded". He was nominated for a Spirited Award in 2008, opened the new Connaught bar (2008) with Agostino Perrone ("For six months when we opened, no one even knew there was such a bar") and later moved to the Savoy's American Bar. Beke was Nightjar's linchpin when it opened. P. 220, Philip Duff: the two "had been feted bartenders in eastern Europe, but when they came to London, they took jobs as cleaners in nightclubs and worked their way up." **Passes because** the gift was invisible in the new place, then seen at the top. **Half-fails:** their own people saw it first and strangers didn't, which reverses Wren's "front row". **Guards:** the book calls Lorincz "a Czech"; I believe he's Slovak, so nationality stays out · Duff is Hero's backup person: carry his line as "a colleague said" · Lorincz's master class was with Vadrna (Innocent's lead): never named · never the page's "the guys from Nepal" line.
- **Wren:** **accept.** Thirty years in the least glamorous corner of the trade, then the gin team, then Tanqueray within a year: the gift didn't match where people had placed him, and the moment it's seen is on the page. What makes it this guest's story, and not a promotion story, is the last clause: he kept arguing, loudly, that big distilleries (his father's kind of place) make good spirit. That's bringing your people with you. The half-fail stands, and the reading owns it by never claiming anyone doubted him: "nobody connected him with it" is as far as it goes. Keep the edge ("irascible" is why he's believable; never soften him into a quiet genius), keep "credited with", and No. Ten is never his.
- **Drink direction (Tomás):** Old-Fashioned · London dry gin · Tanqueray London Dry, recommended (substitute: any juniper-led London dry gin) · rocks glass, one large cube. From the story because his gift is the gin itself: an Old-Fashioned is the spirit with only sugar and bitters, so the everyday big-distillery bottle has nowhere to hide, which is his own argument (large-scale doesn't mean compromised). Never No. Ten (it predates him) and never Tanqueray's Old Tom (*Instead*'s ground, and the Prankster's gin in this family). Rangpur, the one he developed (2007), can come in as the spark if the room finds its sugar on a label. The bottle's strength differs by market, so the room proves both.

## regular-guy-explorer · The Entrepreneur
- **Wren's test:** the story's person **started something from nothing**: little money, no backing, no permission, leaving a safer path. The source shows **when, where and with what**, and that it was a risk. It was made for **ordinary people**, not the rich. It **held**, and best of all they kept their people close. **Out:** tech and billionaire founders, garage legends, luxury brands, buyouts as the ending, and Bermejo, Saunders, Ramos and Ransom.
- **Lead: Tomas Estes and Café Pacifico.** *A Proper Drink* pp. 79–80 (Simonson), secondary (interviews), and Oxford, ESTES, TOMAS (pdf 743, Chantal Martineau) and its contributors' notes (pdf 2260), primary. On the page: Estes was a teacher (a high-school teacher in Los Angeles, says *Proper*; he "left a career as an English teacher in his native California in 1976", says Oxford). Raised in East Los Angeles's largely Mexican neighbourhood, he was drawn to Mexican culture early. After a 1970 trip to Amsterdam he moved there and "filled a perceived hole in the Amsterdam food market" with a Mexican restaurant, Café Pacifico (1976). "My main goal was to support myself," he said. A second Pacifico opened in London in 1982. Danny Smith tended its bar for ten years, and London's young bartenders, "having few places to go for a proper cocktail, found Pacifico." Phil Bayly met him by chance just after the opening, and the two went on to open fifteen more tequila bars (Oxford BAYLY pdf 239); Oxford has Estes opening sixteen more places after the first over four decades. In 2003 Mexico's tequila chamber named him the spirit's ambassador to the EU. **Passes because** he left a safe job for a foreign city, started with a place for ordinary diners, and it lasted forty years with the same people around him. What he built became somewhere others belonged: *Proper* calls the London Pacifico one of the revival's "unofficial and inspirational meeting houses". **Half-fails:** no sum of money or named doubter is on the page, so "risk" is the move itself. **Guards:** the two sources differ on the teaching job (say "a teacher in California") · the *Proper* chapter pairs him with Bermejo (*Half a Rim*, registry): never name Bermejo or Tommy's · Ocho and single-rancho tequila stay out (Magician set Estes aside for exactly that shape) · Bradsell and Conigliaro (Explorer's) are quoted on p. 80: never named · he died in 2021, so past tense.
- **Backup: John Hansell's newsletter.** Oxford, HANSELL, JOHN (pdf 968, Clay Risen), primary. On the page: while working as an environmental chemist for a Pennsylvania power company in the early 1990s, he started a beer-sharing club with a newsletter, *On Tap*. As his interest turned to whisky he renamed it *Malt Advocate* (1994), switched almost entirely to whisky the next year and made it a full magazine, adding staff and leading writers. In 1998 he launched WhiskyFest in New York, then in Chicago, San Francisco and Washington. **Passes because** it began as a club for people like him and grew into a place thousands came to, with him still at its head. **Half-fails:** he didn't leave a safe path on the page. **Guards:** the 2010 sale to M. Shanken is never the ending (buyouts out); tell only that he stayed at the helm · Jim Murray, named as a contributor, is never named (Oxford MURRAY, JIM pdf 1373 records the 2020 objections to his reviews).
- **Wren:** **accept.** It passes where it counts for this guest: he left a safe job for a city that wasn't his, started a place for ordinary diners "to support myself" (the plainest motive on record, and the reading should lean on it), and what he built became where young bartenders gathered, with the same people beside him for decades. That's "a place for people like them". The risk is the move itself: never invent money or doubters. For Tomás: the registry already has two tequila Margaritas (*Half a Rim*, *For Good*), so this can't be a third.
- **Drink direction (Tomás):** Sidecar (a shaken Margarita) · tequila blanco · Sauza blanco and Giffard triple sec, recommended pending a label check, because Pacifico's Margaritas "were made with Sauza Blanco and Giffard triple sec, to Estes' specifications" (*Proper* p. 79; substitutes: a blanco tequila, a French triple sec) · cocktail glass, chilled, no salt. From the story because it's the drink on his page: "much of the tequila went into Margaritas." Any other tequila drink would be a bridge. **Against Wren's "not a third Margarita" (I hold this, the room rules):** it's neither *Half a Rim* (Tommy's: agave syrup, no orange liqueur, rocks glass) nor *For Good* (frozen, goblet), and Robin's rule bars only the same cocktail. If the room still won't have it, the backup (Hansell) beats a bridged tequila highball. Guards: Sauza's blanco today may not be 100% agave, so the room checks the label before naming it and the reading never praises the bottle; the squeezed-limes line on p. 80 is Bradsell's and Conigliaro's (Explorer's), never ours; no half rim.

## regular-guy-hero · The Have A Go Hero
- **Wren's test:** the story's person had **no title, training or authority** for what they took on. They took on a problem **bigger than themselves**, practically rather than by argument, and were **doubted or laughed at**. They **struggled or failed at least once and kept going**. The source shows the **result**, something changed for many. **Out:** pamphlets, speeches and fraud campaigns, rescues, official experts, temperance or health crusades.
- **Lead: Ralph Erenzo and Tuthilltown.** Oxford, HUDSON (pdf 1037, Max Watman), primary. On the page: in 2001 Erenzo bought the old Tuthilltown gristmill near Gardiner, New York (in use for over two hundred years), hoping to run a campground for climbers on the Shawangunk Ridge. His neighbours objected. His research turned up new legislation allowing small distilleries, and Tuthilltown (founded 2003 with Brian Lee) became the first distillery in New York to get the new class of licence, and the first whisky distillery in the state since Prohibition ("there were once well over a thousand"). The first product was a vodka from New York apples. On the page: blackthorn berries steeped in sweetened gin, traditionally served at Christmas and New Year.
