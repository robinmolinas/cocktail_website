## magician-caregiver · The Fairy Godparent
- **Wren's test:** (1) someone quietly changed another person's big moment, unasked and uncredited at the time; (2) the moment is real and theirs, and the helper is absent from it; (3) a decision made without permission, which could have been overstepping; (4) the help did no harm; (5) the cost: they never saw it land, or learned late. Out: cures (the Healer's), a thing handmade for one person as the gift (the Sculptor's), matchmaking.
- **Lead:** the Pink Lady and Hazel Dawn's surprise party. Oxford Companion, PINK LADY COCKTAIL (pdf 1521–1522, Wondrich), primary; its sources are Grimes's 2009 correspondence and the *San Francisco Chronicle*, May 28, 1911. The musical *The Pink Lady* opened at the New Amsterdam in 1911, starring Hazel Dawn (1890–1988). She said the drink was created for a surprise party in her honour at Murray's Roman Gardens on 42nd Street. That first Pink Lady was ojen (a Spanish anisette) shaken with Peychaud's and Angostura. The gin-and-applejack drink that entered the books (Straub, 1913) is probably a different one. **Passes because** a surprise is by definition done without asking (2, 3); the night was hers; whoever made her drink is not on record (1); and their actual gift is the drink that did *not* keep the name (5). Nobody was harmed (4). **Guards:** the account comes through a correspondent of Grimes, reporting what Dawn told them, so tell it as "she said" or "as she told it". The makers and the party's organisers are unknown: never invent who they were. Oxford says "probably not the one" (keep "probably"). Never "ladies' drink" or any gendered reading of the guest. The name contains "pink": Lover's Flirt owns pink as a colour, so the glass needn't be pink and the reading never makes colour the point (own this in one clause). The Waldorf, Straub's source, stays unnamed (*A Brother's Care*'s legend site). The Jack Rose comparison stays out (the Daisy backup in the Genie row sits at Eberlin's, which was famous for Jack Roses). Ojen is an anisette: no louche imagery (*Rosetta*).
- **Backup:** Leopold Mourier and Fouquet's. Oxford Companion, FOUQUET, LOUIS ÉMILE (pdf 835, Castellon), primary. Fouquet opened the Criterion at Saint-Lazare at twenty-three, published *Bariana* (1896, the second French cocktail book) and opened Criterion-Fouquet's Bar on the Champs-Élysées in 1899. After his early death, his friend Leopold Mourier, a famed restaurateur, became the tutor of Fouquet's two sons and built Fouquet's Bar into one of the most famous and fashionable in Paris. **Passes because** the success carries the friend's name, not Mourier's (1, 2), and taking charge of a dead friend's sons and business could have been overstepping (3). **Guards:** only two sentences on the page, so the room adds nothing beyond them (no motive, no feelings, no "he never asked for credit"). Say "died young", not the illness (typhoid: health ground). "Tutor" is Oxford's word; if the room glosses it as "guardian", label it our reading. That the bar still bears Fouquet's name today needs a source before it's said. Test (5) runs the other way here (Fouquet never saw it), so Wren may find it weaker.
- **Wren:** accept. The unknown maker isn't a gap in the story. It's the point: a surprise is help given without asking, the night was hers, and the drink that dropped out of the books while her name went on is the Godparent's cost in the story's own shape (they slip away and never learn how it landed). Rulings: the reading's person is whoever arranged and made it, never Hazel Dawn (she's the one at the ball); every line about the makers is signposted ("I like to think…"), because nothing about them is known; the point is a night arranged unasked, not a drink made to measure for one person (*Quite Alive*'s); pink gets Hester's one clause and nothing more. Lover's Mary Pickford row is also a drink named for a star of the day, so this reading never makes "named for a star" the point. Mourier stays second: it carries grief, and test 5 runs backwards.
- **Drink direction (Tomás):** Old-Fashioned · ojen (a Spanish anisette; no brand, substitute: any Spanish anís) with Peychaud's and Angostura · cocktail glass, chilled. From the story because it *is* the story's drink: the 1911 Pink Lady, a jigger of ojen shaken with the two bitters (Oxford pdf 1521), the one that lost its name. Nothing bridges to it; the spark, if any, is the room's. **Watch:** an anisette shaken over ice may turn cloudy, and a cloud tinged red by Peychaud's is *Rosetta*'s picture ("Wait for the cloud. Then bring the red up into it"). The room has to settle on paper whether ojen clouds when diluted. If it does, the cloud is owned as physics in one clause, never shown or made the point. Hester's one pink clause lands on the bitters, which give the drink its colour (my craft knowledge), not on the name. Veto-free.

## magician-creator · The Genie
- **Wren's test:** (1) a modest request that came back as far more than was asked; (2) the joy of the overflow is on the record, not only its size; (3) the maker's own idea stayed unmade or unasked for, or came much later; (4) a near-miss of "too literal" is welcome, a disaster isn't; (5) the person who asked was delighted. Out: a drink "made for the staff" (*Still Yours*), a film crew (*Overnight*), a happy accident.
- **Lead:** Eben Klemm, from the Campbell Apartment to Pico. *A Proper Drink*, pp. 134–136 (Simonson, reliable secondary; Klemm's words on the record). A biology major who had worked as a molecular biologist, Klemm bartended in New York to pay the bills "while trying to write a novel", teaching himself the trade. At the Campbell Apartment (1999) the management picked ten drinks from an old hotel bar book "without giving us the recipes", so the opening bartenders taught themselves the drinks "based on the ingredients alone". Everyone wanted a light beer anyway, "but Klemm decided to care": with no orange bitters to be had, they mixed Cointreau and Angostura; he found out maraschino was a real liqueur and bought the one bottle Astor Wines had. At Pico (2001) he offered a deal: he'd improve the lagging bar if he could create original cocktails. The owners agreed, and one drink "rendered drinkers speechless". **Passes because** ten names on a list came back as ten rebuilt recipes (1); "I thought, This is kind of cool" is on the record (2); the novel is his own project on the page, and at Pico he asked for his own freedom at last (3); building drinks from their names alone, with a stand-in for orange bitters, is the too-literal near-miss (4); the Pico owners said yes and the drinkers were speechless (5). **Guards:** never say whether he finished the novel (not on the page). The ten drinks came from "the Waldorf-Astoria cocktail book": say "an old hotel bar book" (*A Brother's Care* owns the Waldorf legend). The Anne Hester's Campari foam stays out (Explorer and Lover hold Campari). "Cosmo" and Toby Cecchini stay out (*Still Yours*). His prickly reputation (p. 136) is not for the reading. Klemm is quoted in *Not Only the Way*'s dossier on Uyeda (not its reading): owned here in one clause. Simonson's "slowly taught himself" is his summary; the quotes are Klemm's.
- **Backup:** the Daisy at Fred Eberlin's. *Imbibe!*, pdf 103–104 (Wondrich), primary. On July 7, 1873, Billy Taylor walked into Eberlin's stand on New Street, near the Stock Exchange, and asked the bartender, Frank, for "something new". Taylor "called out one ingredient after another, which Frank mixed and set before him", sipped, and said "By George, that's a daisy!" The *New York Press* told it twenty-four years later, "when Frank was still behind the bar". Eberlin's stayed famous for its Daisies, "particularly when 'Old Frank' Haas" was shaking them; his secret was "something else": "Old Frank, that's all." **Passes because** "something new" came back as a drink family that outlived everyone in the room (1); the delight is on the record (2, 5); Frank mixed exactly what was called out, the too-literal near-miss that worked (4); and his own part was the thing nobody could name (3). **Guards:** Wondrich calls it "later, yet still unusually precise, memory" with "a surprising amount of evidence": tell it as the story told in 1897. The page never says the 1873 Frank and "Old Frank" Haas are the same man: never merge them. The Daisy's later line to the Margarita and the Tequila Sunrise stays out (*Half a Rim*, Explorer's frozen Margarita and blended Margarita). Jerry Thomas printed it in 1876: never name him here (he's the Entertainer row's). The Jack Rose stays out (the Godparent row's Pink Lady).
- **Wren:** accept. Ten names with no recipes came back as ten drinks: that's the overflow. "This is kind of cool" is the joy. And the Pico bargain is the wish finally said out loud, which acts out the Genie's position on the page. Rulings: tell the Campbell half as more than was asked, never as "doing it properly when nobody would notice" (that's the Craftsman's, *Not Only the Way*); building drinks from their names alone is the too-literal near miss, told lightly; the novel appears only as his own project waiting in the wings, never finished or failed. The Daisy backup is weaker: Frank mixed exactly what was called out, which is the Genie's fear, not the Genie's gift.
- **Drink direction (Tomás):** Daiquiri (Collins) · London dry gin and vodka, split · no brand; a fresh rosemary syrup made at home · Collins glass, ice. From the story because it's the drink Klemm made with the freedom he bargained for: the Collins d'Electra, gin and vodka, "he created a fresh rosemary syrup" (*Proper* p. 135). Lemon and soda are my craft call; only its name says Collins. **Watch:** its registry neighbour is *Not Too Polite* (Daiquiri · London dry gin · Collins glass); the split base and the rosemary are the difference, owned in a clause. If the room wants more distance, the page's other Pico drink (bourbon, lightly sweetened apricot–passion fruit tea, soda, mint: Highball · bourbon · tall glass) is free in every plan, but a steeped tea sits near *Overnight*'s tea-steeped rum. The Appointment in Angola's chiles stay out (`spice`). Veto-free.

## magician-regular-guy · The Entertainer
- **Wren's test:** (1) a showpiece that looks easy in public and took long private repetition (practised with water, for years, alone); (2) the audience is ordinary people, not an elite; (3) the payoff is shared delight; (4) a fumble, a risk or a failed night is admitted somewhere; (5) what holds: the joy was real and it spread. Out: hoaxes, exclusive or royal audiences, spectacle for the teller's glory alone.
- **Lead:** the Blue Blazer and the cold-water practice. *Imbibe!*, pdf 140–142 (Wondrich), primary; Oxford Companion, BLUE BLAZER (pdf 297–298, Wondrich), primary. A hot scotch toddy set alight and poured back and forth between two mugs "in as broad an arc as the maker can manage". Thomas's own 1862 note: the novice "should be careful not to scald himself. To become proficient … it will be necessary to practise for some time with cold water." By 1883 a Chicago bartender called it "a great country drink, as the 'jays' think more of watching the blaze than they do of the drink". The same year, in Kansas City, a bartender who tried it for fun "nearly burned his hand off". Matt Higgins, a young New York mixologist, would even mix it out of the customer's sight. It was popular from the 1860s to about 1900, and it came back in the cocktail revival as "a popular and highly visual way for bartenders to demonstrate their skill". **Passes because** the cold-water practice is the hidden hours (1); the "jays" are exactly the ordinary crowd (2); watching the blaze was the pleasure (3); the scald warning and the Kansas City burn are the fumble (4); and it spread and came back (5). Higgins hiding it is a lovely foil. **Guards:** Jerry Thomas is already in *Beside the First* (creator-sage) for the Improved Cocktail. Own it in one clause, and never touch his books' editions, "Improved" or "first author". Asbury's gold-miner tale is legend (Wondrich doubts it); Thomas's "fit of musing" origin was told to "a gullible Englishman": legend. "This drink is solely my own" is contested (a "blue blazes" of 1812; the St. Nicholas Hotel bartenders). The revival's pre-heating trick is DeGroff's (Hero's): never name him. "Necromancer", "nectar for Pluto" and "Satanic boss" are magic puns: never. The Kansas City burn is told lightly, with no injury detail. Fire at home is Tomás's call; the guest is never told to throw flame.
- **Backup:** Huber's Spanish Coffee in Portland. Oxford Companion, SPANISH COFFEE (pdf 1852, Wondrich), primary; Brooke Jackson-Glidden, "At Huber's Cafe, John Pierce Is the King of Spanish Coffee", *PUNCH*, April 14, 2022, secondary (trade press). Oxford: brought to America by Juan Abella in 1965, it "found its true home" at Huber's, which, forty years later, served five thousand of its "elaborated, perfected" Spanish Coffees a week. PUNCH: Pierce has made them tableside for more than forty years, taught by the owner, James Louie ("I was there when James was making them himself, one at a time"). His lesson: "not to show off … Keep it simple, and do it the same way every time", "because when you do that, you have an accident." **Passes because** the flame looks easy because it's done the same way every time (1); thousands of ordinary diners a week (2, 3, 5); and the accident that comes from showing off is on the record (4). **Guards:** Oxford calls it "an elaboration of Pedro Chicote's 1928 flaming Coffee Grog": Chicote is the Sorcerer's Apprentice backup, so he's never named here. Pierce's tenure and the weekly count are dated claims ("in 2022"; Oxford's "forty years later"). Oxford gives Kahlúa, whipped cream and nutmeg: nutmeg is a `nuts` veto (Tomás). Never "the king" as our claim (PUNCH's headline).
- **Wren:** accept. "Practise for some time with cold water" is the hidden hours in the maker's own words, and a crowd that came to watch the fire more than to drink is the ordinary audience. Rulings: "jays" is never the bartender's word, because it mocks the very people the Entertainer plays for, so paraphrase it warmly with attribution. The fumble is the scald warning; the Kansas City burn stays in the dossier (injury). The guest is never asked to light anything. If the proposal is "let one person see the practice", the practice with water is its image. Higgins mixing it out of sight is the room's call, as a foil only.
- **Drink direction (Tomás):** Old-Fashioned (hot toddy) · Scotch, as Thomas's 1862 book called for · Caol Ila recommended, "the brand Thomas had in his cellars" (Wondrich, *Imbibe!* pdf 142; substitute: any Islay single malt) · small heatproof bar tumbler, the recipe's own "small bar tumblers". From the story because it is the Blue Blazer itself (Scotch, boiling water, sugar, lemon peel), and its gesture is the story: the throw between two warmed mugs, **unlit**, practised first with cold water as Thomas told the novice. No flame at home, ever (Wren). Unlit, it needs no cask-strength whisky. **Watch:** pouring between vessels is Innocent's Boadas throw (cold). The Blazer's throw is hot and decades older; own it in one clause. The studio already has three hot toddies (*Off Duty*, Irish whiskey; Quest Seeker, Zirbenz; Geisha, apple brandy): the Scotch and the throw are the difference. Veto-free.

## magician-explorer · The Alchemist
- **Wren's test:** (1) one formula chased over many attempts, with the failures on the record as the method; (2) private or coded notes, or a closely held formula; (3) a real point of no return: a step that commits it; (4) what holds: the patience paid, and the result is better, not just different. Out: accidental discoveries, "secret monk recipe" legends as fact, any elixir or long-life claim, anything *Overnight* or *Off-Label* used.
- **Lead:** Aeneas Coffey and the column still. Oxford Companion, COFFEY, AENEAS (pdf 541), COFFEY STILL (pdf 543) and CELLIER-BLUMENTHAL (pdf 419), primary. An exciseman from 1800 who rose to inspector-general of excise in Ireland, Coffey left the service in 1824, at forty-four, and bought the Dodder Bank Distillery. His prototype stills were wood and iron, one column; they were soon replaced by two copper columns (analyser and rectifier) with perforated copper plates. The result was simpler, sturdier, cheaper to heat, and gave a stronger, purer spirit. The leading Irish distillers scorned it ("These things no more yield whiskey than they yield wine or beer"), yet within ten years of the patent thirteen Irish distilleries had it, and Scotland followed (Grange, 1834; eleven more by 1850). His last foreman built the firm into a leading still maker that is still in business. **Passes because** the wood-and-iron prototype giving way to copper is the method (1); the patent is the held formula (2, partly); leaving the excise at forty-four for a distillery is the step he couldn't take back (3); and the still that came out of it is the basis of the modern column still (4). **Guards:** (2) is the weak point: a patent is protected but public, and no private notes are on the page (tell Wren). Stein (1828) and Cellier-Blumenthal came first; Oxford says Coffey "greatly improved" their design: never "he invented continuous distillation". The patent year conflicts (1830 in COFFEY, 1831 in COFFEY STILL): state no year. His birthplace conflicts ("born in Dublin", or "French-born"): leave it out. The Big Four's quote is from 1878, decades after the patent: never set it at the start. The bayonet wounds from the "poitín wars" stay out (or one plain clause, no glamour). Never Nikka's Coffey Grain as a bottle (Taketsuru is Innocent's and Explorer's).
- **Backup:** Dr. James C. Crow and sour mash. Oxford Companion, CROW, JAMES C. (pdf 607, Bryson), OLD CROW (pdf 1425) and SOUR MASH (pdf 1840), primary. An Edinburgh medical graduate (1822) with an "affinity for chemistry", in Kentucky by 1823, Crow refined and standardized the sour mash process "through the use of proper scientific instruments, experimentation, and recording results". Old Crow, named for him, was being advertised by name as early as 1845. **Passes because** experiment and recorded results are the method (1, and 2 through the records); the brand's early fame is the patience paid (4). **Guards:** Oxford says he is "often credited with 'inventing'" it, but the process predates him (Caribbean dunder by the 1750s; manuals by the 1810s): never "invented". No point of no return on the page (3 fails; tell Wren). Old Crow was Twain's favourite (*Standing By* holds Twain) and Grant's: neither is named. Don't disparage today's bottling.
- **Wren:** accept. On test 2 (Hester's flag): I waive it for the page. The notebook nobody else can read belongs to the persona, and the reading carries it from the person; the story never claims secrecy (a patent is public, so never "secret formula" or "private notes"). The row carries the heart of the test: the wood-and-iron still giving way to copper is the trying, and leaving the excise at forty-four to buy a distillery, years before the patent, is the step he couldn't take back (the room confirms that order on the page before building the proposal on it). Rulings: "better" only in Oxford's terms (simpler, sturdier, cheaper to heat, a stronger and purer spirit), never "better whisky"; the Dublin distillers' scorn is dated as the later view. Crow stays the backup: there's no point of no return on his page, and "Dr." pulls toward the medicine this family leaves alone.
