## outlaw-caregiver · The Gentle Giant
- **Wren's test:** the story's person has real power over someone smaller (size, rank, a reputation that frightens); chooses gentleness when force was expected or easier; breaks with their own kind to do it and pays something; protects without shouting, the holding back visible. Out: crisis rescues, animals rescuing people, medicine, children in danger, any violence.
- **Lead:** Sol Stoumen and the Black Cat Café. US National Park Service, "José Sarria" (nps.gov, secondary, fetched): in 1949 the Board of Equalization suspended the Black Cat's liquor licence indefinitely as "a hangout for persons of homosexual tendencies"; Stoumen, its owner, fought it, hired Morris Lowenthal, argued his customers' right to assemble, and won at the California Supreme Court in 1951. The fight went on: the licence was revoked on Halloween 1963, and he stayed open that last night serving soft drinks to the crowd. Case: *Stoumen v. Reilly*, 37 Cal.2d 713 (1951) (opinion text not fetched: Justia and SCOCAL blocked; the room reads it before quoting). Passes because the licensee held the power over who got served and the easy course was to show the regulars out; he kept them, paid in years and a licence, and did it with a lawyer rather than a scene. Guards: **never state his own sexuality** (Wikipedia calls him straight; no trusted page fetched says so). NPS calls him a Holocaust survivor: single source, keep it out until a second one is found. No police-raid violence in the reading. José Sarria performed there; he stays a named clause at most (his "God Save Us Nelly Queens" is the Subverter's shape, not this row's).
- **Backup (step 4, replaces Hannah, who fails point 3):** Theodore Roosevelt's six cents, 1913. State Bar of Michigan, "Michigan Legal Milestones 4: The Roosevelt-Newett Libel Trial" (marker text, 1987; secondary, fetched): the Ishpeming *Iron Ore*'s publisher, George A. Newett, had printed that Roosevelt got "drunk . . . and not that infrequently"; after a week's trial in Marquette, with witness after witness for Roosevelt, Newett retracted ("I am forced to conclude that I was mistaken"), and Roosevelt withdrew his claim, asking only for nominal damages. The jury gave him six cents. Passes on power (a former president against a small-town publisher), on restraint when he could have ruined the man, and on doing it quietly in open court. Weak on point 3: nothing on the page shows him breaking with his own kind or paying for it. Guards: the libel was about drinking, so the drink tie is honest, but **no Mint Julep** (Lover's one julep row) and none of his testimony about what he drank (not fetched). Wren's guard applies: Newett is never "the small one"; he was an adversary who was wrong, and the story is the strength not used. No Rough Riders, no hunting.
- **Wren:** accept. Passes all four points on what was fetched: he held the licence, the easy course was to show the regulars out, he kept them and paid in years and finally the licence, and he fought with a lawyer, not a scene. The soft drinks on the last night are the holding back made visible. The NPS page is enough for the outline (a federal agency, and the case is checkable); the room reads *Stoumen v. Pity would insult the people the story honours. **Backup Chris Hannah fails my point 3:** no cost on the page. Hester: a page where it cost him, or another backup.

## outlaw-creator · The Rule Breaker
- **Wren's test:** makes something without permission, against a rule or on ground that wasn't theirs; it turns out good, better than what was allowed; the establishment then tries to adopt it (buy, license, make it official); the story shows how they answered. A making, not only a breaking. Out: the absinthe ban, invented origins, "made for the staff", destruction for its own sake.
- **Lead:** George Smith of the Glenlivet. Oxford Companion, GLENLIVET (pdf 917), primary: a tenant of the Duke of Gordon, Smith had made whisky illegally "since at least 1815"; the glen's illicit spirit already had such a name that George IV, in Edinburgh in 1822, "reportedly 'drank nothing else'" and Usher's were selling it by 1821. Urged by his landlord, he took the first licence in the region (1824) under the new Excise Act, and his former colleagues threatened to burn the distillery down with him in it. By the 1880s thirty distilleries had attached "Glenlivet" to their names ("the longest glen in Scotland"), and in 1884 his son won a court order that only theirs was "The Glenlivet". Passes on every point: made without permission, better than the legal stuff, adopted by the state (the licence) and by the trade (the borrowed name), and answered twice, by going legal and by defending the name. Wren's position ("make one thing that isn't against anything, and see it's still yours") is the story's own turn. Guards: the king "reportedly" drank it: keep the hedge. **The pistols** he wore for ten years stay out (family trap: no violence). The court order was his son's (1884), not George's. The threat is a quotation in Smith's words: paraphrase, never quote at length.
- **Backup:** the Carthusians' exile and the Tarragona Chartreuse. Oxford Companion, CHARTREUSE (pdf 439–440), primary: expelled from France in 1903, the order relaunched production in Tarragona and only resettled in the Chartreuse mountains in 1932. For the seizure: Johanna Ngoh, "Tarragona, a Tale of Two Distilleries" (*Distilled*, Substack, named author, secondary, fetched): the government expropriated the trademark and the Fourvoirie distillery, a "government-sanctioned fake" was sold under the name, and the monks sold theirs as the liqueur "made in Tarragona by the Carthusian Fathers" ("Une Tarragone" on French bottles). Passes: a making on ground that wasn't theirs, the state literally taking the name, and the answer (make it anyway, under another name, until the name came home). Weak on "without permission" at the start. Guards: religion respectful, never a pun; the Substack is the only fetched source for the seizure, so the room needs a second one before it goes in; the secret recipe stays a brand claim; Chartreuse's medicinal elixir stays out. Hero reserves the Chartreuse Swizzle (backup): this row never uses it.
- **Wren:** accept. The best fit in the family: made without permission, better than the legal stuff, adopted twice, and his answer is the position itself: he went legal, the thing this guest fears most, and the whisky stayed his. **New guard:** the ending is Smith taking the licence and making the same whisky, **not** the son's 1884 fight over the name. Ending on defending the name lands on *Still Yours* (the Trend Setter's title and its "keep it" position), and my own position line has to lose the words "still yours" too. The threat is told as his old colleagues turning on him, with no fire (violence and fire imagery are family traps). Backup fine as a backup, with Hester's second-source guard.
- **Drink direction (Tomás):** Old-Fashioned · Speyside single malt · The Glenlivet 12 Year Old, recommended (substitute: any unpeated Speyside single malt, not sherry-cask) · small tumbler, one large cube. From the story because the bottle *is* the story: Wren's ending is Smith taking the licence and making the same whisky, so the drink keeps that whisky the loudest thing in the glass, with only sugar, water and bitters around it. Oxford calls the 12 "made without influence of peat or sherry ... subtle, even delicate" (GLENLIVET pdf 918), so the room seasons lightly or it smothers what the story is about. No bridging ingredient: nothing else in the glass claims to be his. **Guard:** the "The" on the label is the son's 1884 court order: fine on the bottle, never told in the reading (Wren's ending). Veto-free.

## outlaw-regular-guy · The Gangster
- **Wren's test:** lives by a code outside the official rules; looks after a specific community the official world failed; is seen by them as one of their own; keeps the code when it costs. The loyalty and the code, never the crime. Out: violence, killings, money or menace as glamour. The family's one Prohibition row.
- **Lead:** Roy Olmstead. Daryl C. McClary, "Olmstead, Roy (1886–1966)", HistoryLink.org essay 4015, 2002 (secondary, fetched): a Seattle police lieutenant (the "baby lieutenant") dismissed after his 1920 arrest, he became the biggest bootlegger on Puget Sound, and his rule on guns was his own words: "I would rather lose a shipment of liquor than a life." By 1924 he was "a popular figure on the Seattle scene". Convicted in 1926 on wiretap evidence (four years at McNeil Island, $8,000 fine); after his release in 1931 he became a Christian Science practitioner who visited the jails and prisons of Puget Sound; Roosevelt pardoned him on 25 December 1935. *Olmstead v. United States*, 277 U.S. 438 (1928), via Cornell LII (primary, fetched): the Court upheld the wiretaps; Brandeis dissented that the Constitution conferred "the right to be let alone", and Holmes called it "dirty business". Passes: a code that cost him shipments; a community he served on both sides of the bars (his crew without guns, the prisoners afterwards); a system that pushed him around being told so by two justices. Guards: he was a lawbreaker: honour the rule, not the trade. No boats, no rum-running routes (smuggling and the sea stay out). Brandeis's dissent is not the law of that case (it became law later, in *Katz*, 1967: **not fetched**, so no date in a reading until the room checks it). Don't use "Good Bootlegger" as a quotation unless a fetched page carries it (HistoryLink says "King of the Puget Sound Bootleggers").
- **Backup:** Texas Guinan and Belle Livingstone. *Joy of Mixology*, pdf 57–58 (reliable secondary); Oxford Companion, SPEAKEASY (old) (pdf 1856), primary: Guinan, speakeasy owner and hostess ("Hello, sucker"), claimed her headwaiter held the liquor concession so her money came from cover charges; when Belle Livingstone came out of her thirty days in prison after a raid, Guinan sent an armoured car to bring her back to Manhattan. Passes on code and loyalty to her own; thin on "a community the official world failed" and on cost. Guards: no "gangster" glamour; the padlock necklace is a costume, not a boast.
- **Wren:** accept. Point 2 lands through the prisons, not the drinkers: after McNeil Island he went back into the jails of Puget Sound, to people he had been one of. A former lieutenant who had seen the system from inside is the learned distrust I sketched, and the no-guns rule is a code that cost him, in his own words. **New guards:** "Christian Science practitioner" stays out (a healing role: medicine is out, and the faith needn't be named); say he visited the prisons. The quotation is the only place guns appear. Swap test against Hero's Gallant (also a Prohibition man loyal to his own, pleading for his waiters): ours is the rule and the prisons after, never a raid. Backup Guinan passable but thin; "Hello, sucker" stays out of guest text (it mocks her own customers, the opposite of this code).
- **Drink direction (Tomás):** Martini (Manhattan-style, stirred) · Canadian whisky (style, no brand) · coupe, chilled. From the story because the base is on the page: HistoryLink says Olmstead saw "an unlimited source of good liquor in British Columbia" and names the cases as "Canadian whiskey", so the whisky is his trade's own, not a Prohibition-era bridge. The shape isn't in the story; I'd stir it so the good whisky is what's tasted, and keep every smuggling cue out of the glass (no boats, no sea, no "rum-runner" anything). Not the Scofflaw: its name makes the drinker the lawbreaker (family trap) and Jester set it aside. Veto-free.

## outlaw-hero · The Maverick
- **Wren's test:** disobeys an order or rule to get a fair result for other people, not themselves; it actually works; they don't stay for the credit (unthanked, uncredited or gone); the cost shows. Out: speeches and pamphlets (the Campaigner's), crisis rescues, violence, stealing for gain.
- **Lead:** Tai Solarin and ogogoro. Oxford Companion, OGOGORO (pdf 1417), primary: Nigeria's palm spirit stayed illegal after independence (1960) under a 1919 treaty that banned local distillation while cheap imports came in. "That finally changed in 1968, when the educator and activist Tai Solarin (1922–1994), a teetotaler, contrived to get himself arrested for possessing the spirit in order to dramatize the injustice of prohibiting a local spirit while allowing foreign ones." Passes on every point, and one better: he broke the law for drinkers when he didn't drink at all, it worked, and the only thing he took from it was the arrest. Guards: it's an act, not a speech: keep it that way so it doesn't drift into the Campaigner's shape (*Not Too Polite*). The page's "finally changed in 1968, when" is its causal claim: say it at that strength, no stronger. Adulterated ogogoro "can be lethal" (same page): stays out. Colonial history told plainly, never as a costume. Any further Solarin detail (his school, other arrests) needs a fetched source first.
- **Backup:** none on a page yet (E. J. P. struck, per Wren). I searched the library for arrests, bans broken for others, licences defied, and "illegally", "blind eye", "disobey"; the locally banned spirits (akpeteshie, bacanora, chinguirito, samogon, okolehao) have no named person who broke the rule for someone else. On the web, Charlie Papazian (taught homebrewing classes from 1973 while it was illegal) is the nearest fit, but Smithsonian and every other trusted page were blocked, and he fails point 4 anyway (he founded and led what followed).
