# Lover family: Wren's sketches (2026-09-30)

All eleven Lover pairings are unauthored. Read fresh from the rewritten personality sheet (brief.py, 2026-09-30). Existing ground to differ from: the pours with a Lover secondary (A Brother's Care / Connoisseur, Standing By / Understanding Spouse, Rosetta / Obsessed) and the near neighbours flagged per row (Sculptor, Angel, Nurse, Rescuer, Trickster, True Friend, Craftsman).

## The whole family first

- **All eleven fear the same word: isolation.** So "you're afraid of being alone" is a horoscope line in this family. It's true of all of them and recognises none. Every reading has to name the *shape* of alone that belongs only to this person. My split:
  - Geisha: the empty table, care with nobody there to receive it.
  - Muse: loved as the idea, while their own making goes uncredited.
  - Rough Diamond: losing someone because they never said it.
  - Pleasure Seeker: the moment the music stops.
  - Knight: arriving in full uniform and finding nobody waiting.
  - Pure Heart: a world where simple love isn't met, and the pull to harden.
  - Flirt: the room where nobody plays back.
  - Charmer: being seen all the way, then left.
  - Bad Influence: being tamed into someone safe, then left for being boring.
  - Sex Symbol: wanted by everyone for an image they built, known by almost no one.
  - Romantic: surrounded by people and still unseen, the short reply to the long letter.
- **Three siblings nearly share one fear:** "not known for who I am" (Muse, Sex Symbol, Romantic). Keep them apart like this. The Muse is *uncredited*: the work goes out under someone else's name. The Sex Symbol is *walled in by their own design*: the image works, and it's theirs. The Romantic is *unmet*: the company is too shallow to follow them down. If a draft for one would fit another, it isn't finished.
- **Family-wide traps.**
  - Nearly every source example is gendered (geisha, Holly, Paula, Juliet, Betty Boop, Marilyn, Courtney Love, Byron's "She walks in beauty"). Never carry the gender over to the guest.
  - A reading must never *require* a romantic partner. "Love" here covers friends, family and a room of people. (Same guard as Standing By.)
  - No sex, no seduction-as-conquest, no drink as an aphrodisiac.
  - Death for love is out as a mirror: Romeo and Juliet's poison, Marilyn's death, Byron's early death.
  - The primary's tone is "all about feeling good". A Lover reading should leave the guest feeling wanted, not pitied.
- **Shared motif pool** (Hester and Tomás, give each to one sibling at most): rose or rose water, champagne or sparkling wine (the Angel already has brut champagne), strawberry, chocolate, anything red, candlelight, "the one", love letters, Valentine's. Letters especially: the Romantic and the Pleasure Seeker (Casanova's memoirs) both reach for paper. Paper belongs to one of them only (see those rows).

## lover-caregiver · The Geisha
- **One true thing:** their love is anticipation. They've noticed the cold, the empty glass, the person nobody's talking to, before anyone asks. The evening only feels effortless because they did the effort beforehand, in private.
- **Hides / fears / gives.** Hides how much of the evening was planned, and how much it matters to them that it lands. Fears the empty table: love with nowhere to go. That's why they wait to be needed instead of asking people in. Gives people the feeling of being *expected*.
- **Siblings.** The Angel (Before the Room) also works unseen in a room, but what the Angel manages is the mood, and it hides its own wants to keep its place. The Geisha deals in specifics and pleasure, and fears having nobody to give to. The Nurse gives everyone the same. The Geisha knows each person's own way. **Collision:** the Nurse's closing line is "For one person, ask how they take it", so the persona sheet's tea-and-how-they-take-it detail is already in the registry. Don't build on tea taken a certain way. The Sculptor makes one object for one person. The Geisha makes a whole evening for a table. The True Friend and the Rescuer already own "let yourself be looked after" as an ending, so that's off here.
- **Traps.** Exoticism: no Japan-as-costume, no kimono or tea-ceremony decoration unless the story truly is Japanese and sourced. "Serving" or "service" as servility. Sexualising the geisha (real geisha are artists of conversation, music and timing). Hospitality as a job title ("the perfect host").
- **Story test.** A real person whose gift was anticipating what others would enjoy, making it look easy while the work stayed hidden.
  - Must show the preparation nobody saw.
  - Must show a moment where the care had nowhere to land, or nearly didn't: the room that didn't fill, the guest who didn't come.
  - Must hold: they kept setting the table.
  - Out: health or remedies (caregiver-owned), quality-only stories ("the best host"), servility, secret recipes.
- **Position fence.** Likely "send the invitation. Don't wait to be needed." Not "rest", not "let them serve you", not "tell them how long it took" (that's the Craftsman's).

## lover-creator · The Muse
- **One true thing:** things get made around them. And they make things too, but the credit goes to the person they inspired, and the story told is "they inspired it", never "they made it".
- **Hides / fears / gives.** Hides their own work, and a small ache each time the credit goes the usual way. Fears being admired from a distance: loved as an idea, not known as a maker. Gives other people the nerve to start.
- **Siblings.** The Visionary (Down the Line) owns the dream that others build on. The Muse doesn't own it and isn't on the cover. The Obsessed (Rosetta) is in love with one thing and dreads "nice". The Muse is in love with people's making. The Sex Symbol builds and controls an image. The Muse is *given* an image by others ("the muse") and it hides the real work. The Romantic fears shallow company. The Muse fears being flattened into a role.
- **Traps.** "The woman behind the man": the guest can be any gender, and the reading can't be about gender even if the story's people are. Beauty as worth. The Greek Muses as decoration. Calling them "an artist" as a label.
- **Story test.** A real person told as the inspiration for a drink, a book or a bar who was, on the record, also a maker in their own right, and whose own part was overlooked.
  - Must show both halves: they inspired *and* they made.
  - The legend gets granted before it's corrected (Robin's rule).
  - Must cost: the name that went on it wasn't theirs.
  - Must hold: the work survives and can be traced back to them today.
  - Out: a person known only for looks; a contested attribution we can't source (then there's no correction to make).
  - Unverified idea for Hester only: drinks "made for" a named patron, where the patron's own contribution is on the page.
- **Position fence.** "Put your name on the next thing you make." Keep it distinct from the Auteur's "let someone else make the second".

## lover-regular-guy · The Rough Diamond
- **One true thing:** they'll drive all night, fix the boiler, sit in the hospital car park. What they can't do is say the sentence. The fear isn't that they don't feel it. It's that the doing won't be enough, and someone will leave without ever having heard it.
- **Hides / fears / gives.** Hides that the words are there, fully formed, and just won't come out. Fears losing someone for never having said it. Gives a love nobody has to wonder about: it turns up.
- **Siblings.** **Collision:** the Sculptor's tagline is "You never say it. You make it." So the Rough Diamond's tagline and whoYouAre can't rest on "doesn't say, does". Split: the Sculptor *makes a precise object*. The Rough Diamond *shows up*, bodily, unpolished, no craft involved. And their positions are opposite: the Sculptor is told to look up; the Rough Diamond is told to *say it*. The Knight's gesture is grand, public and polished (dress whites). The Rough Diamond's happens at 3 a.m. with nobody watching. The Rescuer runs at a crisis for anyone. The Rough Diamond goes for its own people, on ordinary bad days too. The Understanding Spouse swallows a *hurt*. What the Rough Diamond swallows is *love*.
- **Traps.** Rugged masculinity (stubble, sweat, the McClane frame): no gendering, no action-hero glamour. "Diamond in the rough" puns. A drink that sounds cheap or modest (Robin: let the drink sound worth having, never "plain").
- **Story test.** A real person whose loyalty was all deeds, unglamorous and costly (distance, weather, money, sleep), with one recorded moment where the words were missing, or finally came out clumsily.
  - Must show the effort was physical and unseen.
  - Must show the words were the hard part.
  - Must hold: the love was real and it was known.
  - Out: violence, heroics for strangers (the Rescuer's), health or medical crises, anything where the gesture is public theatre (the Knight's).
- **Position fence.** "Say it once, badly. It doesn't need to be good." That's a hard truth made gentle. It must end on what they gain (being heard), not on a fault.

## lover-explorer · The Pleasure Seeker
- **One true thing:** they say yes to the next course, the next city, the next song, and they're the last to leave. Not out of greed. Leaving is the loneliest part of any night, and they'd rather not be there for it.
- **Hides / fears / gives.** Hides the flat feeling on the way home, when the noise stops. Fears that when the music stops, the people won't still be there. Gives everyone near them permission to enjoy things without apologising.
- **Siblings.** The Bad Influence starts the adventure *for others* and wants realness. The Pleasure Seeker chases delight and savours it. The Flirt plays with one room's spark. The Pleasure Seeker wants the whole feast and many rooms. The Obsessed goes deep on one thing. The Pleasure Seeker goes wide. **Paper collision with the Romantic:** Casanova wrote it all down afterwards. If paper appears here, it's writing to *remember* after the fact. The Romantic writes to *reach someone* now. Give letters and memoirs to one of the two only.
- **Traps.** Moralising (the punished hedonist, the lonely old libertine as a warning). Casanova as a seducer: conquest and consent. Excess and drinking more: this is a drinks site, so the reading never praises "one more". Addiction or health.
- **Story test.** A real person who chased pleasure with courage and generosity, whose story has an *after*: the quiet once the feast was over.
  - Must show the pleasures were shared, not hoarded.
  - Must show the after was faced.
  - Must hold: what lasted was the company or the memory, not regret. The after mustn't read as punishment.
  - Out: ruin, debt or disease as the ending; sexual conquest; "he died alone" pity.
- **Position fence.** Something like "stay for the end of one night. It's gentler than you think", or "take someone who'll still be there when it's quiet". Never "slow down" as a scolding.

## lover-hero · The Knight in Shining Armour
- **One true thing:** they love in grand, *promised* gestures and they keep every promise, even the ones the other person has forgotten. Under the polish is the question they never ask: would they be fine without me?
- **Hides / fears / gives.** Hides how much they rehearse the moment, and how unsure they are on the ordinary day after it. Fears arriving in full uniform to find nobody waiting: a gesture that lands on no one. Gives the people they love the certainty that someone will come.
- **Siblings.** **Collision:** the Rescuer (Off Duty) already owns "with nobody to go out for, you're not sure what you're for" and "let them do the going". The Knight's fear is not "what am I for" in a quiet week. It's "*am I wanted*" at the moment of arrival. And the Knight's field is one beloved and a promise, not a crisis and a stranger. The Rough Diamond's love is private and unpolished. The Knight's is public, clean and planned. The Visionary's dream is handed on. The Knight's promise is kept in person.
- **Traps.** The damsel frame: the loved one as helpless and carried out. Gendered rescue (dress whites, the officer and the girl). Military glory. "Hero" puns. Crisis or medical rescue (the Rescuer's).
- **Story test.** A real public, promised gesture of love or loyalty (a vow kept, a journey promised and made, a toast honoured years later).
  - Must show the promise was kept at a real cost.
  - Must show the risk that nobody would be there to receive it, or that it wasn't needed.
  - Must hold: someone was waiting, or the keeping mattered anyway.
  - Out: rescuing strangers, war heroics, a helpless beloved.
- **Position fence.** "Turn up once without the armour, on a day with nothing to win." Not "let someone rescue you" (the True Friend and the Rescuer own that ground).

## lover-innocent · The Pure Heart
- **One true thing:** they've been hurt more often than they show, and every time they decide again not to become guarded. Their openness isn't naivety. It's a choice they keep making.
- **Hides / fears / gives.** Hides how much the last hurt stung. Fears a world with nobody to love simply, and more quietly, fears the day they'd start keeping score. Gives people somewhere they can drop the act, because nobody plays games around them.
- **Siblings.** The Angel (Before the Room) hides its wants to keep its place. The Pure Heart hides nothing but the bruises. The True Friend's kindness is communal ("the world is kinder with you in it"). The Pure Heart's is total, and often aimed at one person. The Charmer is the opposite pole, the one card kept back. The Romantic tries to *understand* love. The Pure Heart just gives it.
- **Traps.** Childish or naive readings, "innocent" puns, the sweet-drink cliché (pink, sugary). Romeo and Juliet: teenage love, poison, dying for love. Being taken advantage of framed as a lesson.
- **Story test.** A real person who gave without strategy (an open-handed gesture, trust given where it seemed unwise).
  - Must cost: they were hurt or let down, on the record.
  - Must hold: they gave again, or stayed open, and it was worth it.
  - Out: tragedy as the payoff, cynicism winning, a con artist's victim.
- **Position fence.** Affirmation: "Keep doing it. It isn't naive, it's brave", in plainer words. The room must not ask them to guard themselves.

## lover-jester · The Flirt
- **One true thing:** the tease is a question: are you playing? It lets them reach someone while risking only a little. They always leave themselves a joke to retreat into.
- **Hides / fears / gives.** Hides the sincere sentence under the wink. Fears the room where nobody plays back, the straight face met with nothing. Gives everyone near them the feeling of being a little more attractive than they were an hour ago.
- **Siblings.** **Collision:** the Trickster (No Accident) owns "you say your truest things as jokes" and "what scares you isn't getting caught, it's being ignored". Split: the Trickster's jokes tug at a *system*. The Flirt's are aimed at a *person*. And the Flirt's fear is not being ignored but being met with *earnestness*, play refused. Keep "ignored" and "truest things as jokes" out of this reading. The Reassurer uses humour to comfort. The Flirt uses it to connect and raise the temperature. The Charmer withholds. The Flirt plays in the open.
- **Traps.** Pin-up and Betty Boop sexualising, innuendo that would embarrass a guest, gendering, "saucy". Tone *can* be funny (Robin): a light reading is right here, as long as it isn't jokes for their own sake. Mild swears in a drink name are fine.
- **Story test.** A real, playful true story (a cheeky name, a teasing exchange, a drink made as a wink to someone) where the play created a real connection.
  - Must show the playfulness was the invitation, not a manipulation.
  - Must show one moment where something sincere sat under the joke.
  - Must hold: someone played back.
  - Out: conquest, humiliation, anything crude; a practical joke on a system (the Trickster's).
- **Position fence.** "Say the next one straight, without the wink, and watch who stays." Or affirmation: the ones who play back are your people. Never ask them to stop playing.

## lover-magician · The Charmer
- **One true thing:** they make one person feel chosen, and keep one card back. The card isn't a trick. It's the part they're sure would make that person leave.
- **Hides / fears / gives.** Hides the ordinary, tender or unlovely part behind the mystery. Fears being seen all the way through, then left. Gives people the rare feeling of being picked out of a crowd.
- **Siblings.** The Trickster reveals how the trick works as a gift. The Charmer's hidden thing isn't a trick at all. The Sex Symbol commands a whole room with an image in plain sight. The Charmer works one-to-one and leaves before the end. The Flirt plays openly. The Charmer withholds. The Pure Heart shows everything.
- **Traps.** The seducer or manipulator reading. Magic words ("spell", "enchant", "magic"): the archetype-pun risk. The Zorro mask as a gimmick. Deception as the virtue (the Trickster's ground).
- **Story test.** A real person known for what they held back (a withheld name, an identity, a recipe, a past), where the hidden part, once known, turned out to be tender rather than a scam.
  - Must show the withholding was protection, not fraud.
  - Must cost: the closeness it kept at arm's length.
  - Must hold: someone saw it and stayed, or the reveal was kind.
  - Out: con men, quality-only secret recipes, a mystery that's only a marketing story.
- **Position fence.** "Turn the card over for one person who's already stayed." Not "be honest" as a scolding.

## lover-outlaw · The Bad Influence
- **One true thing:** their dares are invitations. The midnight swim is how they find out who you are when you stop being polite. And underneath is the fear that if they ever calmed down, nobody would stay.
- **Hides / fears / gives.** Hides tiredness, and the quiet nights they secretly like. Fears being tamed into someone safe, then left for being boring. Gives the people around them the best stories of their lives, and the nerve to be themselves.
- **Siblings.** The Trickster breaks rules on a plan and fears being ignored. The Bad Influence breaks them *with you*, to get close. The Pleasure Seeker chases delight for its own sake. The Bad Influence wants realness, with the delight as the way in. The Trend Setter (Still Yours) is about liking it first. The Flirt plays. The Bad Influence goes all in.
- **Traps.** Glamorising excess, drugs or self-destruction (the Courtney Love frame). **A drinks site must not make this about drinking more or harder.** Harm to others, "rebel" clichés, gendering.
- **Story test.** A real rule-broken-together story (a banned drink shared, a late-night invention, a bar that wouldn't close) where breaking the rule brought people close and was an invitation to be real.
  - Must show the rebel was loved for more than the noise.
  - Must cost: the risk of being dropped once the fun ran out.
  - Must hold: someone stayed for the quiet part too.
  - Out: drunkenness as the fun, overdose or ruin, cruelty.
  - Prohibition speakeasies are allowed only if realness is the point, and only after checking the Outlaw family's claims.
- **Position fence.** "The people who said yes to the swim will stay for the quiet night too. Try one." That's affirmation plus one small risk.

## lover-ruler · The Sex Symbol
- **One true thing:** they built the effect deliberately (the walk, the voice, the entrance), and it works. The cost of building it so well is that people fall for the build, and hardly anyone asks about the builder.
- **Hides / fears / gives.** Hides the person who designed it all, and how much work and control it takes. Fears being wanted by everyone and known by almost no one. Gives a room its moment: people feel more alive when they walk in.
- **Siblings.** The Muse is given an image by others and goes uncredited. The Sex Symbol *authored* theirs and owns it, and that ownership is the wall. The Charmer hides one card in intimacy. The Sex Symbol hides in plain sight, in front of everyone. The Romantic fears shallow company. The Sex Symbol fears people who never try to go past the surface they made. The Auteur owns credit and blame for a work. The Sex Symbol's work is themselves.
- **Traps.** Sexualising the guest in any way (the name is the risk: the reading must be dignified and about power and authorship). Looks as worth. Marilyn's death, or the pitiable lonely star: the power has to stay in the frame. "Uses desire to control" read as manipulation. Gendering.
- **Story test.** A real person who deliberately designed an image or effect and took control of it (their own name, company or brand, against someone who wanted to own it).
  - Must show authorship: skill and choice, not luck.
  - Must show a private self that the image kept out of view.
  - Must cost: being known.
  - Must hold: they kept the control.
  - Out: tragic death, scandal as the story, beauty alone, "used by the studio" victimhood with no agency.
- **Position fence.** "Let one person see how it's built." That's intrigue, not a lecture about vulnerability. The tagline gets the positive frame (Robin): the power first.

## lover-sage · The Romantic
- **One true thing:** they write the long letter. Beauty only feels real to them once they've tried to understand it with someone, and the reply that comes back two lines long hurts more than no reply.
- **Hides / fears / gives.** Hides the fear of being too much (too many pages, too much feeling). Fears being surrounded by people and still unseen, company that can't follow them down. Gives the people they choose a kind of attention that makes them feel worth writing to.
- **Siblings.** **Collisions:** the Connoisseur (A Brother's Care, approved) owns "you've taught yourself to put it into words", "you can always say why" and truth over charm. The Obsessed (Rosetta) owns "you explain the details because 'I love it' sounds like too much". The Romantic is neither *judging* quality nor *hiding* love behind detail. They're longing for a mind to meet. The Pleasure Seeker writes afterwards to remember. The Romantic writes now, to reach. The Muse fears being unnamed. The Romantic fears being unanswered.
- **Traps.** Byron himself (scandal, "mad, bad", the early death, the gendered line). Poetry snobbery. The "intellectual" compliment. Quoting poems at the guest. Paper or letter motifs if the Pleasure Seeker's row takes them: settle in the plan.
- **Story test.** A real exchange of minds: a correspondence, a friendship or a dedication kept up across distance or years, where beauty or understanding was *shared*, not just admired.
  - Must show someone wrote back at the same depth.
  - Must cost: the risk of being too much, or of silence.
  - Must hold: the exchange lasted, or survives.
  - Out: unrequited obsession (the Obsessed's), tasting and quality judgement (the Connoisseur's), death as the romance.
- **Position fence.** "Send the long one anyway. The people who answer at length are the ones to keep." Not "put it into words" (the Connoisseur's).
