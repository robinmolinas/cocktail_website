# Innocent family: Wren's sketches (2026-09-30)

Nine pairings to plan. Four Shares (the True Friend, approved) is existing ground. Each persona was read fresh from Robin's rewritten sheet. These are starting points: every room tests its row again in round 1.

## Family-wide: read this before any row

**The shared fear is the trap.** All eleven share the fear of Punishment, and in the new stories five of them have the same wound: *being mocked or scolded for something sincere, and learning to be careful.* The Naive Artist ("laughed at, then learning to be careful"), the Giggler ("told off… learning to be solemn"), the Heart-throb ("called silly… loving more carefully"), the Dreamer ("scolded for hoping") and the Wise Kid ("told that the truth is rude"). That's this family's version of "all Sages fear deception". So:
- **"Learning to be careful" can be the centre of only one pour.** I'd give it to the Heart-throb, whose story is built on it. The other four need their own angle on the fear (below). The wording ("careful", "shushed", "told off", "grow out of it") goes to one row each, never twice.
- **What gets punished is different in each row, and so is the ending.** The made thing (Artist), the laugh (Giggler), the declaration (Heart-throb), the hope (Dreamer), the question (Wise Kid).

**Age and childhood.** Every persona here is told through a child, a teenager or a storybook figure. The guest is an adult. Never call them young, a kid, childlike, naive or innocent, and never set the reading in their childhood unless the persona demands it (only the Big Sibling does, lightly). The quality lives in the adult now.

**Names that mislead.** "The Big Sibling" (the guest may be an only child or the youngest), "The Little Prince" and "Heart-throb" (gendered sources, never prince/he/she), "Teenage" and "Kid" (not their age). The reading must work for anyone who carries the quality.

**Archetype puns (never).** Innocent/pure/innocence, care-giver, create/creator, explore, hero/heroic, jester, lover, magic/magician, outlaw, rule/ruler/reign/kingdom, sage. **The herb sage in the Wise Kid's drink is a pun.** "Free spirit" vs the drink's spirit is a pun. "Drink Me" is a pun and a cliché.

**Persona examples as touchstones.** The sheet gives famous stories (Toy Story, Alice, Oz, *The Little Prince*, *About a Boy*, *Into the Wild*, *Slumdog Millionaire*, Mr Tickle, Justin Bieber, Dubuffet). Robin allows one familiar touchstone per reading, sourced. It may be the persona's own example, but it's never the mirror: the mirror is still a cocktail, ingredient or person from the bar's history. No celebrity names (Bieber), and no deaths (McCandless, the Little Prince's snake).

**Drinks (for Tomás, one line):** don't let the family become ten soft, pastel, "childish" drinks. No kids' drinks (Shirley Temple, milkshake, sweets) as the base. Robin wants every drink to sound worth having.

**Cross-family ground already taken (check every row):**

## innocent-caregiver · The Big Sibling
- **The one true thing:** they took responsibility for someone smaller before anyone asked, so early they can't remember choosing it. It's not a role they picked up. It's the shape they grew into. The watch never ended: they still count heads.
- **Hide / fear / give:** they hide that they were scared too. They stayed awake in the dark and were afraid of it themselves, and nobody stayed awake for them. They fear someone getting hurt on their watch, and the sentence they'd pass on themselves if it happened (that's their "punishment": self-blame, not someone else's scolding). They give the smaller ones the right to be small. The little ones got to sleep because someone didn't.
- **Versus siblings:** the Rescuer acts in the crisis; the Big Sibling keeps watch *before* anything happens, for years, with no emergency. The Nanny sets rules for the group; the Big Sibling sets none and simply goes without. The Angel hides their own wanting; the Big Sibling gives away their share without noticing it was theirs. The True Friend makes sure nobody is left out; the Big Sibling makes sure nobody gets hurt. The Little Prince tends one chosen thing; the Big Sibling watches over everyone smaller.
- **Traps:** requiring an actual younger sibling (write scenes an only child would recognise: taking the road side of the pavement, staying awake on the night bus so the others can sleep, knowing where everyone is on a trip). The "text when you're home" scene (the Nanny's). "The last biscuit/slice" (the Angel's "last slice"). Any "let someone look after you" ending (Four Shares, Off Duty). A *brother* story (A Brother's Care; own it in one clause if unavoidable). Parenting language, and the word "carer".
- **Position direction:** not "rest" and not "be looked after". Something only they'd need to hear: *tell one of them you were scared too*, so the people they protected can see it was love and not fearlessness. Backup: forgive yourself the one time someone got hurt anyway.
- **Candidate line:** "You were never the brave one. You were just the older one, so you went first."
- **Story test:** the mirror must show (1) someone who took responsibility for another's safety or survival **before anyone asked**, unpaid and not appointed, ideally while young or junior themselves; (2) a watch **kept over time**, not one rescue; (3) a cost to their own share (sleep, time, credit), stated in the source; (4) the one being watched over **came through**, and ideally never knew what it took. **Out:** crisis heroics, medicine and cures, rules and rationing, a shared bowl, a brother preserving a recipe, anything where someone was actually hurt on their watch (it would wound this guest, not mirror them).

## innocent-creator · The Naive Artist
- **The one true thing:** they make things the way something *felt*, not the way it's supposed to look, and they don't know they've broken a rule until someone laughs. For them making is sharing ("look how lovely this is"), not self-expression.
- **Hide / fear / give:** they hide that they've already started editing themselves. They hear the laugh before it comes, and a few things live in a drawer now. What they fear isn't failing. It's being laughed at by someone who knows the rules. They give permission: near them, other people dare to make something badly and enjoy it.
- **Versus siblings:** the Surrealist sees the world is already strange and plays with it on purpose; the Naive Artist doesn't know it's strange. The Experimentalist varies things deliberately; the Minimalist cuts until it's true; the Old Master and the Auteur know every rule. The Free Spirit asks about the world; the Naive Artist answers it by making something. Of the five who share the "mocked" wound, this row owns **the laugh at the made thing, and the drawer**.
- **Traps:** "artist" as a label (the guest may never paint: use a birthday cake iced the colour of the day, a card drawn instead of bought, a table set oddly). Calling them naive or raw (art brut is Dubuffet's term; don't pun on it). "Create/creator". "Strange" and "looked like a mistake" (The Other Berry, Overnight). The famous "invented by accident" trope (No Accident's ground, and it's crowded).
- **Position direction:** *take one thing out of the drawer and give it away*, as it is. Not "don't apologise" (The Other Berry) and not "go back to it" (Off-Label).
- **Candidate line:** "You never set out to break the rule. You just didn't know it was there, and it was lovelier without it."
- **Story test:** the mirror must show (1) a maker **without training or without knowing the convention**, who made something the "wrong" way out of sincerity, **not defiance** (defiance is the Outlaw's); (2) the result was **laughed at, dismissed or corrected** by people who knew the rules, and that's sourced; (3) the thing **was loved or lasted anyway**, by the people it was made for, not because experts came round; (4) making it was about sharing, not status. **Out:** "it turned out better than the pros'" as the whole point (quality-only), deliberate tricks, happy-accident legends, anyone made to look foolish in the telling.

## innocent-explorer · The Free Spirit
- **The one true thing:** their courage is really trust. They walk through the odd little door because they expect it to be wonderful. And their questions aren't challenges. They're delight, and they don't need them answered: a good question is better left open.
- **Hide / fear / give:** they hide how much a flat answer deflates them, the "it's just X" that closes the door. They fear a world labelled so completely there's nothing left to wonder at, and being taken for not serious. They give other people the place back: a walk with them takes longer and goes somewhere you've been a hundred times and never seen.
- **Versus siblings:** **the Wise Kid asks to get the honest answer; the Free Spirit asks to keep the question alive.** That's the line between them, and each row must hold it. The Dreamer hopes for something; the Free Spirit wants nothing but to see. The Outsider steps out to stay clean; the Free Spirit steps *in*, curious. The Researcher and the Doctor ask to find out for others; the Experimentalist tests.
- **Traps:** Wonderland puns ("rabbit hole", "curiouser", "Drink Me"), psychedelia or drug readings of Alice, "free spirit" against the drink's spirit, "explore/explorer". Hardship expeditions (they aren't conquerors). A myth-busting reading, which enacts their fear: if the story's point is "actually it was just…", this guest is hurt, not seen.
- **Position direction:** *don't look it up*. Leave one thing unexplained on purpose, and ask the question even when someone offers the answer.
- **Candidate line:** "You don't ask because you want the answer. You ask because the question is more fun."
- **Story test:** the mirror must show (1) a person, drink or ingredient where **finding out made it more wonderful, not less**, or where the honest answer is *"nobody knows"* and the reading can leave it open with delight; (2) if it's a person, someone who met a strange place or thing **with questions, not conquest or profit**; (3) trust that was rewarded. **Out:** colonial conquest and the spice trade as taking, hardship survival, debunking as the climax, a name-etymology mystery (Four Shares owns "a small mystery" of the name), absinthe hallucination legends.

## innocent-hero · The Underdog
- **The one true thing:** they got here the long honest way and never counted it as winning. So when success comes, the first thing they feel isn't pride. It's the dread that someone will ask how they *really* got it.
- **Hide / fear / give:** they hide that dread behind "I was lucky" (which undersells years of learning the hard way). They fear being accused of cheating when they've only played it straight. They give proof that decency can get there: they make winning look clean, and people root for them without knowing why.
- **Versus siblings:** **the Wise Kid fears being lied to; the Underdog fears being taken for a liar.** The True Friend is loyal; the Underdog is straight. The Visionary chases a dream; the Underdog never aimed. The Rescuer is brave in a crisis; the Underdog is brave without noticing, over years. The Trickster (No Accident) is their exact opposite: never echo "never by accident".
- **Traps:** poverty, class, or "slum" anything. "Hero". Sports-underdog clichés. **Luck as the explanation** (it tells them they didn't earn it, which is the wound). "Impostor syndrome" (clinical). Praising the win (they don't want it praised; they want it believed).
- **Position direction:** *next time someone says "well done", say thank you and don't explain how.* Plain affirmation is right here: you earned it, and it's allowed to be simple.
- **Candidate line:** "Nobody believes the honest version. It sounds too simple. It's the only one you have."
- **Story test:** the mirror must show (1) someone who arrived somewhere improbable **by the long honest road, without cunning or connections**; (2) they were **doubted, suspected or accused** of fakery or not deserving it (sourced); (3) **they really did play it straight**: the source must hold, with no legend where they did cheat; (4) ideally they were the last to realise how far they'd come. **Out:** lottery luck or happy accidents as the reason, clever hustles, a triumph that humiliates the doubters (they don't want revenge), poverty as colour.

## innocent-jester · The Giggler
- **The one true thing:** laughter isn't a performance for them. It's how they check the world is safe. If a room can laugh, it's all right. And when something's funny, it gets out of them before they can stop it.
- **Hide / fear / give:** they hide how often they swallow it, and how much that costs them: a stifled laugh sits in the chest all afternoon. They fear the look (being told off) and turning solemn to stay welcome. They give the room its breath back: one laugh that isn't cruel, and suddenly everyone remembers they're allowed.
- **Versus siblings:** **the Reassurer (Worth the Trip) waits, reads the room and times the joke; the Giggler can't wait and can't time it.** This is the most dangerous overlap in the family: never use the "wrong moment / right one" paradox. The Surrealist notices strangeness; the Giggler just finds it funny. The Heart-throb can't hide love; the Giggler can't hide the laugh (keep the "can't hide it" wording to one row). Of the "mocked" five, this row owns **the look, and the swallowed laugh**.
- **Traps:** "jester", clown, "class clown" (a label). Tickle puns. A reading that tries to be funny and isn't (Robin allows a funny reading; it must be genuinely light). Alcohol as the fun. Mockery of authority (that's the Outlaw's). The taxi (Worth the Trip).
- **Position direction:** *find the other one in the room who's trying not to laugh.* There always is one. The gift: their laugh gives that person permission too. Not "don't apologise".
- **Candidate line:** "You don't laugh to make the room easier. You laugh because it's funny, and then it's easier."
- **Story test:** the mirror must show (1) joy, mischief or laughter that's **harmless and sincere, with nobody made the butt of it**; (2) it **reached into a solemn or serious setting**; (3) it was **frowned on, banned or scolded** (sourced), and (4) people loved it anyway and it outlasted the frown. **Out:** pranks with victims, drunken antics, comic relief in grief (the Reassurer's), rebellion against rules as the point, anything whose humour only works as a pun on the archetype.

## innocent-lover · The Teenage Heart-throb
- **The one true thing:** they say it first. Out loud, before they know if it's coming back. What looks naive is the bravest thing in the room: caring that much where everyone can see.
- **Hide / fear / give:** they hide that they know exactly how it looks, and that every time costs them a little. They fear the day someone calls it silly and they start loving carefully, the cool version of themselves. They give everyone around them permission to feel things out loud: people sing along.
- **Versus siblings:** **the Obsessed (Rosetta) falls for things in private, absorbed at 2 a.m.; the Heart-throb declares in public.** Never use "fall for". The Understanding Spouse stays through the worst; the Heart-throb says it at the start. The True Friend is loyal and quiet about it; the Heart-throb is loud about it. **This row owns "learning to love more carefully"**, the family's shared wound, because the story is built on it.
- **Traps:** age ("at fifteen" only as everyone's memory, never theirs). Requiring a partner or romance: love for a friend, a band, a city, a team counts, and the reading must hold for someone single. Anything sensual or sexual. "Lover". Fan mockery, "cringe". Bieber by name. Tragic lovers.
- **Position direction:** *send the one you'd have sent before you got careful.* Say the big word first again, knowing it might not come back.
- **Candidate line:** "Everyone else waits to see if it's safe. You say it, and then find out."
- **Story test:** the mirror must show (1) someone who **declared love or devotion openly and first**, with no guarantee; (2) a **public, unguarded gesture** (a letter, a dedication, a name given, a song) that some found foolish (sourced); (3) **sincerity with nothing to gain**; and (4) the telling honours the saying itself, **whether or not it was returned**. **Out:** seduction or scandal, affairs, obsession, death for love, marriage as the prize, anyone made ridiculous in the telling.

## innocent-magician · The Dreamer
- **The one true thing:** they trust a hunch before there's proof, and they believe the wonderful thing is usually closer than anyone thinks, already on their feet. Their hope isn't for a far-off life. It's that the ordinary will turn out to be the marvel.
- **Hide / fear / give:** they hide their wishes now, because hoping out loud got them scolded ("don't get your hopes up"). They fear being punished for hoping: the disappointment waved at them like proof they were silly. They give meaning to ordinary days. They notice the sign, and suddenly the day matters to someone else too.
- **Versus siblings:** **the Healer already owns hoping someone will be all right** ("You believe they'll be all right before they do"): the Dreamer's hope must never be about recovery or another person's pain. **The Visionary owns "the dream is yours"** (size, building, handing on): the Dreamer's is small and near, and never "dream" in the tagline. The Free Spirit wants to see; the Dreamer wants something to come true. The Underdog doesn't notice arriving; the Dreamer notices the sign before. Of the "mocked" five, this row owns **the hope, hidden to protect it**.
- **Traps:** "magic/magician", "manifesting", Oz clichés ("no place like home", rainbow, yellow brick road, ruby slippers) beyond one sourced touchstone, luck and superstition (flippant), the absinthe "green fairy" (Rosetta's ground).
- **Position direction:** *make a wish about somewhere you already are.* Point the hope at what's under their feet. Or: say one wish out loud to someone who won't laugh.
- **Candidate line:** "You don't hope for somewhere else. You hope this turns out to be the place."
- **Story test:** the mirror must show (1) **hope or a hunch that came before any proof**, held by someone others thought foolish (sourced); (2) the thing sought **turned out to be near, local or already there**, the far-off prize found at home (an ingredient everyone imported that was growing in the back garden would pass beautifully); (3) the hope changed something, even something small. **Out:** healing and recovery, grand visions and empires, pure luck, the occult, anything where the hope was cruelly disappointed (that enacts their fear).

## innocent-outlaw · The Outsider
- **The one true thing:** they chose small on purpose. What they guard isn't solitude. It's keeping their life clean, uncompromised, theirs. They'd rather have less than owe anyone a pretence.
- **Hide / fear / give:** they hide that it has cost them (some loneliness, some doors closed), and that people read the choice as failure or as judgement of their own lives. It isn't judgement. They fear being pulled back in: the offer, the obligation, the "just this once" that spoils it. They give others a glimpse of another way: people leave their company calmer and remember what they actually need.
- **Versus siblings:** **the Minimalist (Half a Rim) takes away from a made thing until it's true; the Outsider keeps a whole life small.** Never the "less is more / stop cutting" shape. The Free Spirit wanders in; the Outsider has stepped out. The Campaigner and the Trend Setter fight the mainstream; the Outsider doesn't fight anyone. The True Friend needs belonging; the Outsider is fine alone and isn't lonely by nature.
- **Traps:** "outlaw", hermit, loner, dropout, "off-grid" (labels). Assuming they live in a cabin (the guest may have an ordinary city job and simply refuse the promotion, the feed, the second car). Romanticising poverty or the wild. **The death in *Into the Wild***: never. Moonshine or bootlegging (illegal, and a pun). Anti-commercial rants (that's the Campaigner's).
- **Position direction:** affirm the choice, and give one warm opening that isn't compromise: *keep it small, and show one person how you live.* Not "let someone in to rescue you".
- **Candidate line:** "People think you gave something up. You kept something. That's different."
- **Story test:** the mirror must show (1) a person, maker or ingredient that **stayed outside the race** (refused to scale, sell, modernise or chase fame) **for integrity, not protest**; (2) **not bitter, not fighting anyone**; (3) the choice **cost something real and was freely made** (sourced); (4) what they kept is still honest today. **Out:** death or ruin in the wild, poverty, illegality, monastic or religious retreat (a claims risk; green Chartreuse is already in Beside the First), a big-brand buyout told as betrayal.

## innocent-ruler · The Little Prince
- **The one true thing:** they look after one small world, a plant, a friendship, a ritual, with complete seriousness, every day. They don't want more world. They want this one kept properly, and grown-ups' numbers and titles honestly puzzle them.
- **Hide / fear / give:** they hide that people find their seriousness about small things childish, and that they've stopped explaining. They fear failing the one thing in their care (the rose), which frightens them far more than any big failure. They give the one thing the rarest gift: being taken completely seriously. To be someone's "one" is to be tended every morning.
- **Versus siblings:** **the Big Sibling watches over everyone smaller; the Little Prince tends one chosen thing** (tending, not guarding). The Sculptor (Quite Alive) makes one thing for one person; the Little Prince *keeps* one thing, daily, for years. The Nurse gives everyone the same; the Little Prince already has made one special (so don't end on Everybody's move). The Nanny imposes structure on others; the Little Prince's daily chores are only for their own small world. The Auteur controls; the Little Prince tends.
- **Traps:** "prince", he, the gendered title. Rule/ruler/reign/"small kingdom" puns. Quoting the book beyond one short sourced touchstone. The snake and the ending (death). The rose as romance (keep it open: the rose can be anything). A condescending "grown-ups" tone aimed at the guest's own friends.
- **Position direction:** encouragement, since their instinct is right: *keep tending it*, and allow one morning off without believing it will die. The thing you've tended for years can survive one day without you. (A gentle truth to someone whose fear is failing it.)
- **Candidate line:** "Everyone else wants a bigger world. You'd rather keep this one well."
- **Story test:** the mirror must show (1) **one small thing tended daily and seriously over a long time** (one barrel, one vine, one starter, one tree, one bar's one drink); (2) by someone **uninterested in scale, status or numbers** (sourced); (3) the devotion is to *that one thing*, not a brand or empire; (4) it **survived because of them**. **Out:** empire and brand-building, perfectionism and control, making for one person, rules for others, death, the solera (check the fact card: probably already used as "rows of barrels").

## innocent-sage · The Wise Kid
- **The one true thing:** they see straight through someone's act to what that person actually feels, and they say it in the plainest words, not to win but because they want to know. Then they wait. Clever people drop the pose around them and start to care.
- **Hide / fear / give:** they hide that they've been told it's rude, and now they swallow some of their questions. **They trust people, so they can be lied to:** a lie "for your own good" from someone they trusted is the wound (unlike the Connoisseur, who can't be fooled). They give everyone permission to stop pretending: after their question, the room is honest.
- **Versus siblings:** **the Free Spirit asks to keep wondering; the Wise Kid asks to get the honest answer.** The Underdog fears being taken for a liar; the Wise Kid fears being lied to. **The Campaigner owns "Someone had to say it"** (saying what nobody will, as a fight). **The Doctor owns the asker** ("You ask 'since when?'", a caring diagnosis). The Connoisseur can't be fooled; the Wise Kid can, because they trust. So this row's centre is neither the saying nor the asking. It's **seeing through the cool to the person, and the change it causes**. Of the "mocked" five, this row owns **"the truth is rude"**.
- **Traps:** "kid", childish, "wise beyond your years" (Barnum and condescending). **Sage as an ingredient (pun).** The Emperor's New Clothes (the obvious touchstone; allowed once, but it's a cliché, so try to find better). "The truth at the bottom of the glass" (the Trickster's), "Nothing in this glass is pretending" (the Connoisseur's). Detective or gotcha framing. Health questions (the Doctor's).
- **Position direction:** *ask the simple one, then let the silence last.* They're good at the question. The courage they need is to wait through the pause, and not apologise it away.
- **Candidate line:** "You never meant to say anything clever. You just asked, and everyone went quiet, because you were right."
- **Story test:** the mirror must show (1) a **plain question or observation from someone without standing** (a newcomer, a junior, an outsider) (2) that **saw through a pose or a comfortable story** others had kept up, (3) and **made someone change or care**: the truth turned out kind, not humiliating (sourced); (4) the asker **wasn't out to expose anyone**. **Out:** legend-busting as the climax (every pour already "grants the legend before correcting it", so it's crowded), deception as the core (the Connoisseur's and the Trickster's), activism, anyone publicly humiliated by the truth.

## For Hester: collisions to check once, across the family
- One story, person or ingredient per row. The **"far-off thing found at home"** (Dreamer) and the **"stayed small on purpose"** (Outsider) could land on the same local-producer story. Pick which row owns it.
- The **Big Sibling** and the **Little Prince** could both land on a "kept something alive" story: watching over people vs tending one thing. Split them the same way.
- The **Naive Artist** and the **Wise Kid** both involve an outsider without standing: making vs asking. Don't use the same kind of person twice (e.g. two untrained newcomers behind a bar).
- The **Heart-throb** and the **Giggler** both involve something that "gets out" in public: love vs laughter. Keep "couldn't hold it in" wording to one row.
