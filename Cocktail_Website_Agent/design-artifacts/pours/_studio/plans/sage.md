## sage-caregiver · The Mentor
- **Wren's test:** (1) someone who taught or backed another person, (2) who outgrew, surpassed or left them, (3) with the teacher glad of it (on the record or a signposted "I like to think"), and (4) honesty at a cost (advice that could have flattered and didn't, or a teacher correcting their own teaching). The student's success is the student's own. Out: the master who kept the secret, credit fights, health, quality-only. Best if the drink travelled through the student.
- **Lead:** Jerry Berns and Brian Rea. Oxford REA, BRIAN, pdf 1614–1615 (primary tier): Berns, a founder of the 21 Club, "schooled the enthusiastic student", who rose to head bartender, then left, and in 1969 "dreamed up" a job that didn't exist (beverage director), pitched it to six companies, and had a career as consultant, author and "barchivist". Passes (1) and (2) on the page, and the success is plainly Rea's. Guards: (3) is not on any page I read, so it is a signposted "I like to think" only. (4) is not a story fact (nothing on record shows Berns being honest at a cost) and is never invented: per Wren, the Sage half (their own advice turning out false) lives in whoYouAre and the position, in the guest's half. "The father that Rea never had" and his foster childhood stay in the dossier, never in guest text. Rea's war service (Merchant Marine) stays out. The 21 Club's Prohibition past stays out (Outlaw ground).
- **Backup:** Emile Christian Hansen and Percival Greig. Oxford FERMENTATION, pdf 765 (primary): Hansen isolated the first pure yeast cell (1883); in 1893 Greig of Jamaica, "a student of Hansen's", isolated the fission yeast behind "the unique character of Jamaica rum". Passes (1) and (2), and the drink really did travel through the student, which Wren wanted. Guards: (3) and (4) are not on the page, as above. Never "Hansen discovered the rum yeast". Lager is Hansen's; the beer in his story is never the drink (gluten).
- **Wren:** accept the lead, on conditions. Hester's flag: (3) can be signposted, because it's a motive ("I like to think Berns was glad"). (4) can't, because it's an event, and making up a hard truth Berns told him would be exactly the false advice this guest fears. So I drop (4) as a story fact. The Sage half (their own advice turning out false) moves into whoYouAre and the position, where it belongs to the guest, not to Berns. Why the row still stands: the page has the one thing no sibling shares, being outgrown. Berns taught Rea "the bar and restaurant business", and Rea's own idea took the bar out from under the restaurant: a job that hadn't existed ("separate bar and beverage operations from food operations"), pitched to six companies until one said yes. That success is plainly Rea's. Guards added: "the father that Rea never had" and the foster childhood stay in the dossier, never in guest text (the parent trap, and the story would turn into the student being rescued instead of the teacher being glad). Berns is "a founder" (with his brother and cousin), never "the founder". "Arguably the most famous bar" keeps its "arguably". Never tell it as a chain handed on (the Visionary owns that), and Rea teaching others later stays out. Keep the hinge off *Instead*'s "a friend's advice set someone's course". The room should look once for Rea's own words about Berns (his 2017 *B.A.S.T.A.R.D.S.* or interviews, secondary): if they're found, (3) or (4) becomes fact. The backup stays a placeholder: "a student of Hansen's" is the whole relationship on the page.
- **Drink direction (Tomás):** Daiquiri, long (a Collins: Oxford's Southside is "a tall drink of gin, lemon juice, sugar, mint, and soda", SOUTHSIDE pdf 1851; the Codex files the Southside as a Daiquiri variation, p. 128) · gin (London dry; Oxford names no style) · no named bottle · highball glass, ice, soda last. From the story because it's the house drink of the bar where Berns schooled Rea: on the same page, the Southside "has also for many years been the house drink at New York's 21 Club". It's the place's own drink, not a bridge to it. Guards: the page doesn't date "many years", so never "the drink Rea learned" unless a source puts it in his years there. Oxford's own make-ahead version (lemon, syrup and mint steeped in the fridge two or three days, same page) is there if the room wants a gesture that's prepared before the other person pours. Near misses, owned: gin, citrus and mint already meet in *In Your Own Hand* (grapefruit, mint syrup, up) and *Worth the Trip* (gin and brandy highball, a mint sprig), and *Not Too Polite* is a gin Collins in a Collins glass. So the Southside stays lemon and mint, with no grapefruit, ginger or dairy, and the spark comes from somewhere other than more mint.

## sage-creator · The Architect
- **Wren's test:** (1) something designed before it was made, where (2) the design is about what holds it up, the hidden structure, not the look, and (3) it still stands; best if (4) it has a false twin that looked sound and didn't last. The structure must be real and on the page. Out: "it took years to perfect", the Old Master's return to old work, fame.
- **Lead:** the Kentucky rickhouse. Oxford BARREL STORAGE SYSTEMS, pdf 219–220 (primary): in the simplest rickhouses "the racks are all connected so as to form the inner core of the building with the floors themselves attached to that framework and the whole thing covered with a skin of brick, wood, or corrugated tin"; barrels lie with their bellies to the aisles so they can be "rolled out of the racks and moved" from the "intensely hot top floor" to the "relatively cool bottom", which "allows for balanced aging, as does the great amount of air circulation". The false twin is on the same page: palletised storage, "packed very tightly, saving warehouse space, at the expense of air circulation". Oxford BARREL ROTATION, pdf 218: the multi-floor "behemoths in Kentucky" and why barrels move. Passes all four: the racks are literally the load-bearing frame and the walls are only a skin, it is still how bourbon is aged, and the tidy, space-saving twin gives up what made it work. That is the Architect's own fear (the beam that only looks load-bearing) turned inside out. Guards: no person and no inventor: never "someone designed it" (the page gives no designer or date). "Every rickhouse" is false: Oxford says "the simplest rickhouses". Palletised storage is used by named Irish and Canadian distillers: never call it a failure, only the trade-off Oxford names. Wren's (1) is by inference (a frame built to a plan); say so.
- **Backup:** Glenmorangie's stills. Oxford GLENMORANGIE, pdf 919 (primary): whisky made from 1887 "in well-used old gin stills brought north from London"; "Exact replicas of these unique and sinewy stills—an unusual architecture for scotch whisky pot stills—continue to lend the distillery's hallmark top notes"; in the 1990s the distillery became famous for "finished" whiskies (Madeira, Sauternes, port, cognac casks). Passes (2) and (3): the hidden shape of the still is kept exactly while the visible flourishes change. Guards: (1) fails as design (the stills were inherited from gin, not drawn for whisky): tell it as a structure kept, not designed. The finishes are not a "false twin" that failed: they are popular. Port casks touch the Lone Voice row's port: leave port out. LVMH and the packaging row stay out. A named bottle (single malt): Tomás's call, substitute as a style.
- **Wren:** accept. The mirror is the frame against the skin: the racks hold the building up, and the walls only cover it. That is the Architect's fear turned inside out, and it's on the page. Conditions: with no designer on the page, (1) lives in the guest's own scene (the plan in their head), never as "someone drew it". The palletised twin is a trade-off, never a failure. Use "the racks are the building" once: don't spend the same image again in the name or the tagline. Backup is fine as a backup, with Hester's guards (a structure kept, not designed; no port).
- **Drink direction (Tomás):** Old-Fashioned · Kentucky straight bourbon (a style: the page names no distillery, so no brand) · no named bottle · small coupe, chilled, stirred and served without ice. From the story because the page's own word is the Codex's: the racks "form the inner core of the building", and the rest is "a skin". The Old-Fashioned is the family where the spirit is the core and the sugar and bitters only season it, and bourbon is what the rickhouse holds, so the structure is the drink, not a symbol laid on top of it. No ice, so nothing changes the build once it's made. Guards: no gesture that claims the guest's bottle was rotated between floors, and never "every rickhouse". Near miss, owned: *Beside the First* (Old-Fashioned · rye · small coupe, lemon-wiped rim), apart by base and its Improved additions. Kept off the bourbon Old-Fashioned glasses already held: flute (*No Accident*), small tumbler with no ice (*Nothing to It*), cubed ice (Magician's Good Wizard), one large cube (Explorer's Trailblazer).

## sage-explorer · The Philosopher
- **Wren's test:** (1) a question that stayed open, (2) kept being asked, in conversation or across generations, (3) where the obvious answer turned out wrong, or the question mattered more than any answer; best if (4) it is still unsettled, said plainly. Out: a neat solved mystery, the Researcher's chase, anyone who "finally found the truth".
- **Lead:** who invented gin. Oxford SYLVIUS, FRANCISCUS, pdf 1968 (primary): he is cited so widely as gin's inventor that historians "despair of ever stripping him of that title, one to which he has no claim". Morewood (1824) read Kämpfer's note on "Sylvius's oily volatile salt" as "a paternity claim for the spirit in general", "most nineteenth- and twentieth-century authorities followed him without question", and genever dates back "at the very least to the late 1400s", before Sylvius was born (1614). "Only recently" have historians begun to correct it. Passes: the easy answer is the Philosopher's own fear, it held for nearly two centuries because nobody asked one layer deeper, and the true answer is "we don't know who", an honest open question. Guards: Kämpfer's exchange with the shogun was about the "most effective medicine ... for prolonging life": that part never reaches guest text (health). Sylvius was a physician: call him a Leiden professor. Not a "solved mystery": the row's truth is that the inventor is unknown. Never Old Tom (*Instead*). The Symposium's watered wine is not used (Wren's note: it echoes *Fair Measure*). Shape note for Wren: if the Scientist or Lone Voice rows also become "a name on a false claim", only one may run (the Lone Voice backup has that shape).
- **Backup:** the word "cocktail". *Imbibe!* pdf 166 (primary): "the vexed, century-old debate on the etymology", "many, many theories"; a 1798 *Morning Post* satire, found by Jared Brown and Anistatia Miller, lists a "cock-tail, vulgarly called ginger" on a mock tavern slate of politicians' drinks. *A Proper Drink* p. 318 (secondary) ends its account of the best theory: "And that's why they're called cocktails. Maybe." Passes (1), (2) and (4) plainly. Guards: the ginger-and-horse theory is Wondrich's, and Wondrich is not a character here (*Instead*): give the theory, never the man. The horse detail is crude: Wren's call. Croswell's 1806 definition is the Jester family's: not used. The 1798 slate is a satire on politicians, so it can't also feed the Wise Fool row.
- **Wren:** accept, on one condition: the reading lands on the open question ("nobody knows who"), never on the correction. "The famous story is false, and here's the true one" belongs to the Connoisseur (*A Brother's Care*), and it leans towards the Scientist. Here Morewood's misreading is the easy answer, and the gift is an honest "we don't know". Hester's medicine guard stands. Genever as a bottle is Lover's (Pure Heart): Tomás's call. The backup is fine ("Maybe." suits this guest). Wondrich is never named.
- **Drink direction (Tomás):** Old-Fashioned (the Old-Fashioned Holland Gin Cocktail, *Imbibe!* contents pdf 9; Holland gin and brandy were "by far the most popular spirits used for Cocktails", pdf 175) · genever, old-style (oude, malt-wine heavy) as a style · no named bottle · rocks glass, ice. From the story because genever is the page's proof that the easy answer is wrong: it dates "at the very least to the late 1400s", before Sylvius was born. The guest drinks the spirit that was there before the man named as its inventor, and nobody knows who made it, so the question stays open in the glass. Sugar, water and bitters is how Holland gin was mixed before the Dry Martini pushed it out (*Imbibe!* pdf 67–68), so the build isn't a bridge either. Guards: genever as a bottle is Lover's Pure Heart (Highball, a sling, tall glass, ice): owned in one clause; different family, glass and story. Never the Improved form (*Beside the First*), never Old Tom or a Martinez shape (*Instead*). Distilled grain isn't gluten, so it stays veto-free.

## sage-hero · The Grandmaster
- **Wren's test:** (1) a choice that looked like a loss at the time that (2) was chosen on purpose for a later gain and (3) paid off on the record; it must show (4) the moment others thought it a mistake. The sacrifice is real and time passes. Out: a lucky win, cleverness alone, triumph over a rival, health.
- **Lead:** Cedd Moses and downtown Los Angeles. *A Proper Drink* pp. 278–280 (secondary, his own words on the record): "downtown was dead"; "I came downtown with a vision of opening ten bars and revitalizing downtown and cocktail culture in Los Angeles"; he bought property while it was cheap; "Everyone at the time thought I was insane." Golden Gopher came first; Seven Grand (early 2007) was "the big turning point" for Chuck Taggart: "it seemed to launch so many others", and its staff became the city's next bartenders; he "kept opening bars" (the Edison 2008, Las Perlas 2009). The book also says he "could spot patterns and outcomes others couldn't". Passes all four, with a decade between the late-1990s buying and the 2007 turn, which is the patience Wren asks for. Guards: the "patterns" line comes with his teenage horse-race handicapping: gambling stays out, and so does the stock-market money. The bars he built with Petraske (The Varnish, at Cole's) are Lover-family ground: never named. Bukowski stays out. "Ten bars" was his vision; don't say he opened ten unless counted on a page. The payoff is the scene, not anyone losing. The real cost was money and reputation, not a "sacrifice" in the chess sense: say "everyone thought he was mad", not "he gave up".
- **Backup:** Heaven Hill's old stocks. Oxford HEAVEN HILL, pdf 988 (primary): when the American whisky market picked up in the early 2000s, old brands "suddenly began generating a good deal of interest, and its extensive stocks of long-aged bourbon and rye proved to be invaluable". Passes (3) and the time. Guards: (1), (2) and (4) are not on the page (nothing shows the stocks were kept on purpose against advice): weak; the room would need a source. Elijah Craig (a Heaven Hill brand) is the Lover family's Rough Diamond bottle, and Rittenhouse is tied to Wondrich on *Proper* p. 247: both out.
- **Wren:** accept the lead with Hester's guards. Add: the payoff is what other people built (the staff who became the city's next bartenders, the bars that followed), never his wealth. Buying cheap is money talk: one clause at most. What the reading uses is the years of looking mad. The backup is a placeholder only. It can't be promoted unless (1), (2) and (4) turn up on a page. If the lead falls, the room hunts fresh instead.
- **Drink direction (Tomás):** Old-Fashioned · barrel-proof (cask-strength) straight rye, a style · no named bottle · heavy double rocks glass, one large block. From the story because Seven Grand, "the big turning point", was "primarily a whiskey bar" (*Proper* p. 279), the only base the page gives, and because the story is a decade between the buying and the turn. A strong whiskey on one large block is built for its tenth minute, not its first sip: it opens as the block melts.
