## explorer-caregiver · The Conservationist

- **Wren's test:** the story's person found something precious and chose not to own, enclose or exploit it, or kept it going for others; they faced the pull to take it (profit, credit, the easy harvest) and it cost them; the thing survives, ideally still there today. Out: rescue-from-the-brink heroics, a secret, the sea, medicine.
- **Lead: letting the agave flower for the bats.** Agaves flower once and then die; the flowers open at night and are pollinated mostly by bats (Oxford, AGAVE pdf 74). As tequila grew, producers stopped letting plants go to flower, which took away a main food of the *Leptonycteris* nectar bats and narrowed the agave's genetic diversity (Oxford, AGAVE pdf 77; primary). A bat scientist, Rodrigo Medellín (UNAM), and David Suro-Piñera, president and co-founder of the Tequila Interchange Project (a nonprofit for traditional agave spirits), with two UNAM colleagues, argued that letting 5% of reproductive blue agaves flower could feed about 2.3 million nectar bats a month, and proposed "bat-friendly" practices (Trejo-Salazar, Eguiarte, Suro-Piñera & Medellín, "Save Our Bats, Save Our Tequila", *Natural Areas Journal* 36(4): 523–530, 2016; peer-reviewed, secondary). In April 2018 the US Fish and Wildlife Service removed the lesser long-nosed bat from the endangered list, "the first bat ever removed... due to recovery", naming it a pollinator of tequila agave and mentioning "bat friendly tequila" (USFWS press release, 17 April 2018; secondary). **Passes because** it is restraint, not rescue: leave some of the crop unharvested so something wild keeps going; the pull is the harvest itself; the bat and the flowering fields are still there, and a guest could go and see them at night. **Guards:** the delisting had many causes, so never "tequila makers saved the bat"; 5% is the paper's proposal and figure, not a measured industry practice; the cost (a flowered plant is lost to the still) is our inference from "flower once and die" plus the harvest practice, so find it stated before the reading says it; no lecture, no politics, and not Oxford's "the future of agave depends on all of us" tone.
- **Backup: Real Minero's seed nursery.** Graciela Ángeles Carreño runs her family's mezcal house in Santa Catarina Minas, whose nursery grows maguey from seed "to lay a strong foundation for biodiversity"; she stopped certifying the brand "as a political statement and a means of preserving tradition" (Emma Janzen, PUNCH, 17 October 2024; trade press, secondary). **Passes because** it grows back what others harvest wild, for people after her. **Guards:** thin: one secondary source, so the room needs a second before an anchor stands; "political statement" brushes Wren's politics trap; "she gave up the word mezcal" is **not** on the page; mezcal also sits in the Tourist row (own it or skip). *Note:* Shackleton's whisky under the Cape Royds hut fits this test better (found by Antarctic Heritage Trust conservators, three bottles flown to Scotland for analysis and a replica, then returned to the hut in 2013; nzaht.org), but it belongs to the Leader row's expedition. It is usable here only if the Leader row leaves Nimrod.
- **Wren:** accept the lead, on two conditions for round 2. (1) **The story's person has to be someone who actually lets the agaves flower**, a named grower or producer, or the bat-friendly scheme's own growers on a page, not only the scientists who proposed it. A proposal alone reads as "someone had to say it" (the Campaigner's), and my test asks for someone who *chose not to take*. (2) **The cost has to be found stated on a page** (a plant left to flower never goes to the still) before the reading uses it. It's the heart of "it cost them", and I won't build the recognition on our inference. If both hold, it's the right shape: restraint, not rescue, and still there at night for the guest to go and see. The reading never mentions villages or mezcal (the Tourist's), and never says "saved the bat". The backup is too thin to stand. If the conditions fail, the room writes **Open**, and the Nimrod whisky stays with the Leader.
- **Drink direction (Tomás):** Martini · tequila blanco (100% agave) · a tequila from a grower in the bat-friendly scheme, recommended, if the room finds one on a page (substitute: any 100% agave tequila blanco) · a small tulip-shaped tasting glass (copita), chilled, no ice. From the story because the story is in the bottle: buying it pays for agaves left to flower. A stirred, clear drink with no citrus lets the plant be tasted as it is, the persona's "see it as it really is". The page that names a bat-friendly grower also answers Wren's condition (1), so Hester and I look for it together. No agave syrup: it's a bridge to the bats' nectar, and it's made from harvested plants (my knowledge, unsourced), which argues against the story. Stirred and citrus-free keeps it far from *Half a Rim* (shaken, rocks glass), the Innovator (blended) and the Tourist (long). Veto-free.

## explorer-creator · The Innovator

- **Wren's test:** annoyed by an everyday shortcoming everyone else accepted, the annoyance on record; made a new way others now use without thinking; cost: time and being told it was fine; the fix is still in use. Out: a lucky accident, play, a dream of scale, a failed entry kept, medicine.
- **Lead: Mariano Martinez and the frozen Margarita machine.** In 1971 Martinez adapted a soft-serve ice cream machine to make Margaritas at Mariano's Mexican Cuisine in Dallas; the frozen Margarita machine "would soon be a fixture of the age" (Oxford, MARGARITA pdf 1237; primary). Why, on the record: his bartenders couldn't squeeze limes or blend fast enough, and "customers complained the drink was inconsistent and not even cold"; the idea came from a 7-Eleven Slurpee machine; the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History took the original machine in 2005 (Associated Press, via *Chicago Tribune*, 10 October 2005, reprinted by History News Network; secondary). **Passes because** the shortcoming everyone accepted (the blender drink that came out different every time) is on record; the fix is in bars everywhere today. **Guards:** the complaints are on record, his own annoyance is our reading, so the reading says "complaints", not "it drove him mad"; 1971 (MARGARITA pdf 1237) vs 1973 (FROZEN DAIQUIRI pdf 854): no year; Oxford thinks the machine made frozen drinks an "anything-goes crowd pleaser", so never claim it improved the Margarita, only that it fixed his problem; Bermejo's Tommy's Margarita (*Half a Rim*) is on the same Oxford page: keep it out.
- **Backup: Julie Reiner and the jigger at Flatiron Lounge.** At Flatiron (opened 2003, "the first mass-appeal craft cocktail lounge in New York" in Simonson's words), "free pouring quickly gave way to jiggering, then an almost unheard-of practice", because without jiggers the drinks varied from bartender to bartender (Proper pp. 149, 151). The two-ounce jigger came back with the cocktail renaissance (Oxford, JIGGER pdf 1104). **Passes because** it's the same everyday inconsistency, fixed with a tool everyone now uses without thinking. **Guards:** the reason is Simonson's narration, not her quote, so the annoyance is inferred and Wren's test is only half met until her words are found; jiggers date from the 1870s (Oxford), so never "invented"; "first mass-appeal" is Simonson's judgement.
- **Wren:** accept the lead. The customers' complaints on record are enough for my test if the reading says it plainly: everyone else put up with the blender drink that came out different every time, and he wouldn't. His own annoyance is signposted ("I like to think it drove him…"), never stated. The Slurpee machine is his door nobody knew was there, the best line in the row (source it on the same AP page). Guards I'm adding: (a) **let the drink sound worth having**: the reading never calls it a slush or a machine drink; (b) the recognition is the refusal to accept "that's how it comes out", **not the machine as a gadget** (that's the Mad Inventor's pleasure); (c) "being told it was fine" isn't on the page, so it stays out. The backup only half passes, as Hester says, so it's a reserve, not a choice. **Note for Tomás:** this is the family's third agave row (bats, village mezcal, now a Margarita), next to *Half a Rim*'s Margarita. Keep the three glasses and shapes well apart.
- **Drink direction (Tomás):** Sidecar (the Margarita with orange liqueur is the Sidecar's best-known variation, Codex pp. 154, 156), blended · tequila blanco · no named bottle (orange liqueur, Cointreau style) · a large stemmed goblet, chilled, **no salt rim**. From the story because his fix was a Margarita that came out the same every time and properly cold, and at home that's physics, not a machine. Arnold's blender Margarita (LI pp. 114–115) sets the water by weighing the ice, because a blender adds about half as much water again as a shaker. Weighed ice is the refusal of "that's how it comes out" (Wren's recognition), written into the recipe. Kept well apart from *Half a Rim* (Daiquiri family, agave syrup, shaken, rocks glass, one clear cube): different family, texture and glass. No salt rim, because the rim is *Half a Rim*'s name, and no saline dash (*Making the Calls*' epigraph). Never "slush" or "machine drink" in the copy (Wren). Veto-free.

## explorer-regular-guy · The Tourist

- **Wren's test:** someone went somewhere, found one thing a step beyond the usual, brought it home to share, and it became loved at home; the going, the find, the coming back and telling; small risk, a way back, nobody vanished. Out: exile or emigration, plunder told as fun, a holiday joke drink, anyone ending up alone.
- **Lead: Ron Cooper and the village mezcal.** In Oaxaca "for the holidays with friends" in 1986, Cooper found the pure mezcal of the villages, not the industrial firewater sold abroad (Oxford, COOPER, RON pdf 573); he brought it home and sold each village's mezcal under that village's own name, when the only Oaxacan mezcal widely available abroad was a brand whose selling point was the worm (Oxford, DEL MAGUEY MEZCAL pdf 638, 640). For years it had a cult following, until bartenders "clamored" for its cocktail-friendly bottling (Vida, 2010), and his work laid the foundation for other agave spirits in America (COOPER pdf 573). Card: `fact-cards/del-maguey-oaxaca-old-fashioned.md` (F1–F5, C1). **Passes because** it's a holiday with friends, one village further than anyone went, and a way home, and the thing found is shared by name with everyone back home. **Guards:** never "discovered mezcal" (Oxford pdf 640 quotes someone off the record saying an American had to discover it; the villages always had it, and saying so would be the plunder trap); dates conflict (1986 vs 1990, 1995 vs 1996: card C1), so no dates or case counts; no "adventure" (Oxford's word, Wren's trap), no "artist" label; the worm and the 2017 sale to Pernod Ricard stay in the dossier; the Oaxaca Old-Fashioned (Phil Ward) is a different story and stays out.
- **Backup: Dickens, the Sherry Cobbler and the iced drinks he took home.** The cobbler is first recorded in 1838 in the diary of Jane Ellice, a young Englishwoman travelling in New York State ("delicious & easy of composition"). Dickens spent the first half of 1842 in America, praised the julep and the cobbler in *American Notes* (October 1842) and described the cobbler rapturously in *Martin Chuzzlewit*, which "effectively alerted the world"; in 1843 a bar devoted to American iced drinks opened in the Strand (Oxford, SHERRY COBBLER pdf 1782; *Imbibe!* pdf 50 for the *Chuzzlewit* scene). **Passes because** he went, tried the thing locals took for granted, came home and told everyone, and London started drinking it. **Guards:** Oxford says his praise alerted the world, not that it opened the Strand bar, so a sequence, never a cause; how he crossed stays out (sea); *American Notes*' criticisms of America stay out (no condescension); the *Chuzzlewit* scene is fiction and is labelled so; Dickens is a famous touchstone, so it's the reading's one touchstone.
- **Wren:** accept the lead. It's the cleanest pass in the family: a holiday with friends, one village further than anyone went, the way home, and the find shared **by the village's own name**. That last part is the Tourist's gift made literal: you bring it back and tell everyone where it came from. Hester's guards stand, above all never "discovered". I'm adding one: **no condescension in either direction**, so no sneer at the worm brand or at the tourists who bought it. The reading's position (go two streets further) must not echo "one village further" word for word. Owned overlap: the Conservationist's agave. This reading never talks about the plant or bats.
- **Drink direction (Tomás):** Highball · joven mezcal · Del Maguey, a single-village bottling, recommended (Vida, the cocktail bottling in Hester's lead, if the room wants a mixer; substitute: a joven espadín mezcal labelled with its village) · a tall glass with ice. From the story because the find was shared at home, by name. A long drink is the one you make for a table of friends while you tell them where the bottle is from, and the village on the label does the telling. The mezcal leads. The lengthener is the room's call (the Paloma's grapefruit and soda, Codex p. 212, is the obvious start). The Oaxaca Old-Fashioned stays out: it's Phil Ward's story and the fact card's. Far from the Conservationist's stirred tequila and from both Margaritas. The reading never mentions the plant. Veto-free.

## explorer-hero · The Pioneer

- **Wren's test:** a first under watching eyes, with real risk of failing in front of people; the preparation, the fear (on record or honestly signposted), the steady doing, ideally understatement after; it worked and others followed because it was now proven possible. Out: luck, defiance, rescue, a team brought home. "The first" must be solid on the page.
- **Lead: Elmer T. Lee and Blanton's, the first single-barrel bourbon brand.** Blanton's "became the first 'single barrel' bourbon brand when it was introduced in 1984"; Lee, "a longtime distillery employee who had worked under Albert Blanton", guided its creation; the idea came from Blanton's old habit of bottling one particularly good barrel for himself or as gifts; it arrived "after a long slump in American whisky sales" (Oxford, BLANTON'S pdf 283). The modern single-barrel practice "was pioneered in 1984 by Elmer T. Lee" (Oxford, SINGLE BARREL pdf 1806); bourbon sales had fallen for about thirty years, and Lee was "one of the first people to realize" premium bourbon was the way back (Oxford, WHISKY, BOURBON pdf 2143; primary). **Passes because** the first is Oxford's own sentence; the preparation is a long career at one distillery, under the old boss whose habit it was; the risk is a new, dearer bottle in a falling market; others followed (single barrel became a category). And the brand carried his old boss's name, not his. **Guards:** fear and understatement aren't on the page, so signpost them ("I like to think…"); one maker isn't the maker: two executives (Ferdie Falk, Robert Baranaskas) bought the distillery and decided on the business, so Lee "guided the creation"; whether he chose the name isn't on the page, so the name can be noticed but not credited to him; the Japan market and the sale to Takara stay in the dossier; no "fearless", no moon or small-step echoes.
- **Backup: Masataka Taketsuru, Yamazaki and the apple-juice years.** A chemist sent to Scotland to learn whisky (Longmorn, Bo'ness, Hazelburn), he was the distiller at Yamazaki, "Japan's first dedicated whisky distillery" (Oxford, SUNTORY pdf 1956; TAKETSURU pdf 1975; WHISKY, JAPANESE pdf 2156, which notes an earlier licence never used). The company's first blend (1929) failed; later he founded Nikka in Hokkaido, first called "the great Japanese juice company", making apple juice while the whisky aged; the first blend came in 1940 (Oxford, NIKKA pdf 1391). In 2001 a Yoichi malt beat all its Scottish and American rivals in *Whisky Magazine*'s "Best of the Best" (NIKKA pdf 1391). **Passes because** it's a public first that stumbled in front of people and then held, with years of patience nobody saw. **Guards:** dates conflict across entries (1918/1919 to Scotland, 1920/1921 back, 1933/1934 leaving), so no dates; the 1929 flop was Torii's firm's, with Taketsuru as distiller, so don't pin it on him alone; going abroad and bringing a method home is the Tourist's shape, so Scotland gets one clause; Rita Cowan and the TV drama stay out or get one clause; "founding father" is a label.
- **Wren:** accept the lead over the backup. The backup fails my family sheet twice: Taketsuru's years in Scotland are the Tourist's shape (going abroad and bringing a way home, which Cooper already owns), and the apple-juice years while the whisky aged are the Discoverer's word, *time*. Lee fits the Pioneer's own word, *ready*: decades under someone else's name, then the first to go, into a thirty-year slump, and the others followed because he'd proved it could be done (Oxford's own sentences). Conditions: (1) **the recognition line rests on "ready", not on fear.** Fear gets one signposted sentence at most ("I don't know if he was scared. I like to think he was, and went anyway"). It's never the anchor. (2) **Not quality-only**: "premium bourbon was the way back" is a market reading, and the mirror is going first, not a better bottle. (3) The brand carrying his old boss's name is noticed, never credited to him (Hester's guard). That's the understatement my test hoped for, and the room may only point at it, not claim it.
- **Drink direction (Tomás):** Old-Fashioned · bourbon · Blanton's single barrel, recommended (the story names it; substitute: any single-barrel bourbon) · a small tumbler, chilled, **no ice**. From the story because the whole story is one barrel bottled as it is. The Old-Fashioned is the frame that tastes most of the bottle (Codex p. 6: the focus "squarely on the spirit"). With no ice melting after the pour, the last sip tastes of the same barrel as the first: steady hands, in the glass. The room sweeps sugar and water across single-barrel strengths, because every barrel differs and a guest's bottle may be stronger. Kept apart from the Trailblazer (bacon-washed, maple, stirred on one big cube) and *No Accident* (flute, unstirred sugar cube). Blanton's can be hard to find in the US (my knowledge, unsourced), so the substitute style matters. Veto-free.

## explorer-innocent · The Discoverer

- **Wren's test:** a discovery made by stopping for something ordinary that many had seen and passed; the person who took the time and asked why; it turned out to matter; cost: time, being thought slow or odd; the moment on record, not legend. Out: engineered effort, play, a strange thing made on purpose (the Surrealist's), medicine, the sea. Its word is time or busy, never "strange" or "notice".
- **Lead: Tony Conigliaro and the cocktails left to age.** "Conigliaro is a patient man": he put Manhattans and El Presidentes in glass vessels "for years to see how they evolved"; "I'd been trying to float this idea to my customers at Roka for years… nobody paid a bit of attention" (Proper p. 228). He started around 2004; Jeffrey Morgenthaler tasted one of his aged Manhattans at 69 Colebrooke Row and, "being an impatient American", put cocktails in small barrels to rush it; by 2010 barrel-aged cocktails were on menus all over the world (Proper p. 236). In Conigliaro's own words, the idea was that oxidation could be used "in a positive way" if controlled the way it is with wine (Oxford, BARREL-AGED COCKTAILS pdf 216; primary). **Passes because** it's a drink everyone knew, given years instead of minutes by someone who wanted to see what time would do; he was ignored for years, and it turned out to matter worldwide. The patient one and the impatient one are the Discoverer's own "time vs busy". **Guards:** Simonson calls him "the first to toy with aged cocktails", but Oxford shows wood-aged bottled cocktails sold around 1900 (Heublein's, pdf 216), so **never "first"**; his was aging in glass; the 2004 start is Proper's only; Morgenthaler "took the idea to the bank" is Simonson's; *Overnight* owns "leave it and see what the night does", so this reading is about patience and asking, not transformation, and its closing line mustn't echo "leave it".
- **Backup: Ted Haigh and the dusty bottles.** Driving past a sign, "Chicago's oldest wine and spirits merchant" (House of Glunz, founded 1888), Haigh stopped, chatted, was poured a plum brandy, and came back every weekend with his per diem; he hunted neglected liquor stores for forgotten bottles on the bottom shelf ("It's like you stepped out of a time machine," a clerk told him); he mailed a vintage mini-bottle of peach bitters to Fee Brothers, "which led the company to produce a peach bitters" (Proper pp. 64–66). **Passes because** he stopped for the shop everyone drove past, gave it time, and something ordinary came back into the world. **Guards:** his youthful computer fraud (Proper p. 65) is never used; "a bit of the con man" is Simonson's; "Dr. Cocktail" is a self-given nickname (medicine trap: keep it out); the Prohibition-era medicinal whiskey at his house stays out; Haigh helps in Berry's story (Genius lead), so one row only.
- **Wren:** accept the lead. It holds the Discoverer's word, *time*, better than anything I hoped for: a drink everyone knew, given years instead of minutes, by someone ignored for years, and the patient one against "an impatient American". The point of the drink is that every part is named: the recipe is written out in full and the decoded mix is spelled out in the method. Which recovered recipe is the room's call with Hester. The Navy Grog stays out (*Fair Measure*'s grog). **Veto watch:** bought falernum, orgeat and a nutmeg garnish all count as nuts. A falernum made at home from lime zest, clove and sugar, with no almond, keeps the drink veto-free, and that's my default. Ginger may classify as spice (heat), so the table decides before it goes in. Kept apart from the registry's rum Daiquiris (punch bowl, coupe, chilled tumbler) by the tall glass and crushed ice. The Sense Seeker's crushed ice is gin, in a short glass.
