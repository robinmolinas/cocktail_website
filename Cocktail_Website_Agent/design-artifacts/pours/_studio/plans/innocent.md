## innocent-caregiver · The Big Sibling
- **Wren's test:** someone who took on another's safety before anyone asked, unpaid and unappointed, ideally junior; a watch kept over time, not one rescue; a cost to their own share stated in the source; the watched came through and never knew. Out: crisis heroics, medicine, rules and rationing, the shared bowl, a brother keeping a recipe, anyone hurt on their watch.
- **Lead (after Wren's review): rye, the grain nobody asked for.** Oxford RYE (pdf 1714–1715), primary. Pliny, as the Companion quotes him, called rye "a very inferior grain … only employed to avert positive famine" and disliked its bitterness, yet granted that "it will grow upon any soil" and "is employed also as a manure for enriching the land". It sprouts where nobody sowed it (farmers call the strays "volunteers", which is likely why it was first taken for a weed); it grows on rocky or peaty ground and shakes off snow that would frost other grains; and Iron Age farmers kept it once they saw it was useful. Rye whisky came into its own on the rocky soils of Pennsylvania and the Appalachians (pdf 1715). **Passes because** it turns up unasked and unpaid (1: "volunteers" is the farmers' word, the kindness in it is our reading); it has been grown for centuries so nobody would go hungry, through winters that kill other grains (2); its cost is on the page: called inferior and bitter, and even ploughed back in to feed the land for the next crop (3); and the people it fed thought little of it (4). An ingredient, not a person: Wren rules whether that's enough. **Guards:** Pliny reaches us through Oxford's translation ("Pliny, as the Companion quotes him"); any "it came without being asked" is "I like to think"; "so nobody went hungry", never famine scenes; ergot (rye's poisonous fungus): never; the Whisky Rebellion (a tax protest), Washington's Mount Vernon distillery (its enslaved workers) and Prohibition: out; Tomás: rye is Beside the First's base, and the Lover family's Bad Influence and the Explorer family's Mad Inventor hold rye shapes, so the drink differs from all three.

## innocent-creator · The Naive Artist
- **Wren's test:** a maker without training or without knowing the convention, making something the "wrong" way out of sincerity, not defiance; laughed at or corrected by people who knew the rules (sourced); loved or lasting anyway, by the people it was made for; made to share, not for status. Out: quality-only vindication, deliberate tricks, happy-accident legends, anyone made to look foolish.
- **Lead (swapped after Wren's review):** the Kalimotxo, red wine and cola in equal parts. The story goes that in 1972, at a festival in Algorta, a group of young friends (Antzarrak) found the wine they meant to sell had turned bad and rescued it with Coca-Cola and ice; the name joins two of their nicknames. Wine people call it heresy ("cocktail hérétique" is the title of Celhay's article); it's loved anyway across the Basque Country and, under other names, from Chile to Romania. Oxford CALIMOTXO OR KALIMOTXO (pdf 386–387), primary for the drink, legend-grade for the origin (Oxford: "supposedly", from an artists' collective's book); *Cocktail Codex* p. 18 (craft respect for it). Passes (1) no training and the "wrong" mix, (2) the purists' scorn, (3) loved by the people it was made for. Guards: a labelled legend, no anchor on the origin; Spaniards mixed wine and cola from the 1920s (Oxford), so never "invented" by them, only "named"; the wine "tasted… toxic", the "cheap buzz", mixing it in shopping bags: never; it was a fix, not luck: never tell it as a happy accident.
- **Backup (was the lead):** the Alexander, a drink made white. In the best-supported of its disputed origin stories, Troy Alexander, bar manager at Rector's in New York (there on and off 1904–1913), made it for officials of the Lackawanna Railroad, whose advertisements showed Phoebe Snow, an invented young woman who could ride their trains in a spotless white gown; so he made a drink that was perfectly white (gin, white crème de cacao, cream). It was the first cream cocktail to become broadly popular. The people who knew the rules laughed: a 1930 drinks guide called it a drink for "tender young things" just off stick candy, and in 1934 *Esquire* put it second among the ten worst cocktails of the year. It didn't go away. Oxford ALEXANDER COCKTAIL (pdf 92–93), primary. Passes because the drink was made the colour of a feeling for the people it was for (1, 4), was mocked in print by the knowing (2), and outlasted the mockery for decades (3). Weak on (1): nothing says Alexander was untrained; the "wrong way" is cream in a cocktail when that was new. Guards: tell the origin as the best-supported story, never as fact; no year; Phoebe Snow is fictional, say so; *Days of Wine and Roses* never; "stick candy" is the sneer, never our word for the guest's drink; Oxford says its popularity finally faded with the 21st-century revival, so never "still loved today"; the brandy version is MacElhone's later change, not Alexander's; not "the first cream cocktail" (the Only William used cream in the 1890s): "the first to become popular". Allergens: cream (dairy), nutmeg on the brandy version (nuts): Tomás's call. Added after Wren's review: no white-as-purity framing; and the Only William is the Explorer family's person, so the "not the first cream cocktail" fact stays in the dossier, unnamed.

## innocent-explorer · The Free Spirit
- **Wren's test:** finding out made it more wonderful, not less, or the honest answer is "nobody knows" and can stay open; if a person, someone who met the strange thing with questions, not conquest or profit; trust rewarded. Out: conquest and the spice trade, hardship, debunking as the climax, name-etymology mysteries (Four Shares), absinthe hallucinations.
- **Lead:** *Baudoinia compniacensis*, the black fungus on cask-warehouse walls. Antonin Baudoin first identified the black growth in the 1870s (as *Torula compniacensis*); in the 1990s it turned out not to be a *Torula* at all, its only known relative a fungus that grows in Antarctica. It feeds on the spirit that evaporates from ageing barrels, but it's millions of years old and spirits warehouses aren't, so it must live on something else too, and nobody can predict where it will grow. Oxford BAUDOINIA COMPNIACENSIS (pdf 238), ANGEL'S SHARE (pdf 110, "on the roofs and walls of the cellars"), primary; Adam Rogers, *Proof* (2014), Oxford's source, not in the library. Passes because every answer made it stranger (soot, then alive, then a stranger to its own family with an Antarctic cousin) (1); the open questions are on the page ("it is believed", "no simple formula") (1); Baudoin looked and named it instead of scrubbing it off (2). Guards: Oxford says "the 1870s": no exact year unless the room verifies one; keep Oxford's "related only to"; the 2013 Louisville lawsuit and "unsightly" never; never "angels' share" (see family guards); that it improves spirits is what "some distillers believe": label it; never "it's just a mould".
- **Backup:** Sir John Herschel and the blue glow in quinine (1845). A clear, colourless quinine solution in sunlight showed a sky-blue shimmer at its surface; Herschel reported it as a case of colour on the surface of a liquid colourless inside, and in 1852 George Stokes showed that invisible ultraviolet light was coming back out as blue, and named it fluorescence. That's why tonic water glows under a black light. *Liquid Intelligence* p. 364 (quinine "fluoresces intensely under UV or black lights"), craft/science, read on the page; Herschel, *Philosophical Transactions* 1845, "On a case of superficial colour presented by a homogeneous liquid internally colourless" (primary; title confirmed by search, text not yet read on the page); Stokes 1852; secondary: Renz, *Cytometry A* (2013), FSU Molecular Expressions timeline. Passes (1) the answer (light changing colour) is more wonderful than the mystery, (2) a curious man with a glass of medicine in the sun. Guards: one search summary calls him "Frederik William Herschel": wrong man (his father, who died in 1822); it's John: confirm on the paper; quinine's malaria-and-empire history stays out (conquest, medicine); card `dave-arnold-quinine.md` is a caregiver-sage candidate: none of its Arnold facts here; never "tonic cures".
- **Wren:** **accept.** It passes the clause that matters most: every answer made it stranger. Soot, then alive, then not the thing it was named, with its only known relative in Antarctica, and still nobody can say what it lived on before there were barrels. The label keeps failing, and that is this guest's delight exactly. Conditions: end on the open question, never on the explanation; no "mould", no "unsightly"; Antarctica in one plain clause, with no ice, expedition or survival words (the Explorer family's Shackleton ground); "improves the spirit" only as what some distillers believe; never "angels' share" (it's the Lover family's Angel's Share now, as well as a sibling's name); the image brief makes the black walls look like a wonder, not damp.
- **Drink direction (Tomás):** Martini · cognac · no named bottle (a VSOP-style cognac: the story needs a spirit that has spent years in a barrel) · a tulip-shaped glass, chilled. The fungus carries Cognac's name (*compniacensis*; my knowledge, unsourced: the room confirms it on a page) and lives on the spirit that leaves ageing barrels, so the base is a barrel-aged brandy from the place it was named for. Stirred and served in a tulip because the tulip narrows over the drink and holds the scent that rises off it: the same escaping spirit that feeds the black walls, kept for the guest instead of lost. That's physics, not a bridge. A stirred drink (cognac with a fortified wine or aperitif; the spark is the room's) stays clear and still.
