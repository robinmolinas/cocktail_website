## jester-caregiver · The Morale Booster
- **Wren's test:** someone who deliberately made joy for a gloomy group (a crowd, a town, a bar in a bad time), put work and planning into it, and a cost or a time it didn't work is on record. Out: cures and medicine, one person, the front line and the taxi, a lone joke, a hangover.
- **Lead:** Clara Bell Walsh's Sunday cocktail party, St. Louis, 1917. Oxford Companion, COCKTAIL PARTY (pdf 512), primary (Eric Felten, citing the *Wichita Beacon*, April 3, 1917). The first party "so conceived and so called" was hers: Mrs. Julius S. Walsh Jr., a socialite, "responsible for the innovation". She "had tried out other party themes" first, and one of them, guests dressed as babies drinking from bottles, "wasn't quite the enduring success". About fifty guests came to the Walsh mansion on a Sunday; cocktails ran from noon to 1 p.m., then dinner. She served the drinks of the moment (Bronx, Clover Leaf), highballs, fizzes, Martinis and Manhattans, and a few Sazeracs "for the hide-bound oldsters". She lived half of each year at New York's Plaza. The *New York Times* sneered at the whole idea in 1922 ("a gathering of persons who can have a 'good time' only when highly stimulated by strong waters"). **Passes because** it's a room of fifty, made on purpose in a dead slot (a Sunday midday), after a flop that's on the record, and the idea outlived the sneer. **Guards:** "first" only in Oxford's scope ("so conceived and so called"); the 1903 and 1906 "cocktail parties" Oxford sets aside both ended in deaths: never mention them. Alec Waugh's London claim is apocryphal (Oxford): out. The gloom is our reading, and no motive is on record: "I like to think". The church-goers arriving and the baby party "at the St. Louis Country Club" are web-only (VinePair, Mashed; the *Post-Dispatch* 2017 piece is paywalled, unread): not anchors. Her later divorce and "breaking social taboos" (web) stay out. The Juleps on her menu: the reading names no julep (Lover's).
- **Backup:** the Capillaire Club, Edinburgh, and its minuet. Oxford Companion, CAPILLAIRE (pdf 407), primary: during the Scottish Enlightenment the Capillaire Club "mocked local intellectual societies, cheekily promoting the syrup's recreational consumption" while the *Capillaire Minuet* played at its "elegant, often bawdy" parties. Web, secondary (search result only; Jack Campin's Edinburgh music pages timed out, **fetch before citing**): founded by Thomas Erskine, 6th Earl of Kellie (1732–1781), a composer who wrote the minuet; the rule was that capillaire, a syrup, was the only "wine" allowed. **Passes partly:** a planned, invented tradition (its own tune, one absurd rule) to lighten a solemn city's clubs. **Fails** the cost or flop limb: none on record. **Guards:** "bawdy" stays out; the founder and the rule are web-only until the page is read; capillaire was once a pharmacists' tonic (pdf 407): tell it as a party syrup, never as medicine; the Earl's drinking legends are unsourced.
- **Wren:** accept. The planning and the flop are on the page, and they are this person: she tried other themes first, the baby party didn't last, and the one that did is still how people gather. The position grows straight out of it: the night that didn't lift still counted, because it's how the next one came. The "heavy room" limb is thin (a Sunday noon isn't gloom on record), so the reading signposts it once and rests its weight on the work and the flop, not on sadness. Guards I add: keep it apart from the Geisha (*Hoping You'd Come* owns the getting-ready and the fear of the empty room), so no preparations, no waiting for guests, no empty room: this is fifty people's mood, not one guest's welcome. The mansion and the Plaza stay out; wealth is never the point. The Sazeracs "for the hide-bound oldsters" are a gift: she made sure the hardest guests had something too. Laugh with the baby party, never at her.
- **Drink direction (Tomás):** Martini (Oxford calls the Bronx "essentially a Perfect Martini with added orange juice", pdf 358) · London dry gin · no named bottle; sweet and dry vermouth, fresh orange juice · nick-and-nora, chilled. From the story because the Bronx is the first drink on her own menu, one of the drinks "of the moment" (Oxford pdf 512): she poured what fifty guests wanted that season, so nothing bridges. **Watch:** the Clover Leaf on the same menu has egg white (a veto) and the juleps are Lover's, so neither. The Sazeracs "for the hide-bound oldsters" are Wren's gift and a possible spark for the room (Peychaud's is free, Hero left it), but no absinthe louche (*Rosetta*). The glass keeps it off *Quite Alive* (Martini · London dry · coupe), Magician's Siren (frozen gin, small frozen cocktail glass) and Hero's Vesper fallback to a cocktail glass. Veto-free.
- **Room (2026-09-30):** kept the lead (Walsh). The drink moved off the reserved Martini · London dry · nick-and-nora (Innocent's Little Prince holds it) to a Bronx riff · London dry · stemmed wine glass, shaken. Coriander is now claimed as this pour's spark.

## jester-creator · The Wacky One
- **Wren's test:** someone who made something deliberately absurd, owned it fully and publicly, kept at it, and it caught on or became theirs. On purpose (not a mistake, a trick or a hidden oddness revealed). Something pushed back, and they kept going.
- **Lead:** Harry McElhone and the International Bar Flies. Oxford Companion, MCELHONE, HENRY "HARRY" (pdf 1271–1272) and HARRY'S NEW YORK BAR (pdf 972–973), primary (Wondrich; Monti). A Dundee boy who left school at fourteen; his "real importance" was branding through "publicity stunts and paper ephemera": "self-deprecating postcards", "jocular items such as lapel tags requesting that intoxicated patrons be returned to the bar" and ads spelling his address "Sank Roo Doe Noo" (Oxford says so an American could get there by taxi; our reading says only "so anyone could say it"). "Perhaps his greatest stunt" was the IBF, "an organization for serious drinkers", founded in 1924 with the columnist O. O. McIntyre: by 1927 over five thousand members, with branches called "traps" all over the world. His team spread "fantastic stories" of patrons arriving with lions and panthers; the bar still runs a straw poll for every US election. In 1940 he locked his papers in a safe, buried it and fled; after the war he came back and "picked up where he left off". His family still runs Harry's. **Passes because** it's absurd on purpose (a mock society whose branches are traps), owned under his own name, kept up for life, and it caught on by the thousand and is still his. **Guards:** the push-back is the war that took his bar, and that's our reading: nobody on record told him to "be serious". The drinks Harry's claims (Bloody Mary, Sidecar, White Lady, French 75) are "mostly bogus" and came after his death: never. The Scofflaw mocks Prohibition (Outlaw ground) and the Monkey Gland is named for a medical graft: both out. The lions are told as his stories, never as fact. Hemingway's "twenty Whisky Sours" is "said to": out. The Café de Paris "large loss of life": one plain clause at most, only if Wren wants it. Marketing words ("branding", "stunt") stay out of the guest's text.
- **Backup:** Charles Godfrey Leland coins "mixologist". *Imbibe!*, pdf 53, and Oxford Companion, MIXOLOGY (pdf 1334), primary. In an 1856 *Knickerbocker* humor piece a character calls a hotel bartender "a mixologist of tipulars"; readers "began adopting it, at first in jest"; by 1870 a Nevada saloon was advertising its "skilful mixologists" in earnest. Rival coinages ("toddydriver", "cocktail architect", "drinkist") sank. **Passes partly:** an invented, silly word that everyone ended up using, the persona's own "invent a word". **Fails** "kept at it" and "pushed back": neither is on the page (bartenders' modern dislike of the word is our knowledge, unsourced). **Guards:** Charles G. Leland the humorist is not Charles Leland who owned the Metropolitan Hotel where Jerry Thomas worked (*Imbibe!* pdf 34): never merge them; Jerry Thomas stays out.
- **Wren:** accept. A mock society "for serious drinkers", branches called traps, an address spelled out so any American could say it: nonsense on purpose, under his own name, for life, and it caught on by the thousand. That's the invented word everyone ends up using. The push-back limb isn't on the page, and the room must not invent a "be serious". What is on the page will do, told plainly: the war made everything serious, he buried his papers and left, and after it he came back and picked up where he left off. That's refusing to be made normal. Guards I add: the Café de Paris stays out entirely (deaths, and it wasn't his bar). The drunk-patron lapel tag is not the joke the reading leans on; the traps and the spelling are. No taxi (*Worth the Trip*'s epigraph owns it): say he spelled it so anyone could say it. "Stunt", "branding" and "publicity" never in guest text.
- **Hester (after Wren):** agreed, all guards taken. The Café de Paris and the taxi are struck from the row's usable facts; the push-back is only what's on the page (the war, the buried papers, the return).
- **Drink direction (Tomás):** Daiquiri (a sour) · blended scotch and Irish whiskey, split · no brand; lemon and orgeat · coupe, chilled. This is Cameron's Kick, which "first appeared in Harry McElhone's 1922 ABC of Mixing Cocktails" (Oxford A FORGOTTEN CLASSIC, pdf 829). From the story because it's in the vest-pocket book the lead itself names among his paper jokes, and it's a mash-up only a Dundee man running Paris's American bar would print: two countries' whiskies in one sour. Say "first appeared in his book", never "his invention". **Veto flag (avoidable):** orgeat is `nuts`, and the IBF story doesn't need it. The veto-free fallback is the Boulevardier (Martini · bourbon · coupe), printed in his 1927 *Barflies and Cocktails* (Codex p. 90), the book that carries the society's name; but it's credited to Erskine Gwynne, and its Campari and sweet vermouth are Explorer's Spiritualist bottles (and Lover's Flirt has Campari): owned in one clause if the room takes it. **For Hester:** Oxford disagrees with itself on the White Lady (MCELHONE pdf 1271 gives him "an early version"; WHITE LADY pdf 2184: "reputedly", brandy, crème de menthe and Cointreau in the 1922 *ABC*). Conflicting sources, so the plan's "never" stands. The Monkey Gland and Scofflaw stay out.
- **Hester (jester-creator room, r3):** struck my own "a Dundee man running Paris's American bar": in 1922 he was "Harry of Ciro's" (London and Deauville); he bought the Paris bar in 1923 (Oxford pdf 1272, 462). Also on the page now: the IBF's fly emblem (1927 book, p. 94) and the chain behind "with McIntyre" (card `harry-mcelhone-international-bar-flies.md`, C1).

## jester-regular-guy · The Stand-up
- **Wren's test:** an ordinary, everyday drink or habit that everyone shared and nobody thought worth recording, until someone named it and people recognised themselves. Small and common; the person in it is one of the crowd. Out: fame, the big stage, mocking anyone.
- **Lead:** the Boilermaker. Oxford Companion, BOILERMAKER (pdf 306–307), primary (Wondrich). It "seems too simple and straightforward… to have a history", yet it came late: Americans chased whisky with iced water and thought beer "an outlandish substitute" (an 1846 New Englander found whisky and beer together in Scotland remarkable). In 1896 the *New York Herald* ran a story about a man who ordered "beer n' whisky" side by side, and the consternation it caused the bartender and regulars: "I've heard of queer drinks in my time." Mixed versions were named Bohemian (1896), Rough Rider (1904) and Puddler's (1915, Pittsburgh). The unmixed pair got a name that stuck only in the 1930s: "Boilermaker and His Helper", a skilled industrial metalworker and the extra muscle beside him, shortened by the 1940s; in 1948 Billy Rose still had to explain it. "Primarily a working-class drink", it's now back on menus as pairings. **Passes because** it's the plainest order in the bar, with no inventor, named by working people after their own jobs; the man of 1896 could be anyone, and the joke lands on the regulars, not on him. **Guards:** beer means `gluten` (Tomás). The "Depth Charge", the "bomb" shots and the Irish Car Bomb stay out. Bob Dylan's shooters (celebrity) out. Nobody is named as coining "Boilermaker and His Helper": no namer. "As immigrants assimilated" is Oxford's reasoning: say "as habits changed". The "Helper" is this row's; the Straight Man row doesn't use it.
- **Backup:** the Cock-Tail's first decades on record are jokes about ordinary drinkers. *Imbibe!*, pdf 170 and 172, primary. In 1813 the *Tickler*, a Philadelphia humor paper, printed "a comic account" of two ordinary men arguing "about the superior virtues of gin-sling and cock-tail"; an 1818 verse ad by a Natick merchant was reprinted for its humour; and one of only four early mentions of an iced cocktail is an 1840 dialect sketch in which an overheated everyman, Sorney Sapstalk, orders "a brandy cocktail, with a lump of ice in 'em". **Passes because** a common drink was recorded mostly as everyday comedy, and readers recognised themselves. **Guards:** the writers are anonymous, so the mirror is the drinker in the joke, not a person; Croswell's 1806 item (pdf 169) is the Scandalmonger's and stays out; the 1816 "duality of souls" morning drinking stays out (health); "mooks" is Wondrich's word.
- **Wren:** accept. The plainest order in the bar, no inventor, and it got its name when working people looked at it and saw themselves in it. That's the Stand-up exactly: someone watched closely enough, and fondly enough, to name the ordinary, and everyone recognised it. The 1896 regulars' "queer drinks" is good texture as long as the joke stays on the regulars. Guards I add: "and His Helper" is only the name, never the reading's insight (the steady one beside the skilled one is the Straight Man's shape), and its "His" is the old phrase, never about the guest. No class label on the guest: the belonging is in the naming. The drink must sound worth having (a renowned pairing, back on menus), never "a shot and a beer". Beer costs a veto-free row: Tomás weighs the floor.
- **Drink direction (Tomás):** Highball, the nearest root (a spirit with a long, fizzy partner), but served side by side and never mixed, so `freeform` · straight rye whiskey (the page names no whiskey; rye is my call, to keep it off the Straight Man's bourbon) · no brand; a lager (a style) · a small glass for the whiskey beside a beer glass. From the story because the drink is the story: the 1896 "beer n' whisky", side by side, named by working people after their jobs (Oxford pdf 306–307). Nothing bridges, and the spark is the pairing (which beer beside which whiskey, from the *Matrix*), never a dropped shot or a "bomb". **Veto flag (unavoidable):** beer is `gluten`, and the story is a beer. A naturally gluten-free beer would keep the veto but lose the plainest order in the bar, so I wouldn't. The family's other ten rows carry the floor (see Claims).
- **Room (2026-10-01):** kept the Boilermaker; the mirror is the 1896 order (Oxford) plus Spring's 1933 Yonkers column. Apricot-steeped straight rye beside a German-style pilsner · freeform · small stemmed glass + pilsner glass. Claims the Spring column and apricot-in-rye.

## jester-explorer · The Alternative Comic
- **Wren's test:** someone who broke a drink's expected form or ritual on purpose, knowing it might puzzle; it did puzzle before it worked (or failed, and they tried again differently). The risk is real and the aim a real reaction. Out: shock for its own sake, hoaxes and invented history, novelty for marketing.
- **Lead:** the Aviary, Chicago (2011): Grant Achatz, Craig Schoettler, then Charles Joly. *A Proper Drink*, pp. 234–235, reliable secondary, and Oxford Companion, COCKTAIL RENAISSANCE (pdf 522), primary. Achatz had the idea in 2008; he and Schoettler, a cook with no bartending experience who learned the classics "from the ground up", workshopped for years before opening in spring 2011: "We wanted to blend technique modernity with tradition in a way we thought hadn't been done yet." There was no bar to lean on; drinks came "like science projects". In the Rock was an Old-Fashioned sealed in a partly frozen ice orb that you cracked with a small sling, so "what was once in the rock was now on the rocks". Chicago's cocktail crowd "received Aviary rather coolly at first" (Joly: "You need to sit through the experience to understand it… it lacked a sense of hospitality"). With Joly the approach turned "180 degrees": "The drinks were just as bizarre, but now they came with a bartender joke and a chat." **Passes because** the form was broken on purpose (no bar, a drink you break open), the puzzled reception is on record, and the second try kept every bit of the strangeness and added the explanation and the laugh, which is Wren's position ("tell them afterwards what you were trying"). **Guards:** never "molecular" (the chapter's own title rejects it). Achatz's illness: never. The custom glassware, the sling and the frozen spheres are niche kit the rulebook bars at home: the drink echoes the idea (something that opens, a joke that explains), never the gadget. "Expensive" (Oxford) out. Joly's "lacked hospitality" is a criticism: tell it gently, as the room's first reaction.
- **Backup:** Eben Freeman at Tailor, New York (2007–09). *A Proper Drink*, pp. 230–233, reliable secondary. He set out "to adapt the language of the kitchen" to the bar; at Tailor came a bubble-gum vodka Bazooka, absinthe gummy bears, and the Waylon, a whiskey and cola whose cola was smoked over cherry and alder wood and served from a soda gun. Alex Day: some paths "don't necessarily result in the result he wanted. But the path down there is just as important." Eben Klemm: "the master of spectacular failures." **Passes because** he broke the form (the cocktail as a kitchen, a soda gun for a craft drink), risked and sometimes failed, and kept trying. **Guards:** WD-50's fat-washing and the bacon bourbon are Explorer's Trailblazer ground ("never served", Lee did): out, along with Don Lee; "Tesla to a group of more savvy Edisons" is Simonson's metaphor; the profane quote (p. 234) out; Tailor's closing (2009) told gently; Sam Mason named at most once.
- **Wren:** accept. The form broken on purpose (no bar, a drink you crack open), the cool first reception on record, and a second try that kept every bit of the strangeness and added the explanation and the laugh. My position, found in the record. Guards I add: the reading is about the audience's reaction, never about experimenting on ingredients (*Off-Label* owns that). The fix must never read as "they got normal": Joly's own words say the drinks stayed just as bizarre. The room picks one mirror (Schoettler learning the classics from scratch, or the Aviary as a whole), not three names. No fine-dining glamour or price: the guest must see a risk, not a luxury.
