## hero-caregiver · The Saviour
- **Wren's test:** a person gave up something real of their own (money, credit, comfort, a place, a name) so that a specific other person was spared. It visibly costs them. Best if they also refuse help, or can't bear to owe. Out: crisis rescue in danger, snow or mountain rescue, hot drinks for a helper, dying for it, anything medical.
- **Lead: Barney Gallant takes the plea (1919)** (Wren's replacement). *Joy of Mixology* pdf 59 (Regan, relaying Stanley Walker's *The Night Club Era*, 1933), library; re-opened on the page by Hester and by Wren. Under the wartime Prohibition Act, less than four months after it took effect (30 June 1919), Gallant's Greenwich Village Inn was raided and he was arrested along with half a dozen of his waiters; "he agreed to plead guilty providing the waiters were set free". Sentenced to thirty days, "he served far less"; about a month before national Prohibition began he opened Club Gallant. **Passes because** he took the cost *in their place*, for specific people (his waiters), and the cost is visible on his own record, in ordinary working life with no rush and no danger. **Guards:** never "served thirty days" (the sentence, not the time served); "the first person in Manhattan to be jailed under Prohibition laws" is Walker's claim via Regan, scoped to Manhattan and to wartime Prohibition: attribute it or drop it; the not-owing half of Wren's test isn't on the page, so it lives only in whoYouAre and the position, never as a claim about him; motive not on the page ("I like to think" or nothing); the raid is the setting, owned in one clause, never glamour; Club Gallant's house rules (the cigarette girl, the cloakroom girl) are gendered: out; no Outlaw plan exists yet, so this row is Prohibition's only use in the family; library gap if the room wants the primary: Walker (1933).

## hero-creator · The Idealist
- **Wren's test:** a person whose made thing (a drink, a book, a label, a song, a menu) carried a belief well beyond them; they refused to be reduced to it, at the cost of disappointing people who wanted it kept the same; the belief is one any guest can respect (fairness or welcome). Out: aggression, protest turned violent, plain quality stories.
- **Lead: G. Selmer "Baron" Fougner (1884–1941) and *Along the Wine Trail*.** Oxford, FOUGNER, G. SELMER (pdf 831, 833–834), primary. He wrote the *New York Sun*'s daily wine and spirits column from 20 October 1933 until his death. Before him, wine writing tended to be "high-toned and mystifying" and cocktail writing "jocular and even aggressively vulgar", and "Fougner was neither". He didn't condescend to people who drank cocktails or beer, or assume imports were better. He answered readers' questions "with patience, no matter how stupid", and when he didn't know an answer he threw the question open to his readers, many of them old bartenders, whose testimony still lights up the history of famous drinks. Wondrich: he "essentially created modern drinks writing". **Passes because** the made thing, a daily column, carried a welcome (no question too small) well beyond him, and it let a whole crowd say what it knew together, which is Wren's "gives". He refused both house styles of his day. **Gap:** Wren's second clause (refusing to be reduced to the thing he made, at the cost of disappointing people) isn't on the page. The nearest is that refusal of both styles. **Guards:** Beebe's praise ("arch-foe of pretentiousness") is paraphrased, never attributed (Beebe is Lover's). The drinks his readers illuminated stay out (the Daiquiri is the Problem Solver's; the Rob Roy is *A Brother's Care*'s). "Baron" was a nickname, not a title. "Created modern drinks writing" is Wondrich's judgement and must be attributed.
- **Backup: Robert Hess and Drinkboy.** *A Proper Drink*, pp. 66–68 (reliable secondary). A Microsoft executive who taught himself drinks, he became a "cocktail missionary" ("I was an evangelist at Microsoft… I wanted to try and find those bartenders who had the spark"). He launched Drinkboy in early 1998, and its chat room became "an agora" for the revival's future leaders. His Trident (2002) is one of the few modern classics made by someone who has "never been a bartender", built to bring obscure ingredients back. **Passes partly:** made things carrying a welcome ("you didn't feel so geeky", Winchester). It fails the refusal clause. **Guards:** Neyah White's "arrogance" remark is on the same page, and the room decides whether to grant it. He married Audrey Saunders (*Making the Calls*' person): she stays out. His habit of testing bartenders (p. 69) isn't kindness. Living: date him.
- **Also considered (second draft, not re-opened by me):** the Browns' sealed bottle (Old Forester, 1870; Oxford OLD FORESTER pdf 1430, BROWN-FORMAN pdf 365), sold only sealed by its maker when sellers cut whisky, and never any other way. It may pass the refusal clause better than Fougner or Hess. Thad Vogler (*Proper* pp. 176–179), whom Innocent left free.
- **Wren:** **accept Fougner.** The missing refusal clause is carried by what *is* on the page: he refused both house styles of his day (the mystifying and the vulgar), in print, every day. And the persona's own fear, going quiet when it matters, is answered by the record: a daily column from 1933 until he died. The made thing lets a crowd say what it knows together (the readers' answers), which is exactly the Idealist's gift. The reading must not claim a cost he paid or a crowd he disappointed. The belief is welcome ("no question too stupid"), which any guest can respect. Guard: the reading is about making instead of arguing, so keep it off the Campaigner's "voice that goes first". The Old Forester sealed bottle is out: it's a quality-and-honesty story, which my test excludes.
- **Drink direction (Tomás):** Daiquiri (a sour lengthened with soda; the Codex keeps the Tom Collins in its Daiquiri chapter, p. 138) · bourbon, as the reader wrote it (Wondrich and Oxford say rye: the room picks one and says why) · no named bottle · a large round-bowled beer goblet with one or two big cubes, as the reader specified. It's the Ward Eight *as a reader sent it to Fougner's column in 1934*, when he asked readers for its history (*Imbibe!* pdf 243–244, printed with his column as the source). The recipe exists because the column asked and a reader answered, so the drink is his made thing working, not a bridge. Home-made pomegranate grenadine is the column's own belief in the glass (in 1936 he wrote of "innumerable products masquerading" under the name; Oxford GRENADINE pdf 947): a spark the room may take. Fresh mint rather than crème de menthe, which the reader himself called "an improvement". Guards: the Ward Eight's origin is legend and its name is a political boss's ward (Lomasney, who didn't drink), so the reading tells the reader's letter, not the election. *Not Too Polite*'s room set it aside as a runner-up and never used it. Veto-free.

## hero-regular-guy · The Firefighter
- **Wren's test:** a team practised something unglamorous, over and over, for a moment that might never come; the moment came and the practice held; afterwards they gave the credit away; the practice itself is visible in the source. Out: solo geniuses, danger as spectacle, medicine, real fires, 9/11, uniforms as labels.
- **Lead: Phil Ward, the Flatiron Lounge barback.** *A Proper Drink*, p. 152 (reliable secondary; Julie Reiner and Ward on the record). He answered a Craigslist call for a barback and "had no idea what he was walking into" (he was still drinking Long Island Iced Teas). He "set himself to learning the drinks". Reiner wouldn't make him a bartender: too rough around the edges, "Dirty Phil". But "He was the best barback ever… He'd hear the order and every glass was lined up behind me. And he'd hand me the next bottle I'd need." When she hired someone else to bartend, Phil stood behind the new man and fed him the specs ("half, three-quarter, dash, dash") from "this crazy memory", until "I couldn't not make him a bartender." **Passes because** the drill is on the page (every spec learned, every glass lined up), done as the crew's quiet half; the moment (each rush) came and it held; and the credit went to the man in front of him. **Partial:** the moment came nightly rather than once. **Guards:** Reiner speaks in this story, so Explorer's backup row (jiggering at Flatiron) and this one must not both run. - **Backup: the TGI Fridays drill.** *A Proper Drink*, pp. 17–20 (reliable secondary). Trainees learned four hundred drinks and passed a test with twenty-five made blindfolded, in a set time, "through muscle memory". Barbacks weren't allowed to look at or talk to guests. It took nine months to become a bartender. "Magic" Mike Werner wrote a training manual in his spare time, was made a trainer, and set up the UK programmes; later the training was cut from nine months to six weeks. By 2000 the chains' name "was mud" with young bartenders, yet London's revival hired Fridays alumni because they were "clean and efficient", and "the veterans and insiders knew their training systems were solid". **Passes because** the drill is visible, done as a crew, and it held when the craft revival came, uncredited. **Fails** "gave the credit away" (the credit was withheld, not given). **Guards:** the "clean and efficient" line is Bradsell's (Explorer's): never name him. - **Also considered (second draft, not re-opened by me):** the Club de Cantineros (1924; Oxford pdf 227, 1323, 1815) for the backup. I read pdf 227 and 1323: training and English lessons are there, the moment and the credit are not, and the Prohibition tourists are the Outlaw family's ground.

## hero-explorer · The Quest Seeker
- **Wren's test:** a person went after one specific, hard-to-get thing (a lost recipe, a vanished bottle, a plant, an answer) and got it; the source shows a fear or a real risk on the way; the story reveals what the prize was really for, and ideally what came next. Out: taking from others without credit, stories where the prize is the whole point.
- **Lead: Eric Seed and his first pallet of Zirbenz.** *A Proper Drink*, pp. 240–241 (reliable secondary). Simonson calls him perhaps "the most mild-mannered person in the cocktail industry… a soft-spoken, slightly built Midwesterner in a world of brassy characters. But there was nothing safe or boring about his business." He had found an Alpine pine liqueur while studying in Austria and thought he could sell it in Colorado's ski towns. He "went to Austria to find an example" and brought back Zirbenz, made for two centuries. He could afford only a pallet of sixty cases; none of the big distributors was interested; a one-man wine outfit carried it; the product struggled. Then he asked a liquor store what they'd carry, which became his method, and his company (Haus Alpenz) went on to bring bartenders the bottles they'd been missing (Allspice Dram, Velvet Falernum). **Passes because** it's one named, hard-to-get thing, with a real risk on the page (all he could afford, no takers) and what came next. Our reading, signposted: the prize was the proof that a quiet person could do it. **Gap:** the risk is on the page, but a fear in his own words is not. **Guards:** LeNell's scolding of Seed (p. 238) belongs to the Rogue row and never appears here. His crème de violette is *As It Was*'s ingredient-story: out. The Old Tom and Wondrich on p. 241 are *Instead*'s: out. "Mild-mannered" is Simonson's judgement. Living: date him.

He entered Chartreuse's yearly competition four years running and usually did well. Asked back for a fifth, he "worried he had run out of ideas", went tropical, used falernum as his "secret weapon", and won. The drink didn't sell at first and took off only after the chef Michael Mina mentioned it. **Passes because** the fear is on the record, with the prize and the slow success after it. **Guards:** **his surname reads as a joke on the app's name** (Innocent's plan flagged it first). ## hero-sage · The Problem Solver
- **Wren's test:** a person solved a practical problem under real time pressure, with people waiting and watching; with what was at hand (a shortage, a broken plan, a missing ingredient); the source shows the clock and the audience; best if it also shows their pleasure. Out: slow research, pure luck, "invented by accident" myths.
- **Lead: Jennings Cox and the first Daiquiri (1896).** Oxford, DAIQUIRI (pdf 621, 623), primary (Oxford's own report of the later accounts). Cox ran the Spanish-American Iron Company near Daiquirí. His step-granddaughter Carmen Puig, telling the story to Linda Wolfe in 1970, said he improvised the drink for some American visitors after discovering he was out of gin. He had Bacardi Carta Blanca but "feared that it would not be to the taste of the Americans", so he picked limes from a tree in his garden, added juice and sugar, and swizzled it in a pitcher with shaved ice.
