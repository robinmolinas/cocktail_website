# Pour — Good for Years · The Mechanic (regular-guy-magician)

## Cocktail

- **name:** Good for Years
- **tagline:** Everyone says get a new one. You've already got the back off.
- **glassware:** small tumbler, one large cube
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 ml | unaged (joven) mezcal | any joven mezcal from 40% to 50%; an espadín is the easiest to find |
| 1½ teaspoons (7.5 ml, about 10 g) | orange marmalade | an ordinary one, not reduced-sugar: the sugar and a bitter edge in one spoon |
| to fill a jar two-thirds | ice cubes | for shaking |
| 1 | large ice cube | in the glass |

**method**
1. Take a clean jar with a screw lid (about 350 to 500 ml). A marmalade jar that's nearly finished is best: leave about 1½ teaspoons in it and scrape the sides down. Otherwise, spoon the marmalade into any clean jar.
2. Pour in the mezcal. Stir with the spoon for about 20 seconds, scraping the bottom, until the marmalade has loosened into the spirit.
3. Fill the jar two-thirds with ice. Screw the lid on tight and shake hard for about 10 seconds, until the jar is too cold to hold comfortably.
4. Put the large cube in a small tumbler. Loosen the lid a quarter turn, hold it in place with your thumb and strain it through the gap: the lid holds the ice back. A few shreds of peel will come through. Leave them in.

**closingLine:** *Strain it through the gap in the lid. Then leave the jar by the sink, and enjoy a room where nothing's broken.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*No shaker, no strainer, no syrup. Just the last of a jar.*

**whoYouAre**
Someone's lamp has died, or the zip's gone on a coat, and they're already saying it's had its day. You've got the back off before they've finished the sentence. A hair grip, a folded bit of card, tape from the kitchen drawer: you use whatever's there, and it works. It looks a bit odd. It lasts for years.

Quieter, though, is this. At a party where nothing's broken, you're not quite sure where to stand, and a dripping tap in the kitchen feels like being let in. What gets to you isn't something breaking. It's the shrug, and "just get a new one", because a world that throws things away has less room for you in it. So it deserves saying plainly: when you mend something, people keep their own thing, the bike they learned on or a grandparent's lamp, and the piece of their life that comes with it. And you show them what was wrong, so the impossible turns out to be ordinary.

**yours**

1. In Hawaii, people had long known how to bring the sweetness out of the ti plant, a relative of the agave: they roasted its big stems underground for days. Then, before 1800, distilling arrived, and the man usually credited with it is William Stevenson. His still was a makeshift: iron pots off the ships, with a gun barrel to carry the vapour.

2. It caught on. One history from 1891 says that at one time almost every chief had a still. And the contraption left its mark on the name. The spirit is called okolehao, which is Hawaiian: *okole* means bottom, and *hao* means iron. The pots were said to look like one, and the nickname stuck. Distillers on the islands make it from ti root again today.

3. I like to think that's the best ending a makeshift can have: people copied it, made a joke of its looks, and named the whole thing after it. Your repairs go the same way. All over the place, things are still working because of you, held together with something they were never meant to have, and someone smiles at the tape every time they use them. They're smiling because the thing is still theirs.

4. The cocktail I've made for you isn't okolehao. A true one is hard to find outside Hawaii, and not every bottle with that name on it has been made from ti root. So I made do, as a nod to that still. It's a mezcal Old-Fashioned, the cocktail at its most classic: a spirit, a little sugar, something bitter. Mezcal's agave is traditionally roasted underground for days, just as the ti was. There's no syrup and no bottle of bitters: a spoonful of orange marmalade does both jobs, sweet from the sugar and bitter from the peel. And it's best shaken in the marmalade jar itself, the nearly finished one that was on its way to the recycling, then poured through the gap under the loosened lid. The jar is the shaker and the strainer. It comes out soft orange and a little smoky, with a bitter edge and a few shreds of peel.

5. So, when you're next asked round and nothing's broken, leave the dripping tap alone. Stay in the room with everyone else; the tap can wait. They didn't invite you for what you can mend. They invited you because they like having you there. And for once, you can enjoy the evening too.

**Conflicts** (fact card)

- **C1 Did Hawaiians brew from ti before distilling?** Oxford pdf 1419: from the roasted ti "they brewed a mild, beer-like intoxicant". Alexander 1891 p. 87: "Neither fermented nor distilled liquors were used by the Hawaiians" (their drink was awa, kava). **So:** the underground roasting is safe (Oxford; Alexander p. 157 describes the baking), the traditional ti brew is not stated.
- **C2 When it came back.** Oxford: "Only since 2012"; Oxford's own bibliography lists Hoover, "Will New 'Okolehao be Your Cup of Ti?", *Honolulu Advertiser*, 2003. No year for the revival.
- **C3 Whaling pots in the 1790s.** "Whaling" rests on Oxford and Rowley; Alexander says only "procured from ships". I haven't checked when whalers reached Hawaii; until someone does, "pots off the ships" is the safe telling, and "whaling" is Oxford's.
- **C5 Who brought distilling.** Aloha State Daily 2026: "likely" introduced in the years after 1778, when British sailors brought distillation "with iron implements". Against Oxford/Alexander's Stevenson. Keeps him at "the man usually credited".
- **C4 The first legal distillery:** Oxford 1907 (Kona Okolehao Distillery) vs Rowley 1906 (E. H. Edwards imported a still). Out of guest text.

### Open items
