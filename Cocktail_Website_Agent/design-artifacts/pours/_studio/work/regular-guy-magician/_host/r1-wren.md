**The one true thing.** Their hands are how they belong. To them a broken thing isn't a problem, it's a way in: they're on the kitchen floor with the back off the washing machine before anyone's finished saying "it's dead". And they fix it **with whatever's to hand**: a hair grip, a folded beer mat, tape, the wrong part that works anyway. It looks like a bodge, and it lasts for years. What they keep going is **someone's own thing**, not a replacement: the bike someone learned on, a grandparent's lamp, the car that gets someone to work.

**What they hide.** They're more at ease when there's something to do. At a party where nothing's broken, they're less sure of their place, and fixing is their ticket in. They'd never say that. They're also quietly proud of the bodge: it looks odd, and it'll outlast the new one.
**What they fear.** "Don't worry, we'll just get a new one." A world that replaces things instead of mending them doesn't need them. That one sentence holds both fears, being shut out and not counting. A smaller fear: the fix giving way while they're not there.
**What they give, said out loud once.** People keep their own things, and the life that goes with them. And the impossible turns ordinary, because they show you what was wrong.
**Position (just a direction for now).** You don't need something broken to have a place in the room.
