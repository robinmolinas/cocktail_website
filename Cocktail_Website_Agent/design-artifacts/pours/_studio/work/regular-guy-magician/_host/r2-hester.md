📜 **Hester:** Wren, I accept okolehao, and the earliest account makes your fourth test stronger. It also takes one claim away. Oxford's entry is from 2021, so I went to the oldest source behind it: Alexander, *A Brief History of the Hawaiian People* (1891), p. 157, read on archive.org. It has the same bodge, "iron pots, procured from ships, with a gun-barrel used as a tube to conduct the vapor." Then: "At one time almost every chief had his still." So the makeshift was copied and kept, on the page. That's your "taken in". Three cautions come with it. (1) They're **chiefs**, not ordinary people, with no date. Nothing on any page says the still itself held up or was used for a long time. What lasted on the record is the **name and the spirit**, so test 3 gets told that way. (2) Even in 1891 Stevenson "is said to have been" the first, so he's "the man usually credited". "Two", "discarded" and "whaling" are Oxford's words only; 1891 says only "from ships". I haven't checked when whalers first reached Hawaii, so the safe line is "pots off the ships". (3) The roasting comes first, as you asked: Oxford pdf 1419, with the baking also described in 1891. But Oxford's traditional ti "beer" is contested: Alexander p. 87 says Hawaiians used "neither fermented nor distilled liquors". So the roasting goes in and the brew stays out. On the name, the word's meaning is fact. The reason is "the story goes": Rowley (2014, cited in Oxford's bibliography) says the pots "were said to" look like a bottom and that the nickname "stuck". Write "the name is Hawaiian", never "the Hawaiians named it". Tomás, two leads. Rowley tasted a 100-proof cane-and-ti okolehao from Island Distillers in Honolulu (2013); whether it's still sold I haven't checked. If there's no bottle, Berry's own stand-in for mid-century okolehao recipes is bourbon or rye, because the 1960s "okolehao" was bourbon with ti extract (Oxford pdf 1421).
