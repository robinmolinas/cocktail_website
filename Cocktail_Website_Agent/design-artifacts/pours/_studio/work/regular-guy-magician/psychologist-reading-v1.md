## whoYouAre

Someone's lamp has died, or the zip's gone on a coat, or the bike chain won't stay on, and they're already saying it's had its day. You've got the back off before they've finished the sentence. A hair grip, a folded bit of card, a strip of tape from the kitchen drawer: you use whatever's there, and it works. It usually looks a bit odd. It usually lasts for years. Somewhere a gate still shuts and a radio still plays because of an afternoon you spent on the floor with it.

You'd never say this part out loud: you're happiest in a room when there's something to do. At a party where nothing's broken, you're not quite sure where to stand, and a dripping tap in the kitchen feels like being let in. What gets to you isn't something breaking. It's someone shrugging, "don't worry, we'll just get a new one", because a world that throws things away has less room for you in it. So it deserves saying plainly: when you mend something, people keep their own thing, the bike they learned on or a grandparent's lamp, and the piece of their life that comes with it. And you always show them what was wrong, so the impossible turns out to be ordinary.

## yours

1. In Hawaii, people had long known how to bring the sweetness out of the ti plant, a relative of the agave: they roasted its big stems underground for days. Then, before 1800, distilling arrived, and the man usually credited with it is William Stevenson. His still was a makeshift: iron pots off the ships, with a gun barrel to carry the vapour.

2. It worked well enough to be copied. One history from 1891 says that at one time almost every chief had a still. And the contraption left its mark on the name. The pots were said to look like a bottom, and the nickname stuck. The spirit is called okolehao, which is Hawaiian: *okole* means bottom, and *hao* means iron. Distillers on the islands still make it from ti root today.

3. I like to think that's the best ending a makeshift can have: people laughed at it, copied it, and named the whole thing after it. Your repairs go the same way. All over the place, things are still working because of you, held together with something they were never meant to have, and someone smiles at the tape every time they use them. They're smiling because the thing is still theirs.

4. The cocktail I've made for you isn't okolehao. A true one, made from ti root, is rare and hard to find outside Hawaii, and not every bottle with that name on it is made from ti. So I made do, as a nod to that still. It's a mezcal Old-Fashioned, the cocktail at its most classic: a spirit, a little sugar, something bitter. Mezcal's agave is roasted underground for days, just as the ti was. There's no syrup and no bottle of bitters: a spoonful of orange marmalade does both jobs, sweet from the sugar and bitter from the peel. And it's shaken in the marmalade jar itself, the nearly finished one that was on its way to the recycling, then poured through the gap under the loosened lid. The jar is the shaker and the strainer. It comes out soft orange and a little smoky, with a bitter edge and a few shreds of peel.

5. So, when you're next asked round and nothing's broken, leave the dripping tap alone. Stay in the room with everyone else; the tap can wait. They didn't invite you for what you can mend. They invited you because they like having you there. And for once, you can enjoy the evening too.
