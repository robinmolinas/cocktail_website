## whoYouAre

"Wait, let me try something." You say it halfway through something you've made a dozen times, and off it goes somewhere new. You start things before you know what they'll be. That isn't a hole in the plan. It's the part you like best: finding out.

Most of what you try doesn't work, and you honestly don't mind. You rarely mention those ones, and there are more of them than anyone guesses. What gets under your skin is the opposite: something works, and everyone wants it exactly like last time. Being asked to repeat yourself feels like a door closing. And because you keep trying anyway, the people around you start trying things too. You've shown them that something that didn't work isn't a disaster. It's just the next thing to change.

## yours

1. There's a bottle behind almost every bar that nobody pours by the glass. It's Angostura, and it began in the 1820s as a medicine: a surgeon in Venezuela made it for soldiers' stomachs. It was never meant for drinks. Bartenders borrowed it anyway, and ever since, most recipes have used it the same way: a few dashes, a seasoning, never the main thing.

2. Then a bartender in Brooklyn, Giuseppe Gonzalez, made a sour (spirit, citrus and sugar, shaken) with an ounce and a half of it. The bitters became the base. The whiskey, just half an ounce, became the accent. The way he tells it, the drink didn't win the competition he made it for, and he was told nobody would drink it. It wasn't an immediate hit. But it spread, and one of the great reference books on drinks now uses it as its example of a recipe that looks absurd on paper and works in the glass.

3. I like to think he wasn't trying to be clever. He just wanted to know what would happen. That's the question you ask about everything, and like him, you'd rather find out than be told. The part I love most: it never settled into one recipe. Bartenders kept making their own versions of it, which is exactly how you'd want something of yours to be treated.

4. So yours keeps his ounce and a half of bitters and changes the rest. His almond syrup is gone. In its place is a syrup of lemongrass, which sits beautifully beside warm spice and citrus. Then lemon, shaken, and poured into a wide, shallow stemmed glass. And his half ounce of whiskey is now yours to choose: any unsweetened spirit you have open. Gin, mezcal, rum, a smoky whisky. Every one works, and every one makes a different drink. It's built so you never have to make it the same way twice.

5. But it didn't win, and he didn't drop it. Somewhere you've got something you left half-tried: a recipe, a plan, an idea you got bored of just as it started working. Go back to it. Not to make it the way it was. To find out what it turns into if you stay a little longer.
