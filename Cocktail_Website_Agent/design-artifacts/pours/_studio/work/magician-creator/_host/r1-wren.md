🪞 **Wren:** Hester, Tomás, this is who the Genie is. The full card is in `work/magician-creator/psychologist-persona-card.md`.

**The one true thing.** The Genie is *summoned*. Their best work is always an answer to someone else's ask, and they hear the wish under the words. A friend asks for "a few words" and gets a speech that makes the room cry. They never give exactly what was asked, because the literal version of a request is almost always the wrong one. The overflow is joy, and it's also how they make sure they didn't mishear.

**What they hide.** Their own wish. In their head, a wish is something you grant, not something you get. The energy and the jokes make them the answer in every room, so nobody turns round and asks them the question, and they never offer it. The secret wish is freedom: one thing made their own way, not on call.

**What they fear.** A wish granted exactly as it was worded, with a result nobody wanted. It's a fear of *mishearing*, not of things running away with them. That one belongs to the Apprentice.

**What they give, said out loud once.** They take a small ask seriously enough to make it wonderful, and that tells the person their wish mattered.

**The siblings.** The Fairy Godparent (*Their Night*) is unasked and unseen, at the back, asking "did I have the right?" The Genie is asked and loud, in the middle of it, asking "did I hear it right?" The Clairvoyant knows before you speak, and the Genie waits to be asked. The Entertainer plays to the crowd, and the Genie works for one wish. The Trickster fears being ignored, and to the Genie attention is only a side effect.

**Traps.**
1. The Craftsman's "properly when nobody noticed". Tell the Campbell Apartment as ten names turned into ten drinks, with the joy, never as rigour.
2. The Apprentice's "it got out of hand".
3. The Muse's "your own work nobody asks about" and "put your name on it". I'd keep the novel out.
4. The Godparent's missing credit. The Genie gets thanked. What's hidden is the wish.
5. Barnum lines like "you love helping".
6. No quoted Disney lines. Aladdin appears at most once, and only if it earns it.

**My story test.**
1. Someone else's request is on the record.
2. It came back as more, and the maker's delight is on the record too.
3. A too-literal reading of the request is on the page, as a near-miss and not a disaster.
4. The maker's own wish is said out loud, ideally as the price of the yes.
5. The one who asked was glad.

These are out: unasked help, runaway results, happy accidents, and a drink made for the staff (*Still Yours*).

**Verdict on the plan's lead: accept Klemm, re-weighted.** I've read *Proper* pp. 134–136 in full.
- Hester, the too-literal near-miss is better than "names alone". It's the maraschino. In Klemm's words, he found out maraschino is a real liqueur, "not just the maraschino juice that bartenders are using in this drink", and he bought the one bottle Astor Wines had. The word on the page, taken literally, gave a drink nobody meant. That's the Genie's fear, acted out and avoided.
- Pico is the centre. He offered to improve the lagging bar *if* he could create original cocktails. That's the wish said out loud as the price of the yes, and his freedom is what fixed their bar. The position is on the page: say yes, and in the same breath say what you'd like in return. It ends on what they gain, not on holding back.
- Keep the Campbell half short and Pico long.
- One weak point. At the Campbell nobody asked warmly: the management picked the names for how they sounded. So the human asks (a party, a speech, a broken heart) go in whoYouAre.
- Hester, please rule on whether "rendered drinkers speechless" can stand. It was the Anne Hester, with its Campari foam.
