## whoYouAre

Someone asks you for a few words at their party, and you come back with a toast that has the room crying and then laughing. Someone calls with a broken heart, and by the end of the night there's a plan, a playlist and a joke they'll repeat for years. You don't do small, and it isn't showing off. You listen for what the person meant under what they said, and you give them that.

What worries you is getting it wrong in the most literal way: doing exactly what was asked and watching it land flat, because the words were never really the wish. So you listen hard, and you give more, just in case. And with all those ideas flying, nobody thinks to turn round and ask what you'd wish for. You don't tell them either. In your head, wishes are something you grant. It gives people something rare, though. When you take a small request seriously enough to make it wonderful, you tell someone their wish mattered, and they remember that longer than whatever you made.

## yours

1. In 1999 a bar opened inside New York's Grand Central Terminal, in what had once been a railroad executive's private office: the Campbell Apartment. The management picked ten drinks from an old hotel bar book, because the names sounded good, and handed them over with no recipes. The bartenders taught themselves the drinks from the ingredients alone, although most customers wanted a light beer anyway. One of them, Eben Klemm, remembered thinking: this is kind of cool.

2. Half the drinks called for orange bitters, and nobody knew where to buy them, so the bartenders mixed Cointreau and Angostura and used that. Another called for maraschino. Klemm found out it was a real liqueur, clear and made from cherries, not the "maraschino juice" bartenders were pouring, which I take to be the syrup from a jar of cocktail cherries. So he went to Astor Wines and bought the one bottle they had. Taken at its word, that recipe would have made a drink nobody meant.

3. Two years later, at Pico, a restaurant in Tribeca with a bar that was lagging, Klemm offered a deal: he'd improve the bar if he could create his own cocktails. The owners agreed. "They were all original," he said later. "I wanted them to relate to the kitchen." I like to think that's the whole secret: when he finally asked for something, it was to do it his own way, and his own way still had their kitchen in mind. You'll build something out of ten names and no recipe, and you'll go and find the real thing rather than hand someone the jar. What you almost never do is what he did at Pico: say yes, and say what you want.

4. The cocktail I've made for you starts from one of those Pico drinks, the Collins d'Electra. All that’s on record of it is gin, vodka and a fresh rosemary syrup he made for it, so everything else is my own, built like a Tom Collins, one of the great tall classics: lemon, a glass full of ice, soda on top, a sprig of rosemary for the scent. And I've added one small thing from the year before, as a nod to the night they made orange bitters out of two bottles they already had: a teaspoon of Cointreau mixed with two dashes of Angostura. It's tall, bright and herbal, with a warm orange edge underneath, and I like to think the rosemary is where you can taste the kitchen.

5. So this is my wish for you. Whoever asks for your help next, say yes the way you always do, and before you start, name the one part you'd like to do entirely your way. It isn't taking. It's usually where the best thing you make for them comes from, and for once, one of the wishes granted will be yours.
