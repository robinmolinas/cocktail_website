| lineage (the column) | With Repeal coming in 1933, the *New York Sun* gave G. Selmer Fougner a series to help readers find their way around legal wine and spirits, which were unfamiliar to them. His daily column began on 20 October 1933 and ran until he died in 1941 (Oxford pdf 833; F4). | He didn't make a speech. He made a thing that came out daily, with his name on it, and he kept it up. | one true thing: they argue in what they make, not across the table; fear: going quiet when it matters (answered by the record, never claimed of him) |
| reveal (neither) | Before him, wine writing tended to be high-toned and mystifying, and cocktail writing jocular and even aggressively vulgar. Oxford: "Fougner was neither." He didn't talk down to people who drank cocktails or beer (pdf 833; F6, F8). | The belief is shown in how the thing is made. Never "he didn't argue": he did, in print, "gently (usually)" (pdf 834; F11, G3). | Leave a mark; the made thing *is* the argument |
| gesture (the patience) | He answered readers' questions patiently, however silly: one asked whether all scotch was made in Australia (pdf 833; F9). Not "every" question. | Nobody gets talked down to: that's what the guest's made things give. The humour is the reader's question, not Fougner's wit (F7). | what they give: nobody talked down to |
| gesture (the open door) | When he didn't know an answer, he put the question to his readers, nationwide. Many of them had first-hand knowledge of how famous drinks began, and the column "seems to have been" especially popular with former bartenders. Their testimony still lights up the history of famous drinks, the Ward Eight among them (pdf 833–834; F10). | The made thing lets other people say what they know, together. It's a door he opened, never a man stuck for an answer (that's the Problem Solver's ground). | what they give: others get words and room to join in |
| lineage (the drink) | In 1934, while Fougner was seeking more about the Ward Eight, a reader sent the *Sun* a recipe: a whisky sour made to fix "certain objectionable features" of it, with bourbon, grenadine, orange bitters or (as "many prefer") half an orange's juice, plain water or seltzer, in a large, heavy, round-bowled beer glass over "a rather large piece of ice". Fresh mint, when there was any, went in place of crème de menthe, bruised and added with the orange slices ("This is an improvement"). Wondrich prints this letter rather than the only pre-Prohibition recipe, then gives his own build with rye (*Imbibe!* pdf 243–244; W1–W5). | The recipe exists because the column asked and someone answered: the made thing still working decades later. Never "the original Ward Eight" (the origin is legend, W8), and never "the books don't print it" (Wondrich prints the letter). | the made thing carried beyond him |
| riff (our glass) | Spec v1: 75 ml bourbon (40–46%), 30 lemon, 30 orange (half an orange), 15 ml grenadine of pomegranate juice and sugar only, short-shaken for about 5 s (*Codex* p. 138's Collins method), over one large piece of ice in a heavy round-bowled beer glass, topped with 45 ml seltzer, then the mint (bruised), orange half-slices, pineapple and a cherry. **The letter's:** bourbon, lemon, orange juice, grenadine, seltzer, the ice, the glass, the mint and the fruit (pdf 243–244). **Ours:** the shake (the reader built it in the glass), the measures (the letter gives none that convert: "three-quarters full" of a whisky glass), a fixed grenadine dose, and the sugar left out. | Every ingredient comes from someone who wrote to the column, or from Fougner. Only the method and the measures are the bartender's. Never "his recipe". | the made thing carried beyond him; brought others in |
| ingredient (sugar) | The reader: "The amount of sugar should be regulated to taste, and likewise the grenadine" (pdf 244). Spec v1 leaves his barspoon out, and the orange and grenadine carry the sweetness (balance.py: with the spoon it's 8.00 g, OUT). | The letter left room for the drinker, so the drinker's choice is on the page. Say "his letter leaves the sugar to taste", never "he said to leave it out". | leaves room for others |
| ingredient (grenadine) | In June 1936, after Repeal, when American commercial grenadine was imitation, Fougner wrote of "innumerable products masquerading under that name" and claimed the reputable French makers used real pomegranate juice. Oxford: it "took some decades to sink in" (pdf 947–948; G2–G4). Grenadine was in the Ward Eight from the start (pdf 2120). He gave no home recipe (G3). | Where he did argue, he argued in the column, and the point landed slowly. No cause claimed. Our syrup is our choice, tied to his line, never "as he made it", never "fakes", never "the argument", never "my own pomegranate syrup" (*Till Spring*). | put the finished thing out; it lands later |
| pairing (Checks only; one clause in y4) | *Flavor Matrix* pdf 200: pomegranate is "mostly fruity" but "also characterized by sharper aromas such as anise and mint". Mint isn't on its pairings list, and the page describes the fruit and its juice, not a syrup. | The reader's 1934 mint and Fougner's 1936 pomegranate share an aroma. Wording: "pomegranate has a note of mint of its own" or "pomegranate's own aromas include mint". Never "hint" or "faint" (strength words the page doesn't give; "hint" was my own r3 shorthand, struck), and never "the Matrix pairs them". | — |
| gesture (the closing line) | The reader adds the mint "with the slices of orange, &c.", after the glass is filled (pdf 244): last. | "Mint in last, the way the reader wrote it" is on the page. "Wrote", not "did". | — |
