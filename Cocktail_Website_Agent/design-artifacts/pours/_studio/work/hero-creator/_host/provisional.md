# Pour — The Long Answer · The Idealist (hero-creator)

## Cocktail

- **name:** The Long Answer
- **tagline:** You rarely win the row. You make the thing people end up quoting.
- **glassware:** a large, heavy, round-bowled beer glass (about 400 ml), with one large piece of ice
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 75 ml (2½ oz) | bourbon (any, 40–46%) | the reader's own choice, where the older recipes say rye |
| 30 ml (1 oz) | fresh lemon juice (about one lemon) | |
| 30 ml (1 oz) | fresh orange juice (half an orange) | the reader said many preferred it to orange bitters |
| 15 ml (½ oz) | pomegranate grenadine, home-made (below) | real pomegranate, which Fougner wrote the reputable French makers used |
| 45 ml (1½ oz) | cold seltzer (soda water) | the reader wrote "plain water or seltzer"; I chose the seltzer |
| 1 sprig | fresh mint, lightly bruised | his "improvement", in place of crème de menthe |
| 2 | orange half-slices | |
| 1 small piece | fresh pineapple (tinned is fine) | |
| 1 | fresh cherry, or a cherry kept in brandy (not a bright-red maraschino-style one) | |
| | *for the grenadine:* 250 g pure pomegranate juice (unsweetened; a bottle is fine) and 250 g sugar | nothing else: no orange, no acids |

**method**
1. Make the grenadine ahead. Put the pomegranate juice and sugar in a jar, close it and shake (or blend) until the sugar has dissolved. Keep it in the fridge.
2. Put one large piece of ice in a large, heavy, round-bowled beer glass.
3. Pour the bourbon, lemon juice, orange juice and grenadine into a shaker. Fill it with ice and shake briefly, about five seconds. That leaves room for the seltzer.
4. Strain it into the glass, over the ice.
5. Top up with the cold seltzer (soda water).
6. Press the mint sprig once between your fingers to wake it up. Tuck it into the glass with the orange half-slices, the pineapple and the cherry, the way the reader wrote it.

**closingLine:** *Mint in last, the way the reader wrote it. Then see who writes back.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*This glass starts from a reader's letter to a New York paper, in 1934.*

**whoYouAre**
When something matters to you and the arguing starts, you're rarely the loudest person there. Often you go quiet. The right words turn up later, on the way home, and you can lose sleep over the ones you didn't say. Losing an argument, you get over. Staying quiet when it really mattered, you don't.

Then you make something: a page, a song, a picture, a menu, a message that took all evening. In that, you say all of it, plainly, and you mean every word. Sometimes you wonder whether making things instead of arguing is just a nicer way of hiding. It isn't. An argument changes one mind, if you're lucky. What you make travels. It gives people the words they were missing, and room to join in without anyone being talked down to. That's your way of standing up for something, and it brings people together.

**yours**

1. In the autumn of 1933, with Prohibition about to end, the *Sun*, a New York newspaper, gave a writer named G. Selmer Fougner a daily series to help its readers find their way around legal wine and spirits. Wine writing then tended to be grand and mystifying, and cocktail writing jokey, even crude. Fougner was neither. He didn't talk down to anyone who liked a cocktail or a beer, and he answered readers' questions patiently, however silly. One wanted to know whether all scotch was really made in Australia.

2. When he had a view, he gave it, gently, usually. In 1936, when the grenadine sold in America was mostly imitation, he wrote that the reputable French makers used real pomegranate. And when he didn't know an answer, he put the question to readers all over the country, many of whom had been there when famous drinks began, and they wrote back. The column ran daily until he died in 1941.

3. I like to think this was his way of standing up for something: not with a speech, but with a daily column, where nobody was laughed at for asking and anyone who knew better could say so. It's your way too. And look at what came back when he asked. More than one reader wrote to him about a single Boston drink, and one of those letters holds the recipe behind the cocktail I've made for you.

4. It's the Ward Eight, Boston's best-known cocktail, built from that reader's 1934 recipe: bourbon, lemon, grenadine and half an orange, squeezed, which he said many people preferred, over a rather large piece of ice in a heavy, round-bowled glass "of the type generally used for beer". Fresh mint goes in with the orange slices, pineapple and cherry he listed, in place of a mint liqueur. "This is an improvement," the reader wrote. He allowed plain water or seltzer; I chose the seltzer. His letter leaves the sugar to taste, so I left out the spoon, because the orange and the grenadine carry the sweetness. The grenadine is made from real pomegranate, for Fougner's column of 1936. Pomegranate's own aromas include mint, so the reader's improvement and the columnist's point share a scent. It's long, bright and tart, a hazy rosy orange, and strong: the reader called his "highly potent".

5. So here's my suggestion. Somewhere there's a thing you made about what you believe, and never put out: the page, the song, the long message sitting in drafts. It's ready. But sending it is the argument, so it waits. Send it, where anyone can see it. Then ask people what they think, and mean the question. Some of the answers will surprise you, and you'll find you were never standing up for it alone.

**closingLine (Tomás's):** *Mint in last, the way the reader wrote it. Then see who writes back.*
