## whoYouAre
When something matters to you and the arguing starts, you're rarely the loudest person there. Often you go quiet. The right words turn up later, on the way home, and you can lose sleep over the ones you didn't say. Losing an argument, you can live with. Staying quiet when it really mattered, you can't.

Then you make something: a page, a song, a picture, a menu, a message that took all evening. In that, you say all of it, plainly, and you mean every word. Sometimes you wonder whether making things instead of arguing is just a nicer way of hiding. It isn't. An argument changes one mind, if you're lucky. What you make travels. It gives people the words they were missing, and room to join in without anyone being talked down to. That's your way of standing up for something, and it brings people together.

## yours
1. In the autumn of 1933, with Prohibition about to end, a New York newspaper, the *Sun*, gave a writer named G. Selmer Fougner a daily column to help its readers find their way back to wine and spirits. Back then, wine writing tended to be grand and mystifying, and cocktail writing was jokey, even crude. Fougner was neither. He didn't talk down to anyone who liked a cocktail or a beer, and he answered every reader's question patiently, however silly. One wanted to know whether all scotch was really made in Australia.

2. When he had a view, he gave it in the column, gently, usually. In 1936, when the grenadine sold in America was mostly imitation, he wrote that the reputable French makers used real pomegranate. And when he didn't know an answer, he put the question to his readers all over the country, many of them old bartenders, and they wrote back. He kept the column going from October 1933 until he died in 1941.

3. I like to think that was his way of standing up for something: not with a speech, but with a column that came out every day, where nobody was laughed at for asking and anyone who knew better could say so. It's your way too. And look at what came back when he asked. More than one reader wrote to him about a single Boston drink, and one of those letters is the cocktail I've made for you.

4. It's the Ward Eight, Boston's best-known cocktail, built from the recipe that reader sent in 1934, while Fougner was asking for more about it. The reader wrote it with bourbon, lemon, sugar and grenadine, a large piece of ice and soda, in a heavy, round-bowled glass "of the type generally used for beer". Then the reader added that fresh mint, when you can get it, is "an improvement", and passed on that many people preferred the juice of half an orange. So your cocktail has the reader's bourbon, the mint, the half orange and the big glass. The grenadine is made from real pomegranate, because that's what Fougner wrote the good ones were made of. And pomegranate carries a faint scent of mint of its own, so the reader's improvement and the columnist's point turn out to belong together. It's long, bright and tart, with a blush of red.

5. So here's my suggestion. Think of the thing you've made and haven't shown anyone: the page, the song, the long message sitting in drafts. It's ready, or near enough, but sending it would be the argument, so it waits. Send it. Then ask people what they think, and mean the question. Some of the answers will surprise you, and you'll find you were never standing up for it alone.

**closing line:** Tomás's. A direction, if it helps: the big glass and the pomegranate, then *send it* in bar size. Never "put your name on it" (the closing line of *lover-creator*).
