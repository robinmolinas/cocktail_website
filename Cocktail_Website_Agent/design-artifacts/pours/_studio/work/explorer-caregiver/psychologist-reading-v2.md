## whoYouAre

Every time you return to a place you love, you're bracing before you arrive. The café that might have closed. The field that might be houses now. The woods at the end of the lane, with a car park where the gate was. Everyone else sees the view. You're counting what's left.

You start missing things while they're still here. So you touch them as little as you can. You don't pick the flower. The stone stays where it was. The path looks the way you found it. People sometimes take that for strictness, and you'd rather they did. It's easier than saying how much it hurts to watch something thin out while everyone around you is still enjoying it. And easier than admitting the fear under that one: that some day you'll be part of the reason it went. But here's what your care does, whether anyone thanks you or not. When other people arrive, there's still something there for them to love.

**yours**

1. The agave, the plant tequila is made from, grows for years before it flowers, and it flowers only once. A stalk shoots up from its heart, sometimes twenty centimetres in a day. Its flowers open at night, and bats come for the nectar and carry the pollen. Then the plant dies. As tequila grew, producers stopped letting their agaves flower, because a plant spends the sugar in its heart on that one flower, and once it has flowered it's no use to the still. That took away a main food of the nectar bats, and their migrations shifted.
2. David Suro-Piñera makes tequila. With a bat scientist, Rodrigo Medellín, he helped start a programme that asks growers to let some of their agaves flower, at least five in every hundred, and to show that bats have fed from them. To a grower, Medellín said, that's "considered a waste": those plants can never be harvested. One of Suro-Piñera's own tequilas was on the programme's first list. And his reason, in his own words, wasn't only the bats. It was "the necessity of genetic strength": plants left to flower make the crop stronger for later. The lesser long-nosed bat, one of the agave's bats, came off the US endangered list in 2018, the first bat ever taken off because it had recovered. Many things did that, not tequila alone.
3. I like to think the bats came back one year to the fields they'd always fed from and found the flowers gone. That's your fear, exactly. What Suro-Piñera asked for is what you already do everywhere you go: leave some of it standing, even when taking it would make perfect sense, so there's something there for whoever comes next. His reason is yours too. You don't hold back because you're soft about wild things. You hold back because you feel responsible for what a place will be long after you've left it.
4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Martini, the most famous stirred drink there is, made with blanco tequila: the clear, unaged kind, from agave alone. A soft, herbal blanc vermouth goes with it. No peel, no garnish, so the plant is what you smell before anything else. Then a little elderflower liqueur. It isn't the agave's own flower. But an agave that flowers gives its sugar to the nectar instead of the still, so I added a flower to your glass as a nod to the ones left to bloom. It's served small and very cold, without ice, so it's at its best in the first few minutes. It asks you to pay attention while it's there.
5. You don't have to be the only one watching. The next place you're afraid of losing, don't go alone. Take someone and show them what you see: the thing that's thinning out, the one nobody else has noticed yet. Then there are two of you who'd notice if it went. And I think you'll find that watching it with someone feels less like grieving and more like what it really is: loving it while it's here.
