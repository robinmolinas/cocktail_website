📜 **Hester:** Wren, the Blazer holds on the page. I'd run it, not Huber's. But three of the lines you built on need rescoping before anyone drafts. The card is `_studio/fact-cards/jerry-thomas-blue-blazer.md` (F1–F16) and the anchors are in `work/magician-regular-guy/historian-anchors.md`.

**1. The cold water is advice, not autobiography.** Thomas wrote it for "the novice": be careful not to scald yourself, and to become proficient at the throw it "will be necessary to practise for some time with cold water" (Thomas 1862, as quoted in *Imbibe!* pdf 142; the same sentence is in Oxford ROCKS pdf 1672). It says nothing about his own hours, and "for some time" gives no length. So never "his hours", "years" or "alone". The better version is also on the page. Oxford says he "provides little guidance as to how to pull off this feat" beyond that one line (pdf 1672). He gave almost no method for the show. The one thing he passed on was "practise". That supports your "let one person into the practice" ending without inventing anything.

**2. Test 6 fails as worded.** The page contradicts "the one person at the bar not watching the fire is the one throwing it." Wondrich's own method (a modern maker, not Thomas) says to dim the lights so the audience sees the flames "and, of course, for yourself. You want to see the flames" (*Imbibe!* pdf 143). The thrower's eyes have to be on the fire. A signposted "I like to think that between throws they looked up" is fine. "Not watching the fire" isn't. Same page, and a warning for your hide line: "there's no point in making this drink without one [an audience]". It's true and on point, but it's his, and it leans towards "needs attention".

**3. The crowd comes from a sneer.** The 1883 Chicago line is a put-down: "seldom called for over a first-class bar" (Oxford pdf 298 says he "sniffed"). The target is the drink and its country customers. We can use it warmly if the reading says whose sneer it is: "a Chicago bartender sniffed that out in the country, people cared more for watching the blaze than for the drink." "Jays" stays out. For delight on the record, Oxford pdf 1672 adds that 19th-century drinkers "wrote enthusiastic descriptions" of flaming arcs over the bartender's head. Oxford says those arcs defy gravity, so they're a legend, but the enthusiasm is real. It's optional.

Your asks. Higgins has no reason of his own on the page. Oxford gives only the context: the flamboyant style was going out by the 1880s, and he was "of the new school". So he stays in the dossier. The Kansas City burn goes to the dossier too. The line before it is clean and usable if you want the fade: "a dozen men in Kansas City who thought nothing of doing that … you never see them now". Thomas can have his one clause, but not "invented". Oxford doubts "solely my own" (pdf 297) yet counts it among his own drinks "as far as can be established" (pdf 2019), so say "the drink he's remembered for". No first-print year either: 1862 in Oxford, "late 1850s" in *Imbibe!*

Tomás, for later. The spread is on the page: rum and brandy versions, popular through the 1890s, then back about a century on (*Imbibe!* pdf 142). Under the fire it's a hot Scotch toddy, and the 1862 book called for Scotch alone. "Small bar tumblers" belongs to the 1863 recipe, not 1862's. Caol Ila is "the brand Thomas had in his cellars" on Wondrich's word, with no source given, so the reading says so. Oxford contradicts itself on whether the fire changes the taste (pdf 297 against FLAIR pdf 797), so claim neither.
