# Persona card: The Dashing Hero (hero-lover)

Wren, round 1 (2026-10-04). From `persona.py hero-lover`, the six authored Hero siblings' readings, the registry, and the plan row (`plans/hero.md`), with Oxford VESPER (pdf 2099–2100), Oxford pdf 782 and *Joy* pdf 402 re-read in full.

## The sheet, and what's under it
The sheet: "makes danger look like a dance: calm under fire, a joke ready, a perfectly timed exit … never brave for nothing … fights for the people they love and hides how much they love them, because feeling too much looks like weakness. Losing someone they could not protect is the wound they never show." Goal Mastery, fear Weakness; Lover side: fears isolation and coldness. Bond is the sheet's example, and he's the trap as much as the help.

The sheet's surface (polish, nerve, the timed exit) is what everyone sees, and it's flattering, so it's the part a reading must *not* stop at. The real person is in the last two sentences.

## Core truth (the one thing no sibling shares)
**The polish is a way of loving people without having to say so.** The cooler they look, the more someone matters. Every exact, unflustered, well-turned thing they do is aimed at someone in particular, and that person usually never knows it was for them.

## Shows / hides
- **Shows:** ease. They're the one who stays light when it goes wrong, says the dry thing at the right moment, knows exactly how something should be done and does it that way. Exact about small things (how a drink is made, the right coat, the reservation). Never seen to try.
- **Hides:** who it's all for. They'll remember precisely how you take your coffee and never mention they noticed. They'd walk into anything for a few people, and they'd make a joke of it so nobody has to see the feeling. Saying it plainly feels like handing someone a weakness.

## Hidden fear → hidden gift
- **Fear:** that feeling too much will show, and that showing it is weakness. Under that, the wound: losing someone they couldn't keep safe. They never talk about it. The grief shows only as an absence, a thing they quietly stop doing (a place they don't go back to, an order they never make again), and nobody around them knows why.
- **Gift (said out loud once, per Robin's caregiver rule):** the people they love get to feel light. They take the fear in the room onto themselves and hand back a joke, so nobody else has to be afraid. That's how they love, and it's worth far more than the style it's dressed in.

## The "real me" lines to aim for (drafts, not final)
- *"It was never the danger you were hiding. It was them."* (my lead)
- *"The more someone matters, the cooler you get."*
- The loss, gently and only signposted: *"You don't talk about the ones you lost. There's just one thing you never order again, and nobody knows why."*

Barnum check on the third: "a song you skip after a loss" is true of many people. It only becomes this person when it's the *only* form the grief takes and nobody near them knows the reason. Keep it to one sentence, specific, and signposted.

## Truth vs. legend
They want the true story told with style, not stripped bare and not sentimental. A fiction is fine if it's owned in one clause; what they'd hate is being flattered with a glamorous myth (the Dukes Bar origin is exactly that, debunked: Oxford VESPER pdf 2099). Grant nothing they'd have to pretend to believe.

## Tone
Hero (assertive, empowering) × Lover (passionate, inviting). For the bartender: crisp, dry, one wink allowed early (the persona is funny: "a joke ready"), then a turn into plain tenderness that doesn't apologise for itself. Short sentences. Never swagger on the guest's behalf, never gush. The reading itself should behave like the person: cool on top, and then one line that isn't.

## Distinct from siblings (and the near cousins)
- **The Storyteller** (*Hold the Shark*) tells a bad day *afterwards*, larger, so the listeners feel better; what it hides is how bad it was. The Dashing Hero's joke comes *during*, and what it hides isn't the danger but the person they're doing it for.
- **The Pioneer** (*Nothing to It*, Explorer) hides *fear* behind a shrug; its position is "tell them you were terrified". The Dashing Hero hides *love*. So our position must never be about admitting fear.
- **The Firefighter** (*Anyone Would Have*) hides the practice, for a team; the Dashing Hero hides the feeling, for one person or a few.
- **The Saviour** (*Next One's Mine*) takes the cost in someone's place and can't bear to owe. Close in act, different in what's hidden (owing vs. loving).
- **The Knight in Shining Armour** (*Out of Your Way*, lover-hero, the mirror pairing) loves *visibly*: shows up, the whole room turns round. The Dashing Hero is the same depth at the opposite volume. Keep "being there / showing up / the door" out.
- **The Teenage Heart-throb** (*The Big Words*) says it big. The Sculptor (*Quite Alive*: "You never say it. You make it.") owns the never-say-it/make-it frame: our wording must not echo it.
- Later siblings to keep apart: the Superhero (skill hidden in an ordinary life), the Lovable Rogue (kindness disguised as a telling-off), the Warrior (a private fear the group didn't see), the Problem Solver (cool under pressure with people watching: the *problem* is the point there; here it's the *person*).

## Traps
- **Bond as the guest's model.** The guest is never Bond, never aspires to him, and no spy wordplay: no "shaken, not stirred", "licence", "mission", "Mr", "agent", "tux", nothing from Bond titles (*Never Say Never Again*, *For Your Eyes Only*, *You Only Live Twice*; note Bryce's memoir is *You Only Live Once*: keep that title out of guest text too).
- **Flattery.** "Effortlessly cool", "always lands on your feet" are compliments. Say what they *do* with the cool, and who for.
- **Seduction, gender.** Nothing about charm with strangers; the beloved is "someone", never gendered. "Lover" never appears (Oxford's own parenthesis uses it).
- **The death.** Never how or why Vesper dies (Oxford says suicide), never betrayal or "double agent", nothing Bond says after her death (the novel's last line is famously cold; we never quote or paraphrase it).
- **"Calm" is crowded in the registry** (*The Rest of You*, *Before the Room*, *The Long View*, *In One Piece*). Calm can be the setting, never the centre.
- **Rationed hero words:** at most one of "hero", "brave", "fight" (family guard).
- **"Put your name on it"** is the Muse's position (*In Your Own Hand*); "say it back" is the Understanding Spouse's (*Standing By*). Watch both if the position involves naming someone.
- **Grief as a lesson.** The loss is recognised, never solved and never used to scare them into speaking up.

## My story test (for this pairing)
A public style, exact and cool, covered a private feeling for one person or a few; the source shows the feeling only in a gesture (a name, an inscription, a habit), not in a speech; any loss is marked by an absence or a quiet act, told gently; both the style and the feeling are real. Out: seduction, violence, a death told as spectacle, betrayal.

## Verdict on the plan's lead: **accept the Vesper, owned as fiction, with conditions**
I re-read Oxford VESPER whole (pdf 2099–2100). It passes all four limbs, on the page:
1. **The style:** the exact order, word for word (three of Gordon's, one of vodka, half of Kina Lillet, shaken until ice-cold, a large thin slice of lemon peel). In the novel it's "his original on-the-spot invention".
2. **The feeling, as a gesture:** at first it has no name; later he gives it hers.
3. **The loss, as an absence:** after she dies, "he never drinks it again." No speech. That *is* this persona's wound, the one they never show, and few readers know it: that's the "how did they know".
4. **The real layer under the fiction** (new, from re-reading the page): Fleming inscribed his friend Ivar Bryce's copy "For Ivar, who mixed the first Vesper", and Bryce recalls making it with Fleming's help. In print, the drink is the hero's own swagger; in private ink, it's credited to a friend. That's the persona's split (style in public, feeling in a private gesture) in a real person. One clause, as the fiction's grounding; never a second story.

**Conditions:**
- Own the fiction in one clause ("in Fleming's first novel"). Scope "never again" to the novel. My own knowledge, unsourced, for Hester: the 2006 film has him order Vespers after her death, so a guest who knows the films may think we're wrong. "In the novel" has to be there, and Hester should check Oxford's "never drinks it again" against the book if she can (the library doesn't hold *Casino Royale*): does it mean within the book's last pages, or the whole series?
- The *why* of the never-again is never a fact. The novel's own ending is cold, not tender, so any reading of it as grief is "I like to think…", and it must not contradict the book. Read that way, it fits the persona even better: feeling covered with a hard surface.
- "After he lost her" is the most the guest text says about her end.
- Bryce: name, friend, inscription. No OSS, no war (family ground). The butler's "Vespers are served" and the frozen rum drink in Jamaica are on the page too (Oxford: Fleming "apparently" took the name from it): dossier, or a lead for Tomás (below). The Dukes/Preti myth stays in the dossier as debunked.
- No "the original can't be made" (the Kina Lillet quinine change was 1917: Oxford pdf 2100).
- Calabrese stays the backup. He fails my test (the page shows *his wife's* care, not his hidden feeling, and no loss).

## For Tomás (his call entirely; leads only)
The persona wants the drink to behave like them: immaculate and cold on top, with one private thing underneath that only someone who knows would find. Oxford gives a real candidate on the same page: the name came from "a curious drink of frozen rum with fruit and herbs" served at a country house in Jamaica. A trace of that under the exact order would be the private origin under the public polish, tied to the story in one clause. Or anything else the books give that does the same job. Whatever changes, the guest text then says "it starts from his order", never "his recipe". Glass: Oxford's recipe says a chilled cocktail glass; the plan's deep champagne goblet is from memory of the novel, so Hester rules.
