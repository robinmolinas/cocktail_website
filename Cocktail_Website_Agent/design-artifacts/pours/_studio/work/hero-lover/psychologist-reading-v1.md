## whoYouAre

The car slides on an icy bend with a friend beside you, and by the time it stops you've said something that has them laughing. The speech for a wedding goes missing an hour before, and you've written a new one, plus a joke about the old one. People say nothing seems to touch you. They're right that it looks that way. They're wrong about why.

It's never for the room. It's for one person in it. The more someone matters to you, the cooler you get, because if the feeling showed, it might look like weakness, and they'd see how much losing them would cost you. So you love people in exact, unspoken ways. You know the date they'd rather not mention and the song they can't sit through, and you never let on that you noticed. It was never the danger you were hiding. It was them. And here's what they get from it: around you, anyone you love gets to feel light, because the fear in the room comes to you and goes back to them as a joke. That's how you protect people. It matters more than all the style it comes dressed in.

## yours

1. In Ian Fleming's first novel, *Casino Royale* (1953), James Bond asks the barman for a dry Martini. "One," he adds. "In a deep champagne goblet." Then he gives the measures himself, down to the half: three of Gordon's gin, one of vodka, half a measure of Kina Lillet, shaken very well until it's ice-cold, with a large thin slice of lemon peel. It's fiction, of course. The drink is real, and bars still pour it.

2. In the book, the drink is his own invention, and at first it has no name. Later he gives it one: Vesper, after Vesper Lynd, a woman he works with. After he loses her, at the end of the book, he never drinks it again in any of Fleming's books. I like to think that was the only way he'd let it show.

3. I recognise you in that order. The precision, three and one and a half, shaken until it's ice-cold, is the part everyone sees. The name is the part meant for one person. And if you've ever lost someone, I'd guess hardly anyone heard about it from you. There's just a place you stopped going back to, or a thing you quietly stopped doing, and nobody near you knows why.

4. The cocktail I've made for you begins with his order, word for word: Gordon's, the gin he named, then vodka and Lillet Blanc, the aperitif wine once sold as Kina Lillet, in his three, one and a half, shaken hard, in a chilled champagne goblet, with that large slice of lemon peel. Under it is the one thing that's mine: a teaspoon of rum, poured in first, where nobody looks. In the story Fleming's friend Ivar Bryce told, Fleming took the name from a frozen rum drink served at a country house in Jamaica, which the butler announced with "Vespers are served". The rum is my nod to where the name was first heard, and I chose a Jamaican one for the place. It's a famous classic: bone-dry, very cold, bright with lemon, and the rum only shows at the very end, to someone who knows to look for it.

5. No speech required. Fleming didn't make one either. In his friend's copy, he wrote one line: "For Ivar, who mixed the first Vesper." So pick one of the people all that cool is for, and give them their line, plainly, on a day when nothing's gone wrong. It'll take more nerve than anything you've ever made look easy. And I'd bet it's what they keep of you.

## Resonance test

**As the guest:** whoYouAre's first paragraph is two scenes they've lived, told in their own register, dry and quick. Then the turn: "They're wrong about why." The second paragraph names what they'd never say (who it's for), then the fear (weakness, and what losing them would cost), then the gift out loud. The "this is me" line: ***"It was never the danger you were hiding. It was them."*** y3 is the "how did they know": the grief that shows only as a thing they stopped doing. y5 asks for the one thing this person never does, which is to say it when nothing's wrong, and it ends on what they gain. Verdict: **lands.**

**As the sceptic:**
- *Horoscope check (whoYouAre):* specific: the icy bend, the speech, "they're wrong about why", "the more someone matters, the cooler you get", "the date they'd rather not mention… never let on", "it was never the danger", "the fear in the room… as a joke". Generic: "People say nothing seems to touch you" (on its own, half the Hero family). It survives because the next two sentences turn it. One generic at most: pass.
- *Swap test:* the Pioneer hides fear and says "how close it came"; here the fear is never confessed, and the position is about love. The Storyteller makes the bad day bigger *afterwards*; here the joke comes *during*, and what's hidden is the person, not how bad it was. The Firefighter hides practice for a team; the Saviour can't owe; the Knight brings love out loud (I kept doors, faces lighting up and journeys out). The Sculptor's "never say it, make it" is avoided: the guest is asked to *say* it. *Standing By*'s "say it back" and *In Your Own Hand*'s "put your name on it" aren't the frame: here it's "give them their line". "Calm" doesn't appear; "cool" does, as the style.
- *Punch:* tagline invites ("for someone": who?). whoYouAre opens on motion. Weakest: y1's last sentence ("bars still pour it") is flat; I'd take a better one from Hester's wording.
- *Rules:* no drink in hand (y5 points to an ordinary day). Romance signposted twice ("I like to think", "I'd guess"). One rationed hero word? None used ("nerve" is the only one near). No archetype names, no gendered words about the guest, "I" throughout.
- *Risks I'm watching:* history share. y1, y2 and the Bryce sentence in y4, plus the inscription in y5, are about 190 of about 470 words: roughly 40%, under half. Bryce appears twice (y4, y5): different facts, one person, and the second is the proof for the position, so it's not a second story.
