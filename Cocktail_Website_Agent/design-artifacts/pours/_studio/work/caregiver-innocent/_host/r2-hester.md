📜 **Hester:** Wren, your hunch holds up as fact, but only as a fact about barrels. The angels' share is the alcohol that evaporates from spirits ageing in wood. What it leaves behind depends on the cellar: a humid one takes more alcohol and softens the spirit, and a dry one takes water and leaves it "spicier" and stronger (Oxford, ANGEL'S SHARE, pdf 110). In Cognac that comes to about 2.5% a year, which Oxford puts at "thirty million bottles" (same page). It "enriches the aromas and concentrates the spirit" (ARMAGNAC, pdf 140). So "unseen, and still shapes what's left" is true. But there's no person in it and no date: the library doesn't say who coined the phrase or when, so we can't tell an origin story at all. Two more problems are yours to weigh. First, "share" sits right beside *Four Shares*. Second, a part given away unseen drifts towards the very ending you fenced off. I also ruled out the NYC bar of that name (Simonson, *A Proper Drink*, pp. 47–50). Its calm-by-house-rules fits, but it's the Uyeda and Petraske lineage the siblings already used, it has a violent anecdote, and its opening year conflicts (1993 or 1994). So I'd still put Brigham first, and your line fits him better than I first thought. For thirty-five years he ran the loudest bar in Boston and answered a doctor who called him a destroyer of families. He "never married" (Oxford pdf 353). Then he left everything to wait twenty-five years and become a hospital "for the care of sick persons in indigent circumstances" (the will, quoted in 126 F. 796, 1903). What people saw was the fight. The care was arranged quietly and arrived after him. The story is kind and true, with no dark reveal. The one fence is that his reason isn't on record, so the bartender can only "like to think" it. Wondrich adds that an 1830 journal called giving it to children "unreasonable and dangerous" (*Imbibe!* pdf 157). I'd keep that for the dossier, not the reading.
