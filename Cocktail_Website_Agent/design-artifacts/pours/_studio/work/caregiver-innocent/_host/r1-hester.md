📜 **Hester:** Wren, two mirrors for the Angel. I'm opening with the one I'd fight for, because the true story beats anything we could invent. **Peter Bent Brigham**, Boston, 1842. His Oyster Saloon's list of "Fancy Drinks" set the American canon for twenty years (Oxford, BRIGHAM, PETER BENT, pdf 353). A temperance doctor, Charles Jewett, asked him in print how he could, "for paltry gain", destroy "the peace of families" (*Imbibe!* pdf 99). Brigham answered by adding a drink called the Moral Suasion. He "never married" (Oxford pdf 353). His will kept his fortune invested "for the term of twenty-five years from the time of my decease". After that it was to found a hospital "for the care of sick persons in indigent circumstances" (the will as quoted in *Brigham v. Peter Bent Brigham Hospital*, 126 F. 796, 1903, secondary). It opened in 1913, and today it is the Brigham and Women's. The man accused of greed spent his fortune caring for strangers who couldn't pay, and he never saw a single patient. That is the Angel's fear of selfishness, turned into service. Two fences, stated now so they can't creep in later. First, **his motive isn't on the record**: any link between Jewett's "paltry gain" and the bequest is our reading, and it must be signposted. Second, **none of his recipes survive** (Oxford pdf 353). The only Moral Suasion formula is from 1873 and credited to someone else (*Imbibe!* pdf 100), so it can never be "his drink". The risk is yours to weigh: Brigham was loud and combative, not gentle. My runner-up is softer: **angelica**, the gin botanical "which boosts the juniper" (Oxford, GIN, pdf 894). Nobody tastes it by name, and its job is to make another flavour stronger. The story that an angel revealed it in a dream to cure plague is a **legend** (Grieve, 1931). Tomás, one thing you'll like: angelica is one of Bénédictine's 27 plants (Oxford pdf 256), and Bénédictine is in the only surviving Moral Suasion.
