_Round 1, 2026-09-30. New story + new drink required._

## A. Canon Félix Kir (TOP PICK)

**Source:** Oxford Companion, KIR, pdf 1133 (primary)

**Documented facts:**
- F1. Félix Kir (1876–1968), a priest who served as mayor of Dijon from 1945 to 1968.
- F2. Oxford describes him as a "highly respected French Resistance veteran." (pdf 1133)
- F3. The drink Blanc Cassis (white Burgundy wine + crème de cassis) already existed before Kir; it had been popular in France since at least the 1930s and was the official civic drink of Dijon from 1904 under mayor Henri Barabant.
- F4. Kir used the Blanc Cassis at civic receptions. The drink took his name.
- F5. After his death in 1968, the drink became internationally fashionable; food writer James Beard called it the "favorite aperitif." (Oxford pdf 1133, citing Philadelphia Inquirer, 1970)
- F6. The Kir Royale is the variant made with crémant de Bourgogne or champagne. (Oxford pdf 1133)

**Secondary (flagged):**
- S1. French historical sources give more detail on his Resistance activities (helped prisoners escape, used clerical cover). These are secondary to Oxford and must be labelled as such in any reading.

**Mirror assessment:**
- "Calm brought in, not given off": A priest walks into a room; people compose themselves. At civic receptions, his presence and the gesture of pouring set the tone — not by announcement but by being.
- "The half nobody sees": He was a Resistance veteran. The international generation that adopted "Kir" as a brunch drink in the 1970s-80s rarely knew the man. The care and courage were real; the branding came later.
- Innocent quality: He poured what was already there. He didn't invent the drink. He served it. Pure act of service, no authorship claimed.

**Fences:**
- Oxford gives no detail on what he did in the Resistance — only "highly respected veteran." Secondary sources may be used but must be labelled.
- He did not invent the Blanc Cassis. The reading must say he poured it, not created it.
- He was a priest and politician, not a bartender. The drink connection is indirect but documented (civic receptions, naming).

**Natural drink:** Kir (Blanc Cassis: white Burgundy + crème de cassis) or Kir Royale (sparkling + cassis). Both unused in the registry. Tomás to decide.

## B. Peter Bent Brigham (CONSIDERED, SET ASIDE)

Retained from first pour. Brigham (1807–1877, Oxford pdf 353; earlier '1840' was my error, struck in audit v5) ran an Oyster Saloon in Boston, willed his fortune to found what is now Brigham and Women's Hospital.

**Why he's set aside for this rework:**
- "Calm brought in, not given off": Brigham was combative — "constant battles with Boston's many and vocal Prohibitionists", answered temperance accusations with a joke drink (Moral Suasion). His surface was fight, not calm.
- Oxford source (pdf 353) does not document his will as secret or "arranged quietly." The will was read after his death; nothing more.
- "Legacy at a distance" (care executed posthumously through trustees) was Wren's original and correct objection.

**Sources still valid if ever needed:**
- Oxford Companion, BRIGHAM, PETER BENT, pdf 353
- Imbibe!, pdf 99–100 (Jewett accusation, Moral Suasion)
- 126 F. 796 (the will, secondary via case law)

## C. Frank Meier (CONSIDERED, SET ASIDE)

Head bartender at the Ritz Paris 1920–1947. Ran "the most popular and crowded bar in Paris." Published The Artistry of Mixing Drinks (1936). "Seems to have helped the French Resistance (the record is murky)" — Oxford, MEIER, FRANK, pdf 1275–1276.

**Why set aside:**
- Both major drink attributions (Bee's Knees, Corpse Reviver No. 2) are contested within the same Oxford volume (Bee's Knees attributed to Molly Brown in its own entry, pdf 251; Corpse Reviver No. 2 attributed to Craddock in its own entry, pdf 584). No clean drink attribution to build on.
- Resistance role is "murky" by Oxford's own word — weaker than Kir's explicit "highly respected veteran."
- His luxury cocktail (vintage Sidecar, 1923) builds on the Sidecar, which is already used (creator-outlaw).

Oxford: MEIER, FRANK, pdf 1275–1276.
