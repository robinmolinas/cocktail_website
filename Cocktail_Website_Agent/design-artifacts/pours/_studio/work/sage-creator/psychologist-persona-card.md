# The Architect (sage-creator): persona card, round 1 (Wren)

Sources: persona.py sage-creator (essence, story, goal Wisdom, fear Deception, driver PROVIDE STRUCTURE); my family sketch (`plans/work/sage/psychologist-sketches.md`); Oxford BARREL STORAGE SYSTEMS pdf 219-220 and BARREL ROTATION pdf 218, read whole this session.

## The one true thing
They see what holds a thing up before it exists, and they can't trust anything that only looks sound. Other people picture the finished thing; the Architect pictures what's carrying the weight.

The deception they fear is structural: the beam that was never carrying anything, the plan everyone loves with a crack nobody checked.

## Hides
- The run-through. By the time they say "yes, that'll work", they've already watched it fall down a dozen ways and fixed each one. The calm yes has a night of no behind it.
- That they don't much care how it looks. They'll leave the colours, the names, the visible part to anyone. That isn't modesty: it's not where the weight is.
- That they'd rather be thought slow, or a wet blanket, than build something that only looks ready.

## Fears
The thing that looks sound and isn't, especially when it's carrying people. Their own work being that thing is the worst version, but the fear is about the *thing*, not about blame.

## Gives
Things other people lean on without ever thinking about it. The holiday for twelve where nothing goes wrong and nobody knows why. The move, the kitchen, the evening that simply holds. Say it out loud once in the reading (Robin's rule): that's how they look after people. Nobody has to think about the floor.

## Shadow (gentle, for the position)
Packing it too tight. Every hour of the holiday planned, every gap filled, because a gap is something that could go wrong. The story's own false twin does exactly this.

## Siblings and neighbours (keep apart)
- **The Mentor (sage-caregiver, *Further Than Me*, just closed):** its "this is me" line is the fear of someone following your advice into a mistake ("What you couldn't live with is someone following you there"). The Architect's people-lean-on-it is on the **gift** side, never the fear side. The Architect's fear is the false appearance of soundness.
- **The Scientist:** checks the result after; the Architect tests the structure before a piece exists.
- **The Grandmaster:** runs an opponent's moves over time; the Architect runs the weight (what happens when twelve people lean on it at once). No opponent, no clock.
- **The Game-Changer:** changes how everybody does it; the Architect builds one thing that stands.
- **The Visionary (*Down the Line*):** owns seeing the finished thing first and others building on the dream. The Architect sees the bones, not the picture.
- **The Old Master (*Beside the First*), the Auteur (*Making the Calls*), the Perfectionist (*On Their Behalf*):** not revisiting old work, not control of every detail, not "the careless version". It's load-bearing against decorative, and the decorative is happily someone else's.
- **The Nanny (*Fair Measure*):** structure as a rule for others' fun. The Architect's structure is invisible and asks nothing of anyone.

## Traps
- Architect, engineer, builder, designer as labels; blueprint or drafting imagery in the text; praising "precision". The guest who has never built anything must still see themselves: keep it in life scenes (the kitchen, the holiday, the move).
- The story is literally a building, so the label risk is doubled: the reading never says "like an architect" and never explains the mirror ("the racks are like your plans").
- Reserved words and frames (registry and pour files): "plan" in the title block or closing line (*Further Than Me*'s tagline, *Before It Had a Name*'s tagline); "holds up" (*Asked In*'s closing line); "holding it up" (*Still Yours* y5); "finished" in a tagline (*Beside the First*); "leave room" (*Loose on Top*, *For Its Own Good*); "add the why" (*On Their Behalf*); "That's how you know…" (*For Good*); "Everyone…" as a tagline opener (ten taglines already).
- Making the shadow a diagnosis ("you're controlling"). It's said with love, as the cost of caring about the weight.

## Story test
The mirror must show (1) something laid out before it was filled (by inference here, said as such), where (2) what holds it up is hidden inside and the visible part is only a covering, and (3) it's still in use. Best if (4) a tidier twin trades away what made it work. The structure is on the page. Out: years to perfect, fame, a return to old work.

## Verdict on the plan's lead (the Kentucky rickhouse, no person): accept, with conditions
Re-read pdf 218-220 whole. It passes all four limbs on the page: the racks "form the inner core of the building", the floors hang on that frame, and the walls are "a skin of brick, wood, or corrugated tin" (any of the three: the covering doesn't matter, which is the Architect exactly). The open racks give "a great amount of air circulation"; palletised storage packs barrels "very tightly, saving warehouse space, at the expense of air circulation". The twin is also the Architect's shadow (packing it too tight), which gives the position a story-shaped reason.

Conditions:
1. **Oxford contradicts itself on height:** "nine-floor behemoths" (pdf 218) vs racks "as much as seven stories high" (pdf 219). No number of floors in guest text: "tall", "several floors".
2. **Scope:** "the simplest rickhouses" have the unitary frame. Never "every rickhouse", never "every bourbon rested on a rack".
3. **No designer, no date:** never "someone designed it". Limb (1) lives in the guest's scene.
4. **Rotation:** the bellies face the aisle so barrels *can* be rolled out and moved, but rotation "is not widely used" (pdf 218, labour and risk). Never say barrels are routinely moved between floors, in the reading or the drink.
5. **Palletised storage is a trade-off,** used by Irish and several Canadian distillers: never a failure, never "cheaper" or "lazy".
6. **"The racks are the building" once.** Not again in the name or tagline.
7. **No person means the guest has to stand in the building:** the reveal is that nobody who drinks a bourbon thinks about what it rested on. That's the gift, and the reading says so.

## Position I'm leaning to (not final)
Leave air in it. The best frame here is the open one: what makes it work is the gaps. So: next time you lay something out for people, leave gaps nobody's planned. The air is part of the structure. (Earned only after the gift is said; ends on what they gain. Words to avoid: "leave room".)
