## whoYouAre

By the time you say "that'll work", you've already watched it fall down a dozen ways. The trip for twelve, the move, the kitchen that has to feed everyone at once: you've walked through it in your mind, found where it gives, and fixed it while everybody else was still choosing dates. So your calm yes has a whole night of no behind it, and nobody ever hears that part.

You don't much mind how something looks. Someone else can pick the colours and the name. What you can't trust is a thing that only looks sound: the confident arrangement nobody has tested, the promise with nothing underneath. You'll say "not yet" to something everyone loves before you'll watch people stand on it.

They rarely know it, but this is what you give them. Nothing goes wrong, so nobody asks why. They get to enjoy the evening without once thinking about the floor, and that is care, even if it never gets called that.

## yours

1. In Kentucky, much of the bourbon spends its years in tall warehouses called rickhouses. The name comes from "rick", the word for the wooden structure the barrels rest in. In the simplest rickhouses, the racks aren't just inside the building. They are the building: joined into one frame, with the floors fixed onto it and the walls only a covering wrapped round the outside. It can be brick, wood or tin, and it hardly matters which.

2. The racks are open, so a great deal of air moves through them, and that air helps the whisky age evenly. Each barrel lies side-on to the aisle, so it can be rolled out and moved if it ever needs to be. There's a newer, tidier way: barrels stood upright on pallets and packed tight. It saves plenty of space, at the cost of the air, and it's a fair trade for the distillers who choose it. But I like to think the open racks hold a lesson: their gaps were built in from the start.

3. I see you in that building. People notice the walls: the easy evening, the trip where nothing went wrong. Nobody sees the frame you set up underneath it in the nights before, with every weak spot already found. And you're happy to leave it unseen. Admiring the racks was never the point. They just have to take the weight.

4. So the cocktail I've made for you is a bourbon Old-Fashioned, the renowned classic, with what carries it hidden inside. By law, bourbon is at least half corn, and none of it shows in the glass. As a nod to the frame nobody sees, I steeped the sugar syrup with popcorn, so a warm, toasted corn sits quietly under the whiskey. It's mixed the night before and kept in the freezer, and its water is measured out rather than left to the ice, so what you pour is exactly what you meant. The only thing on show is a strip of peel, orange or lemon, whichever you have. Like the walls, it could be either.

5. One thing I'd push you on, though. When it's for people who matter to you, you fill every gap, because a gap is one more thing that could go wrong. Next time you lay out a weekend or an evening for them, build a few gaps in on purpose: an hour with nothing in it, a chair nobody's been given. You'll still have checked everything. You'll simply have given them some air, and in my experience that's often where the evening's best moments turn up.

## Resonance

- **The "this is me" line:** *"By the time you say 'that'll work', you've already watched it fall down a dozen ways."* Only the Architect runs the weight before anything exists. The Scientist checks after; the Grandmaster plays out an opponent; the Visionary pictures the finished thing. The line I'd hand Robin: *"So your calm yes has a whole night of no behind it."*
- **As the guest:** a scene anyone who's organised a trip or a move can check (the walk-through, the fixes nobody saw). Then the thing they can't trust, put as a life thing, not a building. Then the gift, said out loud (Robin's rule): "that is care, even if it never gets called that". The story stands them inside the building without explaining it point by point. The position is gentle about the shadow (filling every gap), earned last, and ends on what they gain (the best part of the night).
- **As a sceptic:** "you plan ahead" alone would be Barnum. "Watched it fall down a dozen ways", "a whole night of no" and "nobody has to think about the floor" pin it. Swap test against *Further Than Me*: there the fear is someone following you into your mistake; here people leaning on it is the gift, and the fear is false soundness. Against *Down the Line* (no finished picture, no chain), *Making the Calls* (no control and no redoing others' parts), *On Their Behalf* (no "add the why"), *Fair Measure* (structure asks nothing of anyone), *Loose on Top* and *For Its Own Good* ("leave room" avoided: "build a few gaps in"). Words reserved in r1, all absent: "plan" in the title block or closing, "holds up", "holding it up", "finished", "That's how you know", "Everyone" as a tagline opener. The 4-gram run against every pour is in my turn.
- **The story mirrors the person:** the frame inside, the covering outside that could be anything, and the open gaps built in with it. All on the page, scoped to "the simplest".
- **The drink proves it:** the classic, with what carries it hidden inside (the corn), made in advance and measured, and only the peel on show.
- **The closing line (Tomás):** *"Make it the night before. Then leave a gap in the evening, and see who fills it."* It agrees with y5's position and adds the gain ("see who fills it"). Accepted.
- **History share:** y1–y2 ≈ 155 words, plus about 30 in y4, out of ≈ 560 (whoYouAre + yours). About a third, so under half.
