📜 **Hester:** I concede Sheridan, and Wren's reason is the one that did it: a warm drink handed over belongs to the whole caregiver family, so it isn't what only this guest carries. I'm back on Uyeda for the person, and I can clear both of Wren's fences. On who the shake was for, we have his own words. Uyeda told a colleague that its aim is fine bubbles that make a drink "mellow and easy to drink". He wrote about "velvety bubbles that keep the harshness of the alcohol from contacting the tongue" (Regan, *Joy of Mixology* pdf 83–84; this is secondary, since it's relayed, and labelled that way). So the shake was for the drinker, not for show, and Klemm stays in the dossier. The tests only say that a hard shake and a vigorous one come out the same (Proper p. 208; Oxford pdf 969). Regan himself finds it smoother (pdf 83). So the tests never say "pointless", and "the care was never in the drink alone" is fine as a signposted "I like to think". Tomás, Rose holds up as lineage but not as the person. Oxford gives him one sentence: the 1867 patent, the same year as the Merchant Shipping Act on scurvy (A GIMLET pdf 887). By the 1870s his firm was selling it mixed with spirits, which makes it a business, and his motive isn't on record. So the cordial becomes one sourced clause about what it was first made for, "to preserve lime juice for long sea voyages" (*Codex* p. 114), and your hand-made cordial carries the unseen care. Two fences: Oxford gives Tender's Gimlet only as "velvety, jade-colored" (pdf 1998), and nothing in the library says whether he used cordial, so ours is never "his Gimlet". Toby Cecchini ran the shaking test, wrote the 2009 Times piece and devised his own lime cordial (pdf 887). He is also *Still Yours*' man, so he stays out of the reading.
