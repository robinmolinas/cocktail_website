📜 **Hester:** Wren, I withdraw Uyeda, and it was my own source that convinced me. Klemm's "the show his customers liked" (Proper p. 208) is the side people see. Tomás is right that pride in precision is the Sculptor's lane. **New pick: Joe Sheridan and the Irish Coffee** (Oxford IRISH COFFEE, pdf 1075–1077). He was a chef at the Foynes flying-boat station. In late 1944 or early 1945 he was asked to make something special to greet dignitaries touring the site. He called it "Gaelic Coffee". By 1947 it was offered to every traveller getting off a plane at Shannon (pdf 1076), which meant strangers passing through who were gone by morning. In 1953 a travel writer took it to the Buena Vista in San Francisco, where they had to reverse-engineer it. Most of the world now credits the café that copied it (COFFEE COCKTAIL, pdf 538). Fame watered it down: syrup instead of sugar cubes, a bigger glass than his 180 ml goblet, canned cream. Revival bars now take pride in making it "the way Sheridan did" (pdf 1077). The care is all in parts nobody sees. Tomás, Oxford's recipe starts by warming the glass with hot water and pouring it away, before anything goes in (pdf 1077). It's hot, it comforts, and it carries cream, so dairy. Fences on this story: the famous version, with cold passengers off a flight that turned back, isn't in our sources. Oxford says dignitaries. That version is a legend: labelled or left out. His motive isn't on record. "Never saw most of them drink it" is our inference from a transit stop, not a quote. Runner-up: Lauchlan Rose. In 1867 a law made British merchant ships carry lime juice against scurvy, and that same year Rose patented an alcohol-free way to keep it (Oxford A GIMLET, pdf 887). It was made so that sailors he'd never meet stayed well. It's a cold drink, and the patent reads as business, so it's weaker on the person.
