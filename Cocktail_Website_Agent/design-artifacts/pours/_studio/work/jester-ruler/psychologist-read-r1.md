# Wren's read: The Straight Man (jester-ruler), round 1

Persona source: `persona.py jester-ruler` (essence "the reliable foil to your moments of fun"; goal Pleasure, fear Boredom; drivers build connection / PROVIDE STRUCTURE; Laurel & Hardy).

## The one true thing (no sibling shares it)
**They're funny by not laughing.** The laugh lands on the other person and comes off them. Their fun is in holding still while someone else goes wild, and in knowing the act wouldn't work without them. Every other Jester gets their laugh by doing something. This one gets it by refusing to, on purpose and with great skill.

Plain-words version for the guest: everyone calls you the sensible one. You're having the best time in the room.

## What they hide
- That they're enjoying it more than anyone. The straight face is a choice, and it takes work.
- That they **need** the chaos. Put them in a room where everyone is sensible and they're bored stiff. They gravitate to the loudest person at the table. People assume they put up with it. They picked it.
- What they'd never say out loud: "I need you to be ridiculous."

## What they fear
- **Boredom** (the persona's own word): the night where everyone behaves, or the friend who made it fun moving on.
- **Being taken at face value** (the Ruler half): mistaken for the grown-up, the one who doesn't get it, the killjoy. Being the frame and never being seen in the picture.
- Chaos with nobody holding it: the act falling apart because nobody played it straight.

## What they give (say it out loud once, per Robin's caregiver edits)
People are at their funniest next to them. They give the wild friend somewhere to land: a straight line to bounce off, a shape for the evening, the steady face that turns a mess into a joke. The other person gets the laugh; the Straight Man is why it was there to get.

## Traps
1. **The boring one.** Never call them boring, sensible-as-dull or the killjoy, even as other people's view, without turning it in the same breath.
2. **Grievance about credit.** The story has credit running the wrong way; the guest must never read resentment into it. They don't want the credit; they want the act to work. (Credit-and-blame is the Auteur's.)
3. **Rules and limits.** The Nanny's ground: no measures, no "Dont Drink too Many".
4. **Control freak.** "Running it" is the frame and the steadiness, never control over people.
5. **A partner required.** The double act is any friend, sibling, colleague: "the loudest person at the table". Never romantic, never a single fixed partner.
6. **Gender.** "Straight Man" is only the personality name; no "man" about the guest, and "behind every great man" is out.
7. **The drink already exists.** *Word Gets Round* (regular-guy-ruler, draft, 2026-10-01) is a bourbon Rickey: bourbon, lime, soda, no sugar, crushed ice, with a lime-or-lemon choice. The plan's reserved drink is that exact cocktail. See verdict.

## Story test
A double act in drink history where (a) one partner is the loud, famous one and the other the steady one; (b) the steady one's quiet move is what made it work, so the punchline lands off them; (c) the steady one stayed with it; (d) the loud one is laughed with, never at; (e) the steady one's enjoyment is signposted ("I like to think"). Out: rules, sole authorship, grievance.

## Verdict on the plan's lead: ACCEPT, with conditions
Read whole: Oxford RICKEY pdf 1661–1664, SHOOMAKER'S pdf 1789; *Imbibe!* pdf 117–118; *Joy* pdf 376.

It passes, and it's funnier than the plan says. The loud one: a lobbyist who "instructed bartenders everywhere how to make it" (*Imbibe!* 117), gave at least three published origin stories (Oxford 1662), and told the *Herald* he "was not the author" (Oxford 1662). The steady one: Shoo's "beloved head barkeeper" (*Imbibe!* 117), its "president" (Oxford 1663), head bartender there for much of the decades to 1917 (Oxford 1789), with "a long association with the Rickey" (Oxford 1663). The punchline: the drink caught on with lime, which Rickey disliked, and Rickey put the change down to one of the bartenders, "more than likely George Williamson" (Oxford 1663). The town ordered it under the colonel's name, made the bartender's way. And Oxford's own deadpan: whether Rickey actually drank it or not, his friends did (1663). That's the double act, and the steady one's lime is the raised eyebrow.

Conditions:
1. **Not a credit story.** The reading tells a double act, not an injustice. "Some said" for authorship (Oxford: Sander, a stranger with limes, Williamson, Rickey; *Imbibe!*: Rickey "invented"; *Joy* via Crockett: "invented and named for him").
2. **Rickey credited "one of the bartenders", not Williamson.** Williamson is "more than likely" (Oxford). Never "Rickey credited Williamson". His 1893 "not the author" pointed to Sander, not to the bar.
3. **"Rickey disliked lime" is Rickey's own account** (Oxford 1663); Brown's story has him approving a lime one. Hester to say whether it's a conflict; if so it's attributed ("by his own account") or dropped.
4. **Don't say Rickey owned the bar when the lime came in.** *Imbibe!* says he owned it when Williamson started making them; Oxford says he owned it 1883–1886 and the lime came "in the late 1880s". Contested timing: out.
5. **Williamson's enjoyment: "I like to think".** "President" in quotes: no gloss unless Hester finds one.
6. **Out:** Rickey's death and money, Confederate service, gambling, bawdy pictures, "blood-cooling" and "less intoxicating" (health), the Kansas joke (dry-state ground), Shoo's closing night (Williamson died 1915), "Dont Drink too Many". The gin-ricksha pun: one clause at most.
7. **Tomás, the drink can't be *Word Gets Round*.** A Rickey as a category is free (Robin 2026-09-28: only the exact same cocktail is off-limits), but bourbon, lime, soda, no sugar, over ice is that pour's cocktail. And its twist is the lime-or-lemon choice, so ours never offers the guest a lemon-or-lime choice. What the person needs from the glass: two partners, one loud and one steady, with the steady one's touch the thing that makes it work, served deadpan (no show). A lead for you, on the page: Shoo's had its own rye, "commonly used in the whisky version of a Rickey" (Oxford 1789). That's the bar's own whiskey beside the colonel's "reportedly" bourbon, if the shape table allows. The spark and the balance are yours. y4 must also stay off *Word Gets Round*'s phrases ("official cocktail of Washington", "spread across America", "nowhere to hide", Regan's secret of the lime).

Backup (vermouth and the 1952 jokes) stays a backup: an ingredient can't enjoy anything, so the persona's heart would be all signpost.

## Position (direction for v1)
Let them catch you laughing. The person they've been playing to all along is you, and your laugh is the one they're after. What the guest gains: the fun is shared out loud, and they're seen in the act, not only as its frame. (Wording to stay off *There It Goes*'s "laugh out loud" and *Unrehearsed*'s "straight face".)
