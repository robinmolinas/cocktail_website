## whoYouAre

Someone says "but that's how it's always been", and you feel it before you can explain it. It's still standing, but it stopped being alive a while ago: the trip nobody enjoys any more, the group that meets because it always has, the way of working everyone defends and nobody would choose today. Sooner or later you're the one who says so. Then you're the one who ends it, and the one they're cross with.

Here's the thing, though: you only ever break the things you love. Nobody fights that hard over something they don't care about. And afterwards you miss it, often more than the ones who were angry with you. You tell no one, in case it's taken for an apology. Your real fear is quieter than any row. It's something good being kept going out of politeness, year after year, until nobody remembers why it mattered, and you, who saw it, said nothing.

And it's worth saying plainly: because of you, the thing they loved gets a second life, the cross ones included. They rarely thank you at the time. But what grows back is usually better, and very often it's still theirs. That's love too: the kind that's willing to end things.

## yours

1. Angelo Gaja's family house in Barbaresco, in north-west Italy, was founded in 1859. He's credited with bringing Barbaresco, a wine made from the Nebbiolo grape, out of obscurity. His single-vineyard wines became famous.

2. But as they grew famous, the family's own Barbaresco, always the house's "historic wine", started being called "normale", the lesser bottle. "Which I never intended," he said. So, starting with the 1996 vintage, he took those single-vineyard wines out of Barbaresco's top classification, down to a plainer regional name. In his words, it was "in support of Barbaresco", so that the house would have one Barbaresco only. It also freed him to add a little Barbera, which the rules didn't allow, for freshness, he said. So there were two reasons, and both were his. It shocked the wine world, and critics mourned Barbaresco's reputation.

3. I like to think the first reason was the one that mattered to him: his own famous wines taken down a rank, so the house's own would stop being second best. And look at what grew back. Barbaresco's other producers held to what it was, and a wine writer living in Italy judged that it gave Barbaresco a sense of identity. Then, from the 2013 vintage, his children brought those three wines back into Barbaresco, all Nebbiolo now, with his support. "Every generation has its own path to follow," his daughter Gaia said.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a grappa Old-Fashioned: the famous classic, built on young grappa, Italy's spirit made from the skins, seeds and stems left once grapes are pressed for wine. Its only sweetness is a syrup of young Langhe Nebbiolo, warmed gently with sugar and never boiled. As it happens, that's the plainer name his famous wines were moved down to. I chose it as a nod to the family wine he put first, the one people had started calling basic: here, a plain young wine is what holds the drink together. Then orange bitters, two dashes, and one big cube; it's stirred only until the tumbler feels cold. No garnish. Strong and cold, with the red wine underneath.

5. When you next end something you love, say why while you're doing it: that it's for the thing's sake. Then leave the rebuilding to someone else, even if they bring back something that looks a lot like the old way. That isn't your break being undone. It's what it was for. And you get to love the thing again, without carrying it alone.
