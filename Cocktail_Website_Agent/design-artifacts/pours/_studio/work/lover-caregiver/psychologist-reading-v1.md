## whoYouAre

Someone's coming over. Long before they get there, you've been at it: the chair moved nearer the heater for the one who always feels the cold, the music down to where people can talk, the light just so. By the time they walk in, it looks as if it all happened by itself. That's the idea. Nobody should feel they put you to any trouble. They should just feel expected.

What you'd never say out loud is: I was hoping you'd come. All that getting ready is how you hope without asking. A room made ready can't be turned down, and a plain question can. The thought you don't let yourself stay with is the ready room with nobody in it. Here's what people take home from your evenings, even when they couldn't say why: the feeling that someone had them in mind before they arrived. Very few people are ever given that.

## yours

1. In New York, back when hotel guests checked in at the bar, the City Hotel's desk clerk and its bartender were the same man, Orsamus Willard. People told a story about him. A guest sailed to England, stayed ten years, and walked back into the bar. Willard greeted him by name, and remembered his old room number.

2. He almost never left the hotel, and he'd do anything he could to make a guest comfortable. He left no book and no account of his own life: it took a historian a great deal of digging to find even the basics. When he went home to Massachusetts, his house had a number on every room door. I like to think he was still getting rooms ready, for guests who might or might not come.

3. That's the part of him I see in you. Not the famous memory. The rooms made ready anyway.

4. One person he served did write him down. A British army captain who drank at his bar printed a few recipes in his travel book, and gave one of them to Willard by name: the Apple Toddy, a drink so loved in America for over a century that it stood for the country itself, and then all but vanished. The cocktail I've made for you follows Willard's: the best apples rolled in wet paper and roasted until soft, then brandy, water, a little sugar and one lump of ice. For the brandy, I took the grape brandy the historian David Wondrich believes Willard preferred. Nearly all of it happens before anyone arrives: the apples roast, cool and wait. I kept it that way as a nod to you, because that's where your care always starts.

5. So next time you're getting a room ready, add the one thing you always leave out. Ask. Not "come by sometime": the plain sentence, to the person you're hoping will come. Getting ready is how you hope. Asking is how they find out they were hoped for. And I wish you a full house.
