📜 **Hester:** Wren, I checked both of your *Imbibe!* finds on the page, and they hold. They're carded in `_studio/fact-cards/orsamus-willard.md`. Three corrections come with them.

**The dates.** The two books agree on four things: he was born in 1792, he left the bar in 1848, he died in 1876, and his house had numbers on every room door (Oxford pdf 2193–94 has "number plates"; *Imbibe!* pdf 22 has "room numbers on every door"). They disagree on everything else: when he started (1811 or 1816), the julep year, and whether he finally went home in 1848 or 1849. That last one also means we can't date the doors. My ruling is that the reading can say he retired once, came back when the hotel had failed, and stayed behind the bar until 1848. No other dates, no "twenty years", and nothing dated about the house.

**The ten-year guest is a story told about him, not an event on record.** Oxford brings it in with "Stories of his legendary feats of memory were common, such as…" (pdf 2193). So signpost it as the story people told of him.

**Your two finds.** Both are on the page, and both need a guard.
- pdf 20 says his first name was hardly ever used. He was "Mr. Willard, if you were being formal", and the "perhaps because it was Orsamus" joke is Wondrich's, so credit it to him if we use it. The better limb is pdf 22: he "left behind no book of recipes or biographical sketch", and even the basic facts of his life took a historian "a great deal of digging". He remembered everyone, and almost nobody wrote him down. That's our reading, not the page's, but it rests on two sourced facts.
- "Repaid the favor" (pdf 175) is also Wondrich's joke. The favour was Willard mixing the captain's drinks, and why the captain wrote the recipes down isn't on record. So the care coming back is our reading. And "the only reason we can make his drinks" goes too far: the page says four of his recipes survived through that book, no more.

**The better story.** The captain's own book credits the Apple-Toddy to Willard by name. It calls him the bar-keeper who "never forgets the face of a customer" (1833). I have that from the Chanticleer Society's transcription, fetched today, so it's secondary for now. I'll read *Transatlantic Sketches* itself before that quote goes in the reading.

**Tomás, this settles your hot-or-iced question.** In that transcription, Willard's own toddy has the apples wrapped in wet brown paper and roasted in the embers, then "a lump of ice". His is the cold one. The hot, half-a-baked-apple version is Jerry Thomas's (1862). So a hot glass can't be called "his". The transcription also drops the spirit from its fourths, so "grape brandy" rests on Wondrich (pdf 139) until I've read the page.

**One new guard.** Never call the captain "Alexander" on his own. The Pure Heart's sibling pour tells Cato Alexander's story, and the surname would read as Cato. Say "a British army captain".

**On asking.** Oxford says he was "lured out of retirement" and names nobody. So "someone came for him" is inference, and the record never has Willard doing the asking. Wren, that suits your position as long as the reading doesn't pin the ask on him.
