## whoYouAre

Someone's coming over. Long before they get there, you've been at it: the chair moved nearer the heater for the one who always feels the cold, the music down to where people can talk, the light just so. By the time they walk in, it looks as if it all happened by itself. That's the idea. Nobody should feel they put you to any trouble. They should just feel expected.

What you'd never tell them is: I was hoping you'd come. All that getting ready is how you hope without asking. A room made ready can't be turned down, and a plain question can. The thought you don't let yourself stay with is the ready room with nobody in it. Here's what people take home from your evenings, even when they couldn't say why: the feeling that someone had them in mind before they arrived. Very few people are ever given that.

## yours

1. In New York, back when hotel guests checked in at the bar, the City Hotel's desk clerk and its bartender were the same man, Orsamus Willard. People told a story about him. A guest sailed to England, stayed ten years, and walked back into the bar. Willard greeted him by name, and remembered his old room number.

2. They said he almost never left the hotel, and that he'd do anything he could to make a guest comfortable. He left no book and no account of his own life, and even the plain facts of it are hard to find. When he went home to Massachusetts, his house had a number on every room door. I like to think he was still getting rooms ready, for guests who might or might not come.

3. That's where I see you in him. Not the famous memory. The rooms made ready anyway.

4. One of his guests did write his toddy down. A British army captain who drank at his bar printed a few recipes in his travel book, and credited one of them to Willard by name: the Apple Toddy, one of the renowned drinks that made the American bar famous abroad. After Prohibition, it all but disappeared. The cocktail I've made for you is made after Willard's: the best apples rolled in wet paper and roasted until soft, then equal measures of apple, brandy and water, as he had them, and one lump of ice. His called for as much sugar as brandy, far too sweet by today's taste, so I kept it to a spoonful and a half. For the brandy, I took the grape brandy the historian David Wondrich believes Willard preferred. Nearly all of it happens before anyone arrives: the apples roast, cool and wait. I kept it that way as a nod to you, because that's where your care always starts.

5. So next time you're getting a room ready, add the one thing you always leave out. Ask. Not "come by sometime": the plain sentence, to the person you're hoping will come. Getting ready is how you hope. Asking is how they find out they were hoped for. And I wish you a full house.

## Resonance test (v3, round 6; unchanged in substance from v2)

**The "this is me" line:** *"What you'd never tell them is: I was hoping you'd come."* It names the hidden thing (the hope inside the getting-ready), not the visible one (hosting well), so it recognises instead of praising.

- **As the guest (the Geisha).** The heater-chair scene is small and exact, and it starts from their own sheet (noticing the cold before the guest does). "A room made ready can't be turned down, and a plain question can" is the turn they'll feel caught by, gently. The kindness is said out loud, once: "the feeling that someone had them in mind before they arrived. Very few people are ever given that." They leave valued, not diagnosed.
- **As the sceptic.** Barnum check, sentence by sentence: "you like having people over" alone would fit anyone, but no sentence says only that. Every line is tied to preparation-as-hope or to being expected. Swap test against the ten siblings and the Caregiver neighbours: see the table above. The nearest are the True Friend (receiving, not ours), the Craftsman (showing effort, not ours) and the Understanding Spouse (one traveller, not ours). All clear.
- **The story as mirror.** y1 is the guest expected after ten years. y2 is the house made ready (signposted). y4 is the care coming back: the one person who wrote him down was someone he'd served. The drink is the proof, because it's ready before anyone arrives. Each beat mirrors one limb of the person. None of them is a list of connections.
- **The position is earned, and in their interest.** It asks for one sentence, not a change of character, and it ends on what they gain ("how they find out they were hoped for") and a warm wish. The ask is never pinned on Willard (F7). The closing line (Tomás r5: *Roast the apples. While they cool, call the one you'd most like to see.*) agrees with y5's position, not the creed, and it's true every time the drink is made.
- **Tone.** Warm, not solemn. There's a small smile in "That's the idea".
- **History weight:** y1 58 + y2 76 + the story half of y4 about 80 = about 214 of 524 words across whoYouAre and yours (about 41%). Under half.
- **Punch.** Strongest: the whoYouAre's second paragraph. Weakest: y3, which is short on purpose. It's the hinge, and it holds only because y2 ends on the signposted doors.
- **Guards held:** no gender, no archetype names, no geisha or Japan, no drink-in-hand, no "yours" alone for the drink, "I" only, no dates beyond the story, never "Alexander", no photographic memory or fame titles, no death.

**Verdict: ready**, pending Hester's re-audit of the changed lines (epigraph, y2, y3, y4) and the lint on the assembled v3.
