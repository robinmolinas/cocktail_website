## whoYouAre

The last train's cancelled, and there are twelve of you on a cold platform. Someone sighs. You make a joke, it lands, and somewhere in the middle of it there's a plan, and everyone hears it: the café that's still open, the walk across town, a bet on who gets home first. Years later, it's the night everyone brings up.

Nobody calls it being in charge, because to them it feels like being asked along. You like it like that. Being obeyed bores you, and it would turn you into the boss. You seldom admit how much you enjoy having your hand on the wheel, and that when you make that first joke, you don't yet know it'll work. What you dread is the night it gets away from you, or the night the fun runs out and you're just someone telling people where to go. And look what you give them: around you, a plan falling apart is where the good part begins. People go further than they ever would on their own, and they come back glad they went.

## yours

1. Six hundred pounds bought you three weeks at the London Academy of Bartenders. It opened in 1996, and it never had enough money. One of its founders, Alex Turner, said they were always one student short of being able to run the next course, so the founders worked bar jobs at night and taught in the mornings. Eventually the school went bust.

2. The name didn't. Douglas Ankrah and Richard Hargroves had a bar called LAB as well, meant, by one account, as a living advertisement for the school. The school didn't survive. The bar did, and it was never a quiet advert. Its owners wanted a party bar, loud and busy every night. Ankrah said other bars were "very, very passively boring". Jamie Terrell, whom Ankrah brought in, remembered what they were after in all that noise: "the best drinks in the world". The two of them wrote five cocktail menus together, with two hundred drinks on each.

3. It could have gone quiet. Early in 1999 it was slow, and hardly anyone noticed the place until good press turned it. After that, one bartender said everybody in the trade used to be there, to hire, to get hired, or to meet the drinks firms' reps, and it helped make bartenders into names people knew. Someone who saw it remembered the bartenders sipping a drink through a straw, then adding "a bit more mint, a little more sugar, a dash of bitters". My favourite detail: in a room that loud, someone was still tasting. I like to think you'd have got on with them. When a plan falls through, you don't mourn it. You throw the party, and you mean the party to get somewhere.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a vodka Daiquiri, built like a Mojito, in the kind of big glass LAB served so many of its drinks in, with crushed ice heaped above the rim. I chose raspberries for it, blended cold into a syrup, so the bold red comes from the fruit alone, with a pinch of toasted cumin in the syrup, a turn you won't see coming that works out, and lime and mint leaves that stay in: bright and generous. Then it's over to you. When you make it, taste it through the straw and make one of those three calls: a few more mint leaves, a little more syrup, or a dash of bitters. That step is there as a nod to those bartenders tasting in all that noise, and the drink is built so that whichever call you make, it holds.

5. Before you go, a thought, and I mean it kindly. Somewhere near you is someone who never makes the first joke, because you always do. When the evening next falls apart, wait a moment. Leave it to them, and follow wherever it goes. You won't lose the wheel. What you'll get is something you almost never have: the fun of not knowing where you're going while someone else steers. I hope it's somewhere wonderful.

## Resonance (v2)

**The "this is me" line:** *"Nobody calls it being in charge, because to them it feels like being asked along."* It's the line I'd stop at as this guest. It names the thing they do on purpose, and it names it kindly. Runner-up: *"when you make that first joke, you don't yet know it'll work."*

**As the guest.** The platform is mine. I've done exactly that, and the plan really was in the joke. "Being obeyed bores you" is true, and I've never heard anyone say it. The two fears are my two nightmares, and the gift sentence gives me the thing I'd never claim for myself. LAB isn't a saint's story: it went bust, it was slow, it was loud. And the two quotes on one page are me, the party and the best drinks in the world at once. The glass is the party, the cumin is the turn that works out, and the straw is my hand on the wheel, in front of everyone. y5 asks me to give up the thing I'm best at for one night, and then tells me what I'd gain. I'd try it. *Who's In?* is what I'd actually say on that platform.

**As the sceptic.** Horoscope: the platform is a scene, "being obeyed bores you" leaves out most of the family, and the hidden pleasure in the wheel is the Ruler's alone. Swap test: *Anyway* (the good night decided and made) has no destination; here the fun is going somewhere, with no flop list and no "anyway". *Go On* keeps the peace; nobody's fighting here. *No Accident*'s plan is private and "three moves ahead"; this plan is said out loud, everyone hears it, and the straw step happens in the open (Hester's fence). *For Good*: the setback isn't forgotten, it's turned. *Off Duty*: no "still deciding". *Worth the Trip*: same ingredient, a different cocktail (ginger line, cooked, gin highball). The hidden-work stock ("effortless", unseen hours) is absent: the five menus are an aim on the record. Magnetic Leader (unauthored): no "they'd leave without you".
