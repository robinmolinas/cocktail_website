| setback | A school for bartenders, opened in 1996, was so short of money that it was always one student short of affording the next course; the founders worked bar jobs at night (whose bars, the page doesn't say) and taught in the morning. It went bust (*Proper* p. 99, Turner on the record; F2–F4). | A real failure, not a hiccup: it could have ended the whole thing. | the setback that could have ended it |
| turn | Its founders opened a bar under the same name; the book says it was meant as a living advertisement for the school, and one founder said they opened it because the school didn't work out. The school didn't survive; the bar did (p. 99; F5). Order-neutral only. | The party had a plan inside it from the first night: it was there to sell something. | fun and control at once; a party with a purpose |
| decision | The owners chose what kind of bar it would be: a party bar, loud and busy, not a quiet temple to the craft (p. 99, Simonson's narration; F6). | The fun was decided, not stumbled into. The steering shows as a choice. | the steering, as an uncontested decision |
| fear (in his words) | Douglas Ankrah, one of its founders, said other bars were "very, very passively boring" (*quotable*, p. 100; F7). Past tense: he died in 2021 (*Spirits Business*, 17 Aug 2021; F16). | Dull is the enemy, in the founder's own mouth. | the fear of dull |
| aim | One of its bartenders, Jamie Terrell, said they wanted to make "the best drinks in the world" (*quotable*, p. 100); he and Ankrah wrote five cocktail menus together, each with two hundred drinks (F9). Never "a thousand". | Ambition inside the noise: the party was going somewhere. Shown as an aim, never as hours nobody saw. | the steering as an aim; going somewhere |
| the wheel (optional) | Someone who saw it remembered the bartenders tasting through a straw and then adding a little more mint, a little more sugar, a dash of bitters, adjusting the drink (the page says "the drink", never "each" or "every") (Simon Ford, pp. 100–101; F11, cut before "free pour", C1). Plural, never Ankrah alone. | The hand on the wheel in a loud room: control as constant small corrections. | control inside the fun |
| edge | At first nobody took much notice; the first months of 1999 were slow, until good press turned it (p. 101; F13). The service could be slow too: a founder who had left joked you should order a beer with your cocktail, to have something to drink while you waited (Turner, p. 101; F12). | It could have gone dull, or tipped into a mess, and nearly did. | the edge owned (dull and chaos) |
| joined | One bartender said everybody in the trade used to be there: to be hired, to hire, to meet a drinks firm's rep (Conigliaro, p. 101). The book credits it with helping knit London's bartenders together and with making bartenders (the page doesn't limit it to London's) celebrities rather than wage slaves (F14); Oxford calls it a breeding ground for new talent (pdf 119; F15). | People came in numbers, willingly, and went further than they would have alone. | people followed because it was exciting; the gift |
| glass | Many of its drinks came in a big glass of crushed ice, about 14 oz (Turner, who had left before the bar opened, says "everything", p. 100; the same pages mention its Martinis, p. 101, so never "everything"), built on vodka and fresh fruit, with "mountains of ice" (Simonson, p. 100; F10). | Loud and generous on top; Tomás's exact balance underneath. | the drink as proof |
