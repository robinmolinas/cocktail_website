# Persona card: The Charismatic Leader (ruler-jester)

Wren, round 1, 2026-10-01. Sources: the xlsx row via `persona.py ruler-jester`, the six drafted Ruler siblings, the registry, the Ruler plan row, and *A Proper Drink* pp. 97–101, which I re-read whole this round.

## The one true thing
**Their joke is how they take charge.** Something goes wrong: the train's cancelled, the booking's lost, it rains on the day. Before anyone has worked out how cross to be, this person has made the first joke, and the joke already has a plan inside it ("Right. Change of plan."). By the end it's the night people still talk about, and almost nobody noticed they'd been led. They were invited, and that's how it felt.

So it isn't fun as a gift (the Morale Booster) and it isn't warmth (the Magnetic Leader). It's **fun as the steering**. They'd rather be followed than obeyed. People who come because it's exciting come willingly, and to this person being obeyed is dull, and it turns them into the boss.

## What they hide
- **They love being in charge**, more than they'd admit. The fun lets them hold the wheel without anyone having to call it that, including them.
- While they're laughing, they're working out the sums: the last train home, who's paying, what happens if it rains again. I'm naming this as **steering**, never as hidden effort (see Traps).
- At the moment they make the joke, they don't know yet that it'll work.

## What they fear
Two ways it can fall apart, and they're both the persona's own: **chaos** (the adventure gets away from them, with them leading) and **dull** (the fun goes, and they're just someone giving orders, so people do as they're told or drift off). Underneath both is the Ruler's fear of revolt: the moment the room stops following.

## What they give (said out loud, per Robin's rule)
Around them, a setback becomes the start of something. People say yes to things they'd never have chosen, and they enjoy getting there. They go further than they would have alone, and when they get back they're bigger than when they set out, because someone made it safe to be adventurous.

## Scenes (ways in, not labels)
The cancelled train and the twelve people on a platform. The barbecue rained off that turns into a living-room picnic. The team's launch slips a month, and they turn the empty month into something. No job titles, and nothing about alcohol in whoYouAre.

## Traps
1. **The hidden-work trap.** My plan test had "cost: nobody saw the work". It's owned four times over. The steering is shown as decisions and aims, never as hours nobody saw.
2. **The forgotten-setback trap** (For Good).
3. **Party as gift** (Anyway). Our guest is going somewhere.
4. **The showman only.** A reading that's all big gestures and never shows the wheel.
5. **The boss.** No job titles, no "leader", no "charismatic" (that's the personality's name and Oxford's word for Bilgray).
6. **Tone.** This one should be allowed to be funny. A solemn reading would misread them.
7. **Manipulation.** "Nobody noticed they'd been led" must read as generous, never as a trick: they lead where people are glad to go.

## Story test (revised from the plan)
1. A real setback on the page that could have ended it.
2. A big, fun answer that people joined, in numbers (no stunt nobody followed).
3. The steering inside the fun, shown as a decision or an aim on the page, uncontested (not hidden hours).
4. Fun and control at once, ideally in the story's own words.
5. The edge owned: it could have tipped into chaos or gone dull.
Out: pranks, comfort humour, peacekeeping with jokes, the fix that vanishes, effort nobody saw.

## Verdict on the plan's lead (LAB)
**Accept LAB, re-aimed.** After re-reading pp. 97–101 whole:
- Limb 1: "We were always one student away from not being able to run the following course" (p. 99); the school went bust.
- Limb 2: the bar, "intended as a living advertisement for the school", became the place where "Everybody in the industry used to be" (Conigliaro, p. 101). It established "bartenders as celebrities, not wage slaves" (p. 101), so they took the whole trade up with them. That's the Ruler's gift of standing, given to everyone.
- Limbs 3–4, the mirror I'd build on: two lines on facing pages. Ankrah: "Other bars are very, very passively boring … LAB was a bar for people who rock every night" (p. 100). Terrell: "At the time, we wanted to make the best drinks in the world. Doug and I wrote five different cocktail menus together, and each of those menus had two hundred drinks on it" (p. 100). The party and the ambition at once. The owners chose it: "They wanted a party bar, high volume and high energy" (p. 99). Ankrah's "passively boring" is this guest's fear of dull, in his own mouth.
- Limb 5: "The first few months of 1999 were slow", until the *Time Out London* review (p. 101). The chaos edge: "freewheeling", drinks "built on the fly" (p. 100), and Turner's own joke "order two drinks … so you've got something to drink while they're making your cocktail" (p. 101). It's funny and true, though he was a visitor by then (he left before the bar opened), so it's attributed, if it's used at all.

**Changes to the row:**

- "Not many know LAB began a school at all" (p. 99) stays out of the recognition (For Good). One clause at most.
- **Stale guard:** Ford's quotes were kept out "because both lead other Ruler rows", but Ford was released in step 4. His memory of the bartenders "putting straws in and sipping, then adding a bit more mint, a little more sugar … adjusting the drink" (pp. 100–101) is the steering inside the noise. Hester rules. If it's used, it's cut before "very much free pour" (contested beside the jiggers). Collins's line stays out.

- The bar's opening year isn't on these pages (1996 is the school). No year for the bar unless Hester finds one. And no opening on "London, late 1990s" (Collins's y1 opens "In 1990s London").
