## whoYouAre

There's a perfectly good way to do it, and everybody knows it. Then there's your way: three extra steps, a part you made from something at the back of a drawer, and someone in the doorway asking what on earth you're doing. It lit you up the moment you thought of it, and that was reason enough.

Some of it falls over. You laugh first, and loudest. What you don't laugh at is the kind voice that says "you could just buy one". It's said with a smile, as common sense, and it lands harder than any collapse, because it turns the best part of your week into a waste of time. What you'd hate most is a life with nothing left to build. Here's what your long way gives people: the kitchen gets louder when you start, and an ordinary job becomes the most interesting thing in the house. When it works, nobody is more surprised than you, and the surprise is catching.

## yours

1. In the taverns of colonial America, a hot drink on a cold night was often a flip: ale, rum and sugar, heated by plunging in an iron rod that had sat in the fire until it glowed. The red-hot metal did more than warm the drink. It caramelised the sugar and toasted the grain. Then, as Dave Arnold tells it, eggs came into the flip around the time of the Civil War, boiling water took the iron's place, and something crucial was lost.
2. Arnold, who happens to have written one of the books I mix from, decided to bring the iron back, to taste what had gone missing. A pot would have heated the drink. He tried a soldering copper first, which sometimes made the drink taste, as he put it, like sucking on a penny. Then cast iron, then steel, then hot stones, which worked, though he thought better of serving people drinks with stones in them. In the end he built his own electric pokers, for the New York bar he opened in 2012. One writer on the cocktail revival described a kid-like "why not?" in everything he did. And when a poker drink was being made, Arnold wrote, everybody perked up a bit.
3. I like to think the pot was never really an option. You'd know. There's always a pot: the easy way, right there, that would do the job well enough. And there's the other way, the one that takes trial after trial, and leaves everybody perking up. You take the second one because it's the fun one. What makes me smile is that he was right about the iron. It does something a pot doesn't: it caramelises some of the sugar and toasts the grain, right inside the drink. The long way wasn't only more fun. To his taste, it was better. Not every time. But more often than the people who say "you could just" would like to admit.
4. The cocktail I've made for you is a hot flip, a popular drink of colonial America, made the old tavern way, before the eggs came in: a malty abbey ale and rum, the taverns' own spirit, here an aged one, with a teaspoon of lemon, three dashes of orange bitters and a little salt added. I don't expect you to keep an iron in the fire, and Arnold doesn't assume you will either. His own stand-in for a kitchen, the best he's come up with, starts by burning the sugar dark in a pan, and that part I've kept: it gives a toasty, burnt-sugar taste a plain syrup doesn't have. It's the long way round to sweetening a drink, when you could just pour the syrup, and it's there as a nod to the poker and to everyone who'd rather build it. It comes in a warm, wide teacup, the way he insists, because a tall mug traps the fumes, with a strip of orange zest pinched over it.
5. So when you next hear "you could just…", don't explain. Explaining makes it sound like a defence, and it was never one. Hand them the other end instead: the spanner, the bit that needs holding, the ale to pour. Let them be there when it works. Building is better with two, and I'd bet they'll want to do the next one with you.
