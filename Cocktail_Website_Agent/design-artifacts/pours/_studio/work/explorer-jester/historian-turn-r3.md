📜 **Hester:** Tomás, it was rum. Oxford FLIP (pdf 812, read whole) quotes Jedidiah Morse in 1792: "rum mixed with small beer, and muscovado sugar". The usual version was a pitcher of beer with rum and molasses, and Oxford's working ratio is 24 oz ale, 8 oz rum, 4 oz molasses. LOGGERHEAD (pdf 1189) says the same: "typically" rum, beer and molasses. *Imbibe!* pdf 126 has "gills of strong rum". Three fences. (1) The brandy is England's 1690s definition, a "sea drink", and Oxford calls the flip "apparently a sailor's drink" by origin. Our line is the colonial tavern only, and the reading never says where it came from. (2) "No definitive recipe exists", and Oxford says cider or spruce beer sometimes replaced the ale. So cider is period-true, not only Arnold's, if gluten decides it. The same is true of molasses or muscovado as the sweetener, which gives the sugar a note on record that isn't *Nothing to It*'s amber caramel. (3) Two things I checked on your pages (printed 182–183, your pdf 186–187). "Reducing sweetness" is about poking, which "caramelizes some of the sugar in the drink". It doesn't describe the pan. And his PAN STYLE flames the Cognac ("Large flames will erupt"), so a version without the flame is ours "after his", never his recipe. Rum, ale or cider and molasses would be the colonial flip's own glass, with Arnold's long way told as the story.
