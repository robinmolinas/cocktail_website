## whoYouAre

There's a perfectly good way to do it, and everybody knows what it is. Then there's yours: three extra steps, a part you made yourself out of something from the back of a drawer, and someone in the doorway asking what on earth you're doing. You couldn't tell them exactly why. It lit you up the moment you thought of it, and that was reason enough.

Some of it falls over. You laugh first, and loudest, and you mean it. What you don't laugh at is the kind voice that says "you could just buy one". It's always said with a smile, as plain common sense, and it lands harder than any collapse, because it turns the best part of your week into a waste of time. Underneath it is the thing you'd hate most: a life where everything comes ready-made and there's nothing left to build. Here's what your long way gives people. The kitchen gets louder when you start. People who'd never try it themselves lean in, and for an evening an ordinary job is the most interesting thing in the house. When it works, nobody is more surprised than you, and that surprise is catching.

## yours

1. In the taverns of colonial America, a hot drink on a cold night was usually a flip: beer or cider, a spirit and sugar, heated by plunging in an iron rod that had sat in the fire until it glowed. The red-hot metal did more than warm the drink. It caramelised the sugar inside it. Then, around the time of the Civil War, eggs came into the flip, boiling water took the iron's place, and in Dave Arnold's words, something crucial was lost.
2. Arnold, who happens to have written one of the books I mix from, decided to bring the iron back, to taste what had gone missing. A pot would have heated the drink. He tried a soldering copper first, which sometimes made the drink taste, as he put it, like sucking on a penny. Then cast iron, then steel, then hot stones, which worked, though he thought better of serving people drinks with stones in them. In the end he built his own electric pokers for his bar in New York. A writer who knew him described a kid-like "why not?" in everything he does. And when a poker drink was being made, Arnold wrote, everybody perked up a bit.
3. I like to think the pot was never really an option. You'd know. There's always a pot: the easy way, right there, that would do the job well enough. And there's the other way, the one that takes a winter of trying metals and makes the whole room turn round. You take the second one because it lights you up. What makes me smile is that he was right about the iron. It really does something a pot can't, burning the sugar inside the drink. The long way wasn't only more fun. It was better. Not every time. But more often than the people who say "you could just" would like to admit.
4. The cocktail I've made for you is a hot flip, the old tavern drink as it was before the eggs came in: dry cider and an aged rum, with a little lemon and a few drops of bitters. I don't expect you to keep an iron in the fire, and neither does Arnold. His own answer for a kitchen, the one I've used, is to burn the sugar dark in a pan first, so the toasty, bittersweet taste the iron used to give goes in with it. It's the long way round to sweetening a drink, when you could just pour the syrup, and I've kept it as a nod to the poker and to everyone who'd rather build it. It comes in a wide teacup, the way he insists, so the smell of it reaches you first.
5. So when someone next says "you could just…", don't explain. Explaining makes it sound like a defence, and it was never one. Hand them the other end instead: the spanner, the bit that needs holding, the sugar to watch. Let them be there when it works. Building is better with two, and I'd bet they'll want to do the next one with you.
