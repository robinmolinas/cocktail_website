# The Lovable Rogue (hero-outlaw): persona card

Wren, round 1, 2026-10-04. Read from `persona.py hero-outlaw`, the Hero plan row, the sibling readings (whoYouAre of all eight authored Hero siblings, plus *Can't Watch*, *Just This Once*, *Accomplices*, *In One Piece*, *Till Spring*, the Knight and the Noble Ruler), and *A Proper Drink* printed pp. 237–239 re-read whole (the LeNell chapter, "Bringing It All Home").

## Core truth

**They never say they'll come, and they always do.** The Lovable Rogue keeps people's expectations low on purpose. No promises, no plan anyone can see, a joke where the reassurance should go. Then, when it counts, they're the one standing there. The roguishness isn't the opposite of their honour. It protects it. A promise would be a leash (they can't stand being told what to do), and a broken one would be unbearable (they'd hate letting a friend down more than anything). So they never make one, and they never break faith.

## Shows / hides

- **Shows:** the rough edge. Late, loose, a rule bent, a plan "made up as I go", the grin when it goes wrong. People introduce them with a warning ("don't listen to a word they say").
- **Hides:** how seriously they take the people they're loyal to, and how much thought went into the thing that looked improvised. Also that it stings, a little, when someone believes the reputation all the way, and is surprised that they came good. They'd never say so. They'd make a joke of it.

## Hidden fear → hidden gift

- **Fear (Hero + Outlaw together):** being thought soft (weakness), and being tied down by what people expect of them (restriction). Underneath both: letting someone down who was counting on them. The rogue's way out is that nobody openly counts on them, so nobody can be let down, and yet nobody is.
- **Gift, said out loud (Robin's rule):** when it really matters, people stop asking who's sensible and ask for them. They turn up where the respectable ones don't, without a speech and without a bill. And they're living proof to everyone around them that you can be rough round the edges and completely dependable. People with rough edges of their own feel welcome near them.

## The "real me" lines to aim for

1. ***"You'd rather surprise them than let them down."*** (the one I think lands hardest: it names the hidden fear and the honour in a single plain sentence, and it explains the whole act)
2. *"You never said you'd come. You always do."* (tagline candidate: invites, about the person, positive frame)
3. *"Nobody would put you in charge of the plan. Everybody wants you there when it falls apart."* (a scene line for whoYouAre; check against *Anyone Would Have* when drafting)

## Truth vs. legend

They like the legend, and they like it with a wink: grant the reputation first, the way they'd grant it themselves ("fair enough, I'm trouble"), then show what's under it. Not a Trickster reveal (no con, no "the story was a trick"). The pleasure is that the rough story and the faithful story are **both true** at once.

## Tone

Hero: assertive, encouraging. Outlaw: bold, direct. For the bartender: dry, warm, a little cheeky, never preachy. This one can be funny; the reading should have one grin in it. Gravity only at the turn (the fear of letting someone down), and short.

## Distinct from siblings (and the near neighbours)

- **The Knight** (*lover-hero*): "You said you'd come, so you come." The Knight declares it, in the good coat, with everyone watching. **The Rogue never declares it.** Same arrival, opposite announcement. That's the cleanest line in the whole family, and the reason "promise" stays out of the reading's words.
- **The Dashing Hero** (*One Line*): cool hides devotion to one person, "if the feeling showed, it might look like weakness". It owns "feeling = weakness". The Rogue's cover is a *bad name*, not polish, and what it hides is loyalty to a crew, not love for one. Don't write "afraid it would look soft" as the turn: their turn is the promise they won't make.
- **The Firefighter** (*Anyone Would Have*): prepares everything, leaves nothing to the day. The Rogue looks like they prepared nothing. (If the reading shows hidden preparation, it must stay a glimpse, never the point.)
- **The Saviour** (*Next One's Mine*): takes the cost, can't bear to owe. Not this one's wound. No "favour", no "owe", no "put it on me".
- **The Superhero** (*Day Job*): ordinary cover, extraordinary skill. The Rogue's cover is *disreputable*, not ordinary.
- **The Maverick** (*Can't Watch*, outlaw-hero, the mirror pairing): bends rules for others because they can't watch. For the Rogue, the rule-bending is just how they move; the point is faith with their people. Never "rule that isn't fair".
- **The Bad Influence** (*Accomplices*): "People call you a bad influence, and you let them." Owned. I won't write "you let them think…" or "drop the act".
- **The Scoundrel** (*Just This Once*): charm gets them let off. The Rogue isn't asking for a yes.
- **The Healer** (*Till Spring*) owns "help easy to take" and "keep turning up". Use "turn up" once at most and never "keep turning up".

## Traps

- **The bad-boy cliché:** pirates, swagger, a scoundrel's charm, any gendered rogue. No Jack Sparrow, no sea (also family ground).
- **Rule-breaking as the point,** crime, Prohibition, smuggling (Outlaw family ground). Any rule in the story is small, and done for someone else.
- **Insulting the guest:** "unreliable", "a mess", "you push people away" as flat statements. Grant the reputation as *other people's* view, then turn it.
- **Diagnosis:** "you're afraid of commitment". Say it plainly and kindly: a promise feels like a leash, and breaking one would be worse.
- **Flattery:** "heart of gold". Say what they *do*.
- **The Trickster's reveal**, *Accomplices*' "drop the act", the Knight's "you said you'd come", the Dashing Hero's "it might look like weakness".
- **Rationed Hero words:** hero, brave, fight: one at most across the reading.

## My story test (for this pairing)

A person with a doubtful reputation (temper, rule-bending, an unlikely bet) who **kept faith with specific people without ever announcing it**, at a cost to themselves; the kindness arrived rough-wrapped or off the record; any rule bent was bent *for others*, never for gain. All on the page, with no motive claimed beyond it.

## Verdict on the plan's lead: LeNell Smothers and her Red Hook shop. **Accept, with conditions.**

I re-read *A Proper Drink* pp. 237–239 whole. Clause by clause:

1. **Doubtful reputation:** passes on the page. "Instantly legendary, as was its owner, though often as much for her cantankerous personality as for her inventory"; the scoldings "almost a rite of passage"; and the shop itself was where you'd put a business if you wanted to *hide* it (end of Van Brunt Street, remote Red Hook). The shop looked like the last bet anyone would make. "People found LeNell's anyway."
2. **Faith kept, unannounced, at a cost:** passes as a *choice*, not as a loss. She "crammed her shop with what they needed": rye "when no one had rye", amari, bitters nobody else carried. Most stores "didn't bother" with stock "that didn't move quickly off the shelves", and her own words set friends buying cases of Hennessy for a good price against her "chasing down oddball cases of amaros". **Never claim she lost money.**
3. **Rule bent for others:** passes. She "flouted local liquor laws" to carry Peychaud's and Underberg, which counted as food products, so grocery-store stock. Small, for her customers, not for gain. One clause, never glamour, never "outlaw".
4. **Kindness rough-wrapped:** passes only as juxtaposition. She yelled at Damon Boelte for unwrapping the tissue-papered Red Hook Rye, and "later hired" him. **The page doesn't link the two.** So two plain facts side by side ("She yelled at him. Later, she hired him."), and anything more is signposted ("I like to think…"). **Not** "her telling-off was a kindness": that's unsourced, and the guest isn't a shouter.

**What I'm changing from my plan-review note:** the mirror is no longer "kindness disguised as a telling-off". It's **the place nobody would bet on, which had exactly what you came for, without ever saying it would.** That matches the core truth (never says they'll come, always does) better than the yelling, and it keeps the guest out of the role of someone who shouts at customers. The temper stays as the reputation, granted with a grin.

**Conditions (for Hester):**
- The lease ending is **not** her sacrifice (the landlord refused; her part in the rise is "partly"). And the reading must **not** turn on "the world caught up" ("you could buy rye anywhere"): that's *Still Yours*'s and *The Long Answer*'s ground. One closing clause at most, as impact.
- Eric Seed's telling-off stays out (the Quest Seeker's); Don Lee and St. John Frizell stay out (Explorer's); Peychaud's stays out of the glass (Sazerac, *Beside the First*).
- "Fiery Alabamian" is Simonson's phrase: attribute it or skip it. She's living: date her (interview of October 2014, book of 2016). LeNell is "she" in the story, which is fine; the guest never is.
- Boelte's "Oh my God, you worked at LeNell's!" is the lovable half on the page (the people she scolded wore it as a badge). I'd like it, paraphrased with attribution.

**For Tomás (he builds v1 in round 2, since I accept):** rye "when no one had rye", and Underberg, **the very bottle the rules said she shouldn't stock.** The bitters in the glass are the rule she bent for her customers, which is exactly this person. One possible delight for Hester to check before anyone leans on it (unsourced, so not a fact yet): Underberg comes in small single-serve bottles wrapped in paper, and the bottle Boelte got yelled at for unwrapping was wrapped in tissue paper. If it's true, it's owned in one plain clause, never a gesture the guest has to perform. The spark is Tomás's call. My only ask is that the drink looks unpromising on paper (a bitter, odd German digestif) and turns out to be the one you'd drive out to Red Hook for.

## Position (to earn, in yours; my lean, final in round 3)

- **(a), my lean:** once, say it before you do it. Tell one person you'll be there, ahead of time, and let them count on it. You've never let them down. Let them have the comfort of knowing it, instead of the surprise. (It's in their interest: the reputation finally gets to match the person. Distinct from the Knight, who already announces; this one is being asked to for the first time. The word "promise" stays out.)
- **(b):** let one kindness go out without the joke attached, and stay to be thanked. (Neighbours the Dashing Hero's "give them their line, plainly" and the Saviour's receiving. Kept as a fallback.)
