# Pour — No Promises · The Lovable Rogue (hero-outlaw)

## Cocktail

- **name:** No Promises
- **tagline:** You never said you'd come. You always do.
- **glassware:** small rocks glass (single old-fashioned, about 200 ml), one large ice cube
- **contains:** `["nuts"]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 ml | straight rye whiskey, 40-43% | any straight rye at that strength; stronger bottles run too hot over one cube |
| 7.5 ml (1½ teaspoons) | sorghum syrup | pure sorghum syrup; or pure cane syrup. Measure it: half a teaspoon either way tips the balance |
| 5 ml (1 teaspoon) | Underberg | recommended; or another digestif bitters |
| 1 strip | orange peel | |

**method**
1. Spoon the sorghum syrup and the Underberg into the glass.
2. Pour in the rye and stir with a bar spoon until the syrup has disappeared into the whiskey, about fifteen seconds. Do it before the ice: once it's cold, the syrup clings to the spoon.
3. Add one large ice cube and stir for another twenty to thirty seconds, until the outside of the glass feels cold.
4. Squeeze the strip of orange peel over the top so its oils fall on the drink, then drop it in.

**closingLine:** *Stir the sorghum in first: once it's cold, it clings. Then tell one person you'll be there, ahead of time.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*This glass holds grocery-store bitters. A Brooklyn liquor shop wasn't supposed to sell them.*

**whoYouAre**
A friend asks if you can help on moving day. "Probably," you say, with a grin that convinces nobody. On the day you're the first one there, with a van you borrowed from someone you won't name, and you leave after the last box is in. People introduce you with a warning: don't believe a word they say. You've never minded the warning.

Here's what it hides. You don't say you'll be there, because saying it out loud is a leash to you, and breaking it would be worse than anything they could call you. So you keep the bar low and clear it every time. You'd rather surprise them than let them down. And this is what your friends get from it: when something really matters, they stop asking who's sensible and call you. You're the proof that someone can be rough round the edges and completely dependable, and to them that beats a good name any day.

**yours**

1. In 2003, LeNell Smothers opened a wine and liquor shop at the far end of Van Brunt Street in Brooklyn, almost on the waterfront. One drinks writer jokes that if you wanted to hide a business from its customers, you could hardly have picked a better spot. Bartenders found it regardless. She'd filled it with what they were looking for: bourbons not distributed in New York, rye when hardly anyone stocked it, a rye bottled from Kentucky barrels she'd chosen herself, bitter Italian liqueurs, and bitters that local liquor rules said belonged in a grocery store. She stocked those anyway.

2. Most liquor shops didn't bother with bottles that sold slowly. She told that writer, in 2014, that she knew how hard many of those things were to find if you loved classic cocktails, and while friends in the trade worried about buying Hennessy by the case at a good price, she went after odd cases of amaro, the bitter liqueur Italy makes from herbs. She was famous for her shelves, and often just as famous for her temper. Being told off by her became almost an initiation for her regulars. The first time Damon Boelte walked in, he started peeling the tissue paper off one of her bottles of rye, and she yelled at him on the spot. Later, she hired him. He says people are amazed when he tells them he worked at LeNell's.

3. I like to think the shop was a lot like you. It looked like the last place you'd count on, with a sharp word waiting at the counter, and it had exactly what you came for. And the people she told off were the ones most devoted to the place. That's you, as far as I can see. You give people every reason to expect nothing, and then you're the one who's there. You never ask them to rely on you. They just find out they can.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a straight-rye Old-Fashioned. That's the great classic of whiskey, sugar and bitters, here with rye, the whiskey she stocked when hardly anyone did. I swapped the usual bitters for a teaspoon of Underberg bitters, as a nod to the rule she bent so her customers could have it. The sweetness is a spoon and a half of sorghum syrup, the pressed juice of sorghum stalks boiled down into syrup, because its sweetness sits well with rye. It's stirred over a single block of ice in a short, heavy glass, with orange peel twisted above it for its oils. Deep, dark, bitter at the edges, and exactly what you'd drive to the end of the street for.

5. So here's my one request. Next time someone you'd go anywhere for has a big day coming, skip the grin and the "probably". Say yes, plainly, the week before. You won't lose an inch of your freedom: you were always going to go. What changes is theirs. For once they get to know, instead of hope. And you might find the warning was never what they believed about you.

**closing line:** Tomás's: *Stir the sorghum in first: once it's cold, it clings. Then tell one person you'll be there, ahead of time.* It agrees with y5's position (say it before), not the creed. y5 avoids "you'll be there" and "ahead of time", so the two don't repeat each other.
