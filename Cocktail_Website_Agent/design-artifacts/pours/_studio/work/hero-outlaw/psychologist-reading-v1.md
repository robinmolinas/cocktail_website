## whoYouAre

A friend asks if you can help on moving day. "Probably," you say, with a grin that convinces nobody. On the day you're the first one there, with a van you borrowed from someone you won't name, and you leave after the last box is in. People introduce you with a warning: don't believe a word they say. You've never minded the warning.

Here's what it hides. You don't say you'll be there, because saying it out loud is a leash to you, and breaking it would be worse than anything they could call you. So you keep the bar low and clear it every time. You'd rather surprise them than let them down. And this is what your friends get from it: when something really matters, they stop asking who's sensible and call you. You're the proof that someone can be rough round the edges and completely dependable, and to them that beats a good name any day.

## yours

1. In 2003, LeNell Smothers opened a wine and liquor shop at the far end of Van Brunt Street in Brooklyn, almost on the waterfront. One drinks writer jokes that if you wanted to hide a business from its customers, you could hardly have picked a better spot. Bartenders found it regardless. She'd filled it with what they were looking for: bourbons not distributed in New York, rye when hardly anyone stocked it, a rye bottled from Kentucky barrels she'd chosen herself, Italian amari, and bitters that local liquor rules said belonged in a grocery store. She stocked those anyway.

2. Other shops didn't bother with bottles that sold slowly. She told him, in 2014, that she knew how hard those things were to find if you loved classic cocktails, and while friends in the trade bought Hennessy by the case for the price, she went after odd cases of Italian amaro. She was famous for her shelves, and almost as famous for her temper. Being told off by her became almost a ritual for her regulars. The first time Damon Boelte walked in, he started peeling the tissue paper off one of her bottles of rye, and she yelled at him on the spot. Later, she hired him. He says people are amazed when he tells them he worked at LeNell's.

3. I like to think the shop was a lot like you. It looked like the last place you'd count on, with a sharp word waiting at the counter, and it had exactly what you came for. And the people she told off were the ones who came back and said her name with pride. That's you, as far as I can see. You give people every reason to expect nothing, and then you're the one who's there. You never ask them to rely on you. They just find out they can.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a straight-rye Old-Fashioned. That's the great classic of whiskey, sugar and bitters, here with rye, the whiskey she stocked when hardly anyone did. I swapped the usual bitters for a teaspoon of Underberg, a German herbal bitters, as a nod to the rule she bent so her customers could have it. The sweetness is a spoon and a half of sorghum syrup, dark and rich, boiled down from the juice of a cane-like grass, for the Southern background she says shaped her shelves. It's stirred over a single block of ice in a short, heavy glass, with orange peel twisted above it for its oils. Deep, dark, bitter at the edges, and exactly what you'd drive to the end of the street for.

5. So here's my one request. Next time someone you'd go anywhere for has a big day coming, skip the grin and the "probably". Say yes, plainly, the week before. You won't lose an inch of your freedom: you were always going to go. What changes is theirs. For once they get to know, instead of hope. And you might find the warning was never what they believed about you.

**closing line:** Tomás's: *Stir the sorghum in first: once it's cold, it clings. Then tell one person you'll be there, ahead of time.* It agrees with y5's position (say it before), not the creed. y5 avoids "you'll be there" and "ahead of time", so the two don't repeat each other.
