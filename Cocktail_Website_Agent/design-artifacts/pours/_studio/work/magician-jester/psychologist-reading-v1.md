## whoYouAre

Someone at dinner is certain of something. You don't argue. You put a thing on the table, say nothing, and wait. Then comes the moment you love: what they were so sure of tips over, and they laugh. A minute later they're showing the next person as if it had been their own idea, and you let them.

You always have a reason, though. You picked that thing because you wanted them to see something differently, and it mattered to you that they did. You keep the reason to yourself, because you think the fun would turn into a lesson once it's explained. And you watch their faces all along, because that moment can tip the wrong way, into feeling foolish or played. You'd never want that for anyone. It's why nothing you show is fake. It's all right there, and the eye still won't believe it. So with you, being wrong becomes a pleasure. They walk away with one certainty looser, and glad of it. It's a kindness, and a rare one.

## yours

1. Through the later 1800s, bar guides in America filled up with a drink named the Pousse Café. Liqueurs were poured one on top of another, each trickled slowly off an upturned spoon, so it settled on the one below as a band of colour. Nothing keeps the bands apart but their weight. The sweetest liqueurs are the heaviest, so they go in first, and the spirits, with no sugar, go last, on top. Early in the twentieth century six or seven bands were normal, and there were reports of eleven, even fourteen.

2. It's an honest drink. Everything in it is right there to see, and the explanation takes one sentence. It still looks as if it shouldn't stay put. After World War II it slowly faded from bars. In 1972 someone wrote to Amy Vanderbilt's etiquette column about "a fascinating drink" bars used to serve: "You never see them any more. What happened?" Her answer passed on what a New York restaurant owner had told her: they were being shaken now, before serving.

3. In New Orleans, Nick Castrogiovannoni, a bartender at Nick's Big Train Bar, was known locally for making dozens of them a night, with as many as thirty-four bands. Whatever kept him at it, I like to think part of it was the faces: someone who knows perfectly well it's only weight, and still can't quite believe the glass. That's what you build for people. It's true, and it doesn't look it.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Pousse Café, the old after-dinner classic, in three bands. White crème de cacao goes in first: clear as water, tasting of chocolate, and the heaviest. Tawny port is next. Last, poured slowest, comes bourbon steeped for half an hour with raw beetroot: deep red, and the lightest of the three, because there's next to no sugar in it. So the darkest band ends up highest, and your eye will tell you the glass is upside down even once you know why. That's there as a nod to you. Beetroot and chocolate are a pairing you'd bet against, and the port carries one to the other: an earthy whiskey first, then rich port tasting of dried fruit, then a last small taste of chocolate. Rush that final pour and the bands run together for good. It's the pour you'd guard.

5. So once, after the moment lands and they've laughed, give them the reason. Say what you hoped they'd see, and why it mattered to you. You're afraid it'll turn into a lesson. It won't. Explaining the sugar doesn't move a single band, and explaining yourself won't undo what they saw. It gives them one thing more: they'll know you did it for them.
