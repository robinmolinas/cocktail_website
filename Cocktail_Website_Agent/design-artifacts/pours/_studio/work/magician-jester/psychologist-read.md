# Wren's read: The Illusionist (magician-jester), round 1

Sources: persona.py row (essence, story, goal/fear, Escher), the Magician plan row, the registry, and the drafted siblings' readings (Charmer, Entertainer, Surrealist, Alternative Comic, Wise Fool, Subverter, Trickster).

## Who this is
Someone who would rather show you than tell you. Ask them a question and they may hand you something to look at instead. They set it up, let you see it, and watch your face at the moment what you were sure of tips over. They love that moment, but the moment isn't the point. The point is afterwards: you see a little differently, and you think you got there by yourself.

## The one true thing no sibling shares
**Nothing they show is fake, and it still doesn't add up.** The Illusionist's puzzles are honest: everything is in plain view (Escher draws every step of the staircase), and the eye still can't make it agree. They change what people are sure of by showing, never by arguing, and the change survives the explanation. Knowing how it works doesn't undo it.

## What they hide
That there's a reason underneath the play, and that it matters to them. They keep the reason to themselves because they believe that, once explained, the thing turns into a lesson, and the fun (theirs and everyone's) is over. "Being figured out completely" is, at bottom, this: if people see the purpose, the play stops working.

## What they fear
Unintended results. The wobble that lands wrong: someone who leaves feeling foolish, or played, instead of delighted. They watch faces closely after the moment, because the line between "oh!" and "you got me" is very thin, and they never want to be on the wrong side of it. (The persona's "no surprises left" is the second fear: the explanation that ends it.)

## What they give
They make being wrong a pleasure. People leave with one certainty looser than they arrived, and glad of it. Said plainly, once per reading (Robin's rule): that's a kindness, and it's worth a lot.

## Proposed position (y5)
**Tell them why you did it. It won't stop working.** Once, after the moment lands, say what you hoped they'd see. Knowing the reason doesn't undo it, exactly as knowing it's sugar and weight doesn't stop the layers looking impossible. What they gain: people get to know it was for them.

## Traps
- "Mysterious", "enigma", optical-illusion puns, "magic" more than once, "nothing up my sleeve": family traps.
- "Never quite what they seem" in any form (the Alchemist's tagline sits right there).
- An existing epigraph says "Nothing in this glass is pretending": no "pretending".
- Fire: Oxford's flaming brandy top layer is the Entertainer's ground. Never.
- Jerry Thomas, the Pousse l'Amour (egg yolk), Baker's "rainbow-hued", Chartreuse as medicine, the Angel's Kiss/Tit (cream, and "angel"), the Knickebein: all out (plan guards).
- *Rosetta*'s syrup-under-absinthe layering and "bring the red up": no anise, own the layering in one clause.
- Escher: the persona's example. At most one clause, and only if Hester sources it; the drink should carry the illusion.
- The guest can't be asked to "trick" anyone: the position is about saying the reason, not playing a trick.

## Story test (for Hester, round 2)
1. **Honest:** nothing in it deceives; it is exactly what it looks like.
2. **It still fools the eye or a certainty**, openly.
3. **Knowing how doesn't undo it.** This carries the position, so it must be literally true of the story and of the drink.
4. **Built on purpose, for other people to see.** The maker's delight is on record or signposted.
5. **A cost or a risk on record:** it can go wrong (the unintended result), or the world stopped bothering.

## Verdict on the plan's lead (the Pousse Café): accept, with conditions
It passes all five. The layers are held apart only by their weight (1, 2), and knowing it's sugar doesn't stop the eye (3). The page gives a human and a cost I'd missed in the plan: Nick Castrogiovannoni of Nick's Big Train Bar in New Orleans, "local fame for making dozens nightly and achieving as many as thirty-four layers" (4), and the 1972 letter to Amy Vanderbilt: "a fascinating drink called a Pousse-cafe ... You never see them any more. What happened?", answered that they were now "shaken before being served" (5) (Oxford POUSSE CAFÉ, pdf 1558).

Conditions:
1. **The reading's point is "knowing how doesn't undo it"**, not the steady hand, not "keep the old way alive" (*Still Yours* and *Anyway* own persistence). Castrogiovannoni and the 1972 letter are welcome as story and cost, but not as a lesson about holding on.
2. **I withdraw my plan wording "the second look":** it's *The Other Berry*'s gift, and *Look Again* is a name. Say it with "still".
3. **The fear goes in the glass:** a rushed pour runs the layers together. That's the unintended result, and Tomás's method should make the care visible (in the guest's own make, never assuming the drink is in hand).
4. **Drink (Tomás, v1 in round 2):** honest layers, few of them (three is plenty), each good to taste on its own and together; the spark should be a pairing you'd bet against that works, layer to layer, so the drink acts out the moment a certainty tips. No anise, no cream, no egg, no flame, veto-free. The closing line can't be "don't stir/shake it" (Trickster's).
5. **Facts (Hester):** the eleven- and fourteen-layer drinks are "one hears of"; Castrogiovannoni's thirty-four is "achieving as many as": keep both as Oxford's. "Fascinating" is the correspondent's word, attributed. Whether he made them for fun or delight is not on the page: signpost.

Backup: Poire Williams *prisonnière* still passes on the same test (an honest impossible object; knowing it grew there makes it better). Keep it ready if the drink can't be made good in layers.
