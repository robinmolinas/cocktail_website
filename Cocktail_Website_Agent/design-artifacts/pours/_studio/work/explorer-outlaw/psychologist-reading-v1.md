name: Fine by Me
tagline: Most people hear "only if" as a no. You hear a way in.
epigraph: It started with someone else's leftover bacon fat, and a question asked as a joke.

## whoYouAre

Somebody once told you, "You can, if you do all of it yourself." They thought that would be the end of it. You said fine. Then you spent your own evenings on it, without a word of complaint, because a hard yes is still a yes, and a yes is a way in. You ask for odd things too, the ones nobody else wants, half as a joke, and you're often the only one who comes away with them.

You knew, though. You knew the "only if" was meant to put you off, and you took it anyway, in plain sight, with a grin. You'd rather carry the whole load than wait on anybody's say-so. What frightens you comes later: when the thing you made becomes the thing you're known for, and everyone expects you to keep making it. The open road starts to look like a fenced one, with your name on the gate. But here's what your stubbornness gives people. Because you carried it the first time, nobody after you has to. They get the yes for free. That's a generous thing to leave behind, and you rarely get thanked for it.

## yours

1. Don Lee came to bartending from a job in IT. Eben Freeman, the barman at a New York restaurant called WD-50, told him about fat-washing: melt a fat into a spirit, freeze it, lift the fat away, and the flavour stays behind. You may have heard Lee invented it. He didn't.
2. At PDT, the bar where Lee worked, a regular had a lot of bacon fat and no idea what to do with it. Lee asked about it, as a joke at first, then asked for some. When he took the idea to Jim Meehan, who ran the bar, Meehan was wary, and fairly: infusions take staff time. Lee could put it on the menu if he did all the infusions himself. In Meehan's words, "in true Don Lee fashion, he did." It became the bar's best-selling drink, and soon bartenders everywhere were washing spirits with every meat and nut you can think of. Freeman tried Lee's version and said it was better than his own.
3. Whatever Meehan expected, I like to think Lee heard a yes, and got to work. So would you. The rest of it comes later. Lee is proud of the drink, and a bit haunted by it: he says it's the one that will probably follow him around for the rest of his life. Years on, people even praised it as a drink about not wasting food, and he said that was never what he'd had in mind. I'd guess you know that feeling, of watching something you started go places you never meant it to.
4. The cocktail I've made for you starts where his did: bourbon washed with bacon fat, the way Lee printed it, with Benton's bacon from Tennessee and Four Roses bourbon if you can get them, or a smoky dry-cured bacon and any straight bourbon. Nobody sells it ready, so the washing is yours to do: warm the fat, stir it into the bottle, wait four hours, freeze it for two, lift the fat off and strain. A little maple and Angostura bitters, as in his Old-Fashioned, a renowned classic. Then I take it somewhere his recipe doesn't go: a tall glass packed with ice, topped with a crisp, dry sparkling cider, so the apple meets the smoke and the maple. I did that as a nod to everyone who took his idea their own way.
5. So let it follow you. The thing you're known for isn't a fence. It's proof you went, and that door stays open whether you're standing in it or not. You're free to walk on to the next odd thing, and to be glad, out loud, that this one's yours.
