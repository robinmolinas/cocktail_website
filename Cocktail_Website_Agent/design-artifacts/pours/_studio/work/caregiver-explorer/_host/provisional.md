# Pour — Serviceable · The Researcher (caregiver-explorer)

## Cocktail

- **name:** Serviceable
- **tagline:** You care about people you'll never meet.
- **glassware:** highball glass, filled with ice
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 50 ml | blended scotch whisky, steeped with dried hops | Any unpeated blended scotch. Steep 4 g of dried aroma hops (from a home-brew shop) in 250 ml of the whisky for about two hours at room temperature, then strain it through a coffee filter. This makes five drinks. |
| 125 ml | soda water, very cold, from a freshly opened bottle | Poured last. It's the part that began with him. |
| 1 | long strip of lemon peel | Twisted once as it goes in, and left in the glass. |

**method**
1. Fill a highball glass with ice, then pour in the hopped whisky.
2. Pour the soda water in slowly, down the inside of the glass, so it keeps its bubbles.
3. Stir once, gently, only to bring them together.
4. Twist the strip of lemon peel once, as if wringing it, and slide it down into the glass. It stays there and scents the drink as you sip.

**closingLine:** *Pour the soda slowly, down the side. And when something doesn't work, say so: someone out there needs to know.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*The fizz in this glass was once meant to cure sailors. It never did.*

**whoYouAre**
When someone you care about is told there's nothing more to be done, you don't quite accept it. That same evening you're reading, comparing, writing down questions for the next appointment, finding the one person who might know. Not because you think you're cleverer than the experts. Because "that's just how it is" has never once been enough for you when someone is hurting.

Most of what you try goes nowhere. Those tries never make it into the story you tell, but you keep them, every one. And here's something I'd guess you've never said out loud: you love it a little. The not-knowing, the chase. Some days that feels almost wrong, as if it made it about you. It doesn't. It's why you're still looking after everyone else has stopped, and why your care so often reaches people you'll never meet.

**yours**

1. Joseph Priestley was an English clergyman who ran experiments on the side. He found that a bowl of water hung over a vat of fermenting beer came back sparkling. What he had in mind was sailors. In his day scurvy, a disease that struck them at sea, was thought to be a kind of rot, and that gas seemed able to stop rot. So he hoped his fizzy water might cure it. He wrote that it could be "particularly serviceable" to them.

2. He didn't take it to a shop. He took it to the Navy. The doctors they sent him to approved it, and a trial was ordered on the King's ships. He published a way anyone could copy, with chalk and acid, and never went into the business himself. And it was no cure. His water was only water and gas.

3. Others took it up after him, and half a century later soda water found its way into mixed drinks: the highball, the Collins, the fizz. Every one of them owes something to a man who was trying to help sailors. It failed at what he'd hoped for them, and turned out useful in a way he never planned. I like to think that's how it often goes for you. You look hard for one answer, for someone else's sake, and what you find turns up years later, somewhere you'll never see, helping someone who will never know your name.

4. So yours is the plainest drink those bubbles made possible: whisky and soda, tall, over ice. Whisky starts life as a kind of beer, so I steep it with dried hops, the flower that gives beer its bitterness. That's my own nod to the vat he started with; the record doesn't say what was in his. Then very cold soda, poured slowly and stirred once, and a long strip of lemon peel, twisted once as it goes in, and left in the glass. More than two thirds of the glass is the kind of fizzy water he hoped would be a cure. It's also what makes it a highball.

5. Don't hide the tries that went nowhere. Someone else is about to set off down the same road, and what you found there could save them the trip. One wrong answer ended up in drinks all over the world. And stop treating the love of the looking as the selfish part. It's what carries the help out of the room.

**closingLine (Tomás, agreed):** *Pour the soda slowly, down the side. And when something doesn't work, say so: someone out there needs to know.*

**Conflicts** (fact card)

- **C1, the year.** Oxford: 1771 for the method. The pamphlet is 1772. Pawley's account describes the published method as chalk + oil of vitriol (sulfuric acid), not the beer vat. Safe: the beer vat is how he first did it (Oxford); what he *published* was a way anyone could do it with chalk and acid (Pawley). Don't say "he published the beer-vat method". No year in the reading, or "1772" for the pamphlet only.
- **C2, "never sold it".** The pamphlet itself was priced at one shilling (title/dedication page, per the transcription). He sold the booklet; he never made a business of the water. Say "never went into the business" / "left the selling to others".
