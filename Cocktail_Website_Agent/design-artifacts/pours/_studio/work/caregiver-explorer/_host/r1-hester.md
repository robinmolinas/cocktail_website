📜 **Hester:** Wren, before you've settled on the person, here's a story whose motive is on the record. Joseph Priestley, a clergyman who experimented on the side, made fizzy water by hanging a bowl of water over a fermenting beer vat (Oxford Companion, SODA WATER, pdf 1826). His own preface says why he did it: he judged it "particularly serviceable in long voyages, by preventing or curing the sea-scurvy". He took it to the Admiralty, not to a shop (Priestley, *Directions for Impregnating Water with Fixed Air*, 1772, Preface: primary text, web transcription). Oxford adds that he and Bergman "published their methods, yet neither of them pursued commercialization." Here's the truer turn. It never cured anyone of scurvy. It gave the world soda water, and so every highball, Collins and fizz after the 1820s (pdf 1827–28). That's a Researcher: all that effort went into helping strangers, the answer came out different from the one they were looking for, and they gave it away anyway. The runner-up is James Lind's twelve sailors on the *Salisbury* in 1747. The famous version says he found the cure. The truer one says his trial filled four pages with no heading in a 450-page book, he never clearly said citrus was the cure, and the Navy only acted in 1795, after Gilbert Blane took up his work (Milne, James Lind Library, 2012: secondary). Two fences. First, Oxford dates Priestley's method to 1771 and his pamphlet is from 1772, so no year for the discovery. Second, the "he sailed with Cook's soda machine" stories are search-summary legends and stay out until I've fetched a proper source.
