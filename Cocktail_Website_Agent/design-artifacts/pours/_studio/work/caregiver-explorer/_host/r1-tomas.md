Its bitterness is quinine, from the bark of the cinchona tree. People in Bolivia and Peru used that bark as medicine long before anyone wrote it down, and Europeans have used it since the 1500s (*Liquid Intelligence* p. 364). So the drink's backbone is a cure someone had to go out and find, which is care and exploration in one ingredient. It's physics too. Quinine glows blue under ultraviolet light (same page), and a lab's cool blue is what this person's palette already is. That gives the image brief something real to show, not garnish-as-poetry. Arnold closes his book on the G&T (pp. 361–368), with a home tonic syrup made from bark bought online, so it's makeable. One caution for later: I'd have guests use bought tonic rather than boil their own bark. Home extraction makes the quinine dose hard to control, and that's a guest-safety call, not taste.
