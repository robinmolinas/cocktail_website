## whoYouAre

When someone you care about is told there's nothing more to be done, you don't quite accept it. That same evening you're reading, comparing, writing down questions for the next appointment, finding the one person who might know. Not because you think you're cleverer than the experts. Because "that's just how it is" has never once been enough for you when someone is hurting.

Most of what you try goes nowhere, and you rarely mention those parts. You keep them anyway, every one. And here's something I'd guess you've never said out loud: you love it a little. The not-knowing, the chase. Some days that feels almost wrong, as if it made it about you. It doesn't. It's why you're still looking after everyone else has stopped, and why your care so often reaches people you'll never meet.

## yours

1. Joseph Priestley was an English clergyman who ran experiments on the side. He found that if he hung a bowl of water over a vat of fermenting beer, the water came back sparkling. He wasn't trying to invent a drink. In his day scurvy, the disease that killed sailors at sea, was thought to be a kind of rot, and the gas from fermenting seemed to stop things rotting. So he hoped his fizzy water might cure it. He wrote that it could be "particularly serviceable" on long voyages.

2. He didn't take it to a shop. He took it to the Navy, and the doctors they sent him to approved a trial on the King's ships. His apparatus even went aboard Captain Cook's ships. He published a way anyone could copy, with chalk and acid, and never went into the business himself. And it didn't cure scurvy. What stops scurvy is vitamin C, the thing in lemons. His water was only water and gas.

3. Other people took it from there. Someone else made a business of it, and about half a century later soda water found its way into mixed drinks: the highball, the Collins, the fizz. Every one of them goes back to a man trying to help sailors he would never meet. It failed at the one thing he wanted, and turned out useful in a way he never planned. I like to think that's how it often goes for you. You look hard for one answer, for someone else's sake, and what you find turns up years later, somewhere you'll never see, helping someone who will never know your name.

4. So yours is tall and simple, and his bubbles finish it. Gin, lemon, and a syrup steeped with dried hops, the flower that gives beer its bitterness, for a breath of the brewery he took his fizz from, without any beer. It's shaken briefly, then the soda goes in last. The failed cure is the final thing poured, and it's what lifts the whole drink.

5. The next time something you've tried for someone goes nowhere, don't file it away as a failure nobody needs to hear about. Tell someone what you found on the way; one wrong answer ended up in drinks all over the world. And stop treating the love of the looking as the selfish part. It's the part that gets the help anywhere.
