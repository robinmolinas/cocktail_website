## whoYouAre

The lights go out in the middle of a party, and you already know where the fuse box is. The car won't start on the way to the airport, and you've got the number saved. Afterwards, you shrug: anyone would have done the same. But you know that isn't quite true. You found the fuse box the week you moved in. You drove the route the day before. You don't trust yourself to be at your best on the day, so you don't leave anything to the day.

What you'd never say is why. It isn't pride. You're afraid of being the one who wasn't enough when someone was counting on you. So you practise, quietly, for things that may never happen, and when it goes well you hand the credit to everyone else. Being singled out has always felt a bit like being left out. But everyone near you gets through a bad day more easily because of a hundred ordinary ones you never mention. That's how you care for people, and it deserves more than a shrug.

## yours

1. Marco Dionysos started out clearing tables at a Mexican restaurant in Portland, Oregon. (He chose that surname himself at twenty-one, after the Greek god of wine: the same god this place is named after.) He worked his way up to the bar, where he mostly made frozen drinks, and liked it. Then one day a guest ordered a Singapore Sling. Marco asked the two senior bartenders, and neither of them knew the recipe. As his story is told, that didn't sit right with him. He went to a bookshop, bought his first cocktail book, and kept going back.

2. His books ran into the hundreds, though he said he learned more from watching other people work. He moved to San Francisco and tended bar there for years. He once described a Friday night with sixteen drinks going at once, a lot of it muscle memory, and the part he loved: "I got this." One writer says he could have become the city's star bartender, but "didn't seize the mantle or seem to want it". Asked what he'd like to be known for, Marco said "making consistently excellent cocktails". Asked who had taught him, he said: "Probably dozens."

3. I like to think he never quite forgot that guest at the bar, waiting for a drink nobody there could make. Nobody had done anything wrong. Someone had simply asked, and nobody was ready. I think that's the moment you practise against: not failing, but being the one who wasn't enough when it mattered. So you put in the hours nobody sees, and when the day comes and goes fine, you shrug. Like Marco, you'd rather be good every time than great once.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Mojito, a renowned classic. It's the drink he remembered making at his first job in San Francisco. "Pretty simple drink," he said, "but nobody made them." Plenty of bartenders still call it too much trouble: lime and sugar stirred together in the glass, mint pressed gently so it gives its scent without turning bitter, ice, white rum, cold soda. I added one thing, a spoonful of Bénédictine, a honeyed herbal liqueur that goes into the Singapore Sling. It's there for the night nobody could make that drink, the day all the practice began. [Tomás: taste words and the "pressed gently" clause wait for your spec.]

5. You've spent years getting ready for other people's hard days. What you forget is that the others have been getting ready too, and some of it was for yours. Being enough was never meant to be your job alone. So before the next day like that, practise as much as you like. Then look at who's around you, and trust their hours the way they've always trusted yours. You were never the only one who was ready.

**closing line:** Tomás's. It should agree with y5 (trust the crew's hours), not with the creed (practise more).
