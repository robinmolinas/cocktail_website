📜 **Hester:** Wren, I've read Dionysos on the page: *Proper* pp. 54, 60, 71–77, 173 and 186, plus his own 2010 interview (*FoodGPS*, fetched in full). My verdict is that he passes your clauses 1 to 4 better than Phil did. Clause 5 fails for his early years, and the name isn't a coincidence.

**What we didn't have yet (p. 72).** In Portland, early on, a customer ordered a Singapore Sling. He asked two senior bartenders and nobody knew how to make it. The book says that "didn't sit right", so he bought his first cocktail book at Powell's and "kept going back". That's your person's fear shown on a real day: a guest the crew couldn't serve. It's also the start of the practice. "Didn't sit right" is Simonson's narration, not a quote from Dionysos, so any feeling beyond it is "I like to think".

**Clause by clause.**
- (1) Practice: "a lot of it is still muscle memory", in his words (p. 75). His hundreds of books are on p. 60 (three hundred) and in the 2010 interview (350), so say "hundreds".
- (2) Moment: "That moment where: I got this" (p. 75). It comes every Friday night; it isn't one day.
- (3) Glory: in 2010 he said he'd like to be known for "making consistently excellent cocktails". Asked about mentors, he said "Probably dozens… I take something from each person." That's the credit given away, in his own words. "Didn't seize the mantle or seem to want it" is Simonson's judgement, and "not really looking for glory" is the journalist Camper English's. Attribute both and keep the "seem".
- (4) Staying: he stayed at one bar for years "long after the rest of the original bar staff had moved on". So he stayed in the place, but the crew left: never "stayed with his crew". He also moved through a string of bars, and p. 73 calls him "restless".
- (5) Spotlight fails when he was young. In Portland he was "charismatic and quick", a "cocky kid" spinning cocktail trays (p. 73). No line may say he never wanted attention. The honest arc is that he grew into the man who "seemed most contented simply being a bartender".

**The name.** He was born Mark Johnson and chose "Marcovaldo Antonin Dionysus" at twenty-one, the god from Greek mythology among its sources. Simonson reads the name as a sign he was "more extroverted" (p. 71). So owning it means saying he chose it; a clause that treats it as luck would be untrue. The book's text uses the app's spelling, but its own recipe header (p. 77), Oxford (pdf 440 and 2075), *Joy*, *PUNCH* and he himself all spell it Dionysos. Guest text: **Dionysos**. What we share with him is the god, not the spelling. Whether the name is told at all is your call.

**"Out-ring" (my reading, labelled).** "Ring up" means recording a customer's payment on a cash register (Cambridge Dictionary), so Waugh means he rang up more sales than anyone else on the shift. Waugh doesn't explain it. It's a second contest he "won, every single time", along with the coat, and winning puts him in front of the crew. There's also a family collision: the page says "coat", never white, and the Superhero row keeps Constante's *white jacket* as its image. I'd keep both contests out of the reading, or at most mention one as a detail. The Sling and "I got this" carry the story.
