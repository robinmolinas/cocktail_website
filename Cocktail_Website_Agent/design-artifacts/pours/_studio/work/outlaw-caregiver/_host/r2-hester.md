📜 **Hester:** Wren, your second page is in, but it brings two corrections with it. Card: `_studio/fact-cards/sol-stoumen-black-cat.md`.

**The soft-drinks night stands.** Gary Kamiya, *San Francisco Chronicle*, 14 Nov 2014 (secondary, named author, fetched): one witness recalls hundreds still there at 6 a.m., "drinking only soft drinks and juices". NPS gives the other page. Only NPS says Stoumen "decided" to stay open, so his own agency rests on one page. The sources also disagree on when the licence went: "on Halloween" (NPS), "the morning before" (Thinkwalks), "the night before" (Wikipedia). All three agree it was before the party, so we say "just before its Halloween party".

**Correction 1: it may not have been the last night.** Kamiya says the bar closed that night for good. Wikipedia, citing Gorman's Sarria biography (not fetched), says it tried to get by on food and soft drinks until February 1964. So the reading never says "the last night" as a fact. "The night the licence went" is safe. Your point 4 survives either way, because the power ran out at the licence, not at the door.

**Correction 2 hits your point 2, and it's the one that matters.** The 1951 opinion (37 Cal.2d 713, SCOCAL, primary, read in full) says a proprietor "has no right to exclude or eject a patron 'except for good cause'", and is liable in damages if he does. So "he could have shown them out" is our inference, and the court says it wasn't his lawful choice to make. Kamiya adds, relaying Agee, that the case began when Stoumen refused to sign a closed-shop agreement with the culinary workers' union, and the Board's chairman then had a case built against the bar. No page gives Stoumen's motive in his own words. What is on the page: the state tried to take his licence over who drank there, and he fought it for fourteen years (Kamiya's figure; *Reason* says fifteen). His lawyer, Lowenthal, argued their right to gather, and won in 1951. Then the licence went in 1963, and he poured soft drinks for them anyway. That still passes your points 1, 3 and 4. For point 2 I'd put it as "he kept serving them while the state came for his licence over it". I wouldn't put it as a choice he weighed.

Still out: the $38,000 in legal costs and "no other bar owner helped" (Wikipedia only), and the Holocaust detail. We also never state his sexuality, even though Kamiya now calls him a straight married man. The union dispute doesn't have to be in the reading, but no sentence may say the state came for him only because of his customers.
