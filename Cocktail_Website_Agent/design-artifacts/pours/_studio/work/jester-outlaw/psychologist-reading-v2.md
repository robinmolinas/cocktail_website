**tagline:** Rooms get more honest around you. Rarely politer.

**epigraph:** *The cocktail's earliest known definition in print ends with a dig at the other side.*

## whoYouAre

Dinner, eight of you, and everyone has spent the starter praising something nobody there actually likes. You put your fork down and say so, in the bluntest words available. There's a gasp. Somebody laughs and turns it into a cough. And you wait to see who'll say it back.

Hardly anyone hears it right. It comes out like a verdict, but you mean it as a question. You enjoy the gasp, and you won't pretend you don't. And often you go too far on purpose: once you've said the outrageous version, the honest one everybody's been sitting on suddenly sounds mild, and someone dares. What wears you down is good manners doing their quiet work: the gasp smoothed over, someone offering round the potatoes, and the same people agreeing with you afterwards, by the coats, where it doesn't count.

It's time someone told you what that's worth: you take the blame for the first line so the honest conversation can happen in the room. You turn "how dare you" into "hang on, is that true?" Not everyone thanks you for it. They should.

## yours

1. In May 1806, in Hudson, a river town in New York, a newspaper editor called Harry Croswell printed a mocking ledger of a losing candidate's election drinks. Under "Gain", it said "Nothing". Among the losses were twenty-five glasses of "cock-tail". A reader wrote in: he'd never heard of it. What was it?

2. Croswell's answer is the earliest known definition of the cocktail in print: spirits of any kind, sugar, water, bitters. Then came the dig. It was said, he added, that the drink suited a candidate of that party, because anyone who'd "swallowed a glass of it" was "ready to swallow anything else". A rival paper printed a letter attacking him for "publishing grog stories". The line is still one of the most quoted in the history of American drinking.

3. By then Croswell had already been convicted of libel, for printing that the President had paid a writer to attack Washington and Adams. Whether that was true isn't mine to say, and at his trial the court wouldn't let him prove it, or explain why he'd printed it. Alexander Hamilton took his appeal and argued that a free press meant the right to publish the truth "with good motives, and for justifiable ends". The judges split, and the conviction stood. I like to think you'd have recognised Croswell at once: he put in print what was being said behind hands, and I'd bet he enjoyed the cock-tail line enormously. Hamilton's rule has a catch you know too: true words said with bad motives weren't covered. Some nights it's only for the gasp, and you can tell which ones. The others, the ones with a reason behind them, you'd stand behind anywhere.

4. The cocktail I've made for you starts from his four things, with a spirit of my choosing: aged rum from Jamaica, a little bitters, and a measure of water, ice-cold, poured in the way the old recipe has it, all served without ice in a glass frozen beforehand. Last comes a bright pinch of orange oil, with its peel dropped in. My one change is the sugar: it arrives as a banana liqueur, something nobody could have poured in 1806. Many aged Jamaican rums already have a hint of ripe banana in them. I put it in plain sight as a nod to Croswell's line: the banana that's often already in the rum, said out loud. Expect something rich, round and smooth, and far better than it sounds when you say "banana" at a dinner party.

5. Keep saying it. A room needs someone who will. But when the gasp next gets smoothed over and the potatoes start going round, don't reach for a bigger line. Ask it as the question it always was, out loud, and then wait. Someone usually answers. Then you get the conversation you came for, there and then, while it still counts.

## Resonance

**As the guest:** "You put your fork down and say so" is me, and the potatoes are cruelly exact. The line that lands: *"It comes out like a verdict, but you mean it as a question."* Nobody says that about the person everyone calls rude. The gift is said out loud ("you take the blame for the first line") and I don't feel excused or flattered: "some nights it's only for the gasp, and you can tell which ones" is the honesty I'd demand, and it comes from Hamilton's own rule, not from a scolding. The ending gives me something: the answer I was fishing for, in the room.
**As the sceptic:** "You enjoy shocking people" fits anyone who's ever been cheeky. What doesn't: the dare hidden in the verdict, going too far so the honest view sounds mild, and the hatred of the agreement saved for the coats. Those are this person's alone.
