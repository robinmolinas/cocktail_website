# Pour — Quote Me · The Scandalmonger (jester-outlaw)

## Cocktail

- **name:** Quote Me
- **tagline:** Rooms get more honest around you. Rarely politer.
- **glassware:** small tumbler (about 200 ml), chilled in the freezer, no ice
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 ml | aged Jamaican rum (40-43%; not spiced, not sweetened) | the spirit; many aged Jamaican rums carry a stewed-banana taste |
| 20 ml | Giffard Banane du Brésil banana liqueur (about 25%), recommended for its real bananas, or any banana liqueur of about 25% (a sweeter *crème de banane*: 15 ml) | the sugar, given as banana, out in the open |
| 2 dashes | Angostura bitters | the bitters |
| 30 ml | ice-cold still water, poured from a jug of water and ice | the water, measured: none of the ice goes in |
| 1 | strip of orange peel | squeezed over the top, then dropped in |

**method**
1. At least two hours ahead, put the rum, banana liqueur and bitters in a small jar or plastic bottle (not filled to the brim), close it, and put it in the freezer. Put the tumbler in the freezer too.
2. Just before serving, fill a jug with water and ice and let it stand for a few minutes.
3. Pour the cold mix into the frozen tumbler. Measure in 30 ml of the ice-cold water from the jug, and none of the ice.
4. Stir a few turns with a spoon.
5. Hold the orange peel skin side down over the glass and squeeze it, so its oils fall on the drink. Drop it in.

**closingLine:** *Name the banana as you pour it. Then ask who else thinks so, while everyone's still at the table.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*The cocktail's earliest known definition in print comes with a dig at the other side.*

**whoYouAre**
Dinner, eight of you, and everyone has spent the starter praising something nobody there actually likes. You put your fork down and say so, in the bluntest words available. There's a gasp. Somebody laughs and turns it into a cough. Then you watch for whoever will say it back.

Hardly anyone hears it right. It comes out like a verdict, but you mean it as a question. You enjoy the gasp, and you won't pretend you don't. And often you go too far on purpose: once you've said the outrageous version, the honest one everybody's been sitting on suddenly sounds mild, and someone dares. What wears you down is good manners doing their quiet work: the gasp smoothed over, someone offering round the potatoes, and the same people agreeing with you afterwards, by the coats, where it doesn't count.

It's time someone told you what that's worth: you take the blame for the first line so the honest conversation can happen in the room. You turn "how dare you" into "hang on, is that true?" Not everyone thanks you for it. They should.

**yours**

1. In May 1806, in Hudson, a river town in New York, a newspaper editor called Harry Croswell printed a mocking ledger of a losing candidate's election drinks. Under "Gain", it said "Nothing". Among the losses were twenty-five glasses of "cock-tail". A reader wrote in, never having heard of it. Was it local? Was it new?

2. Croswell's answer is the earliest known definition of the cocktail in print: spirits of any kind, sugar, water, bitters. Then came the dig. It was said, he added, that the drink suited a candidate of that party, because anyone who'd "swallowed a glass of it" was "ready to swallow anything else". A rival paper printed a letter attacking him, with a swipe at his "publishing grog stories". His answer is still among the most quoted lines on American drinking.

3. By then Croswell had already been convicted of libel, for printing that the President had paid a writer to attack Washington and Adams. Whether that was true isn't mine to say, and at his trial the court wouldn't let him prove it, or explain why he'd printed it. Alexander Hamilton took his appeal and argued that a free press meant the right to publish the truth "with good motives, and for justifiable ends". The judges split, and the conviction stood. I like to think you'd have recognised Croswell at once: he put in print what was being said behind hands, and I'd bet he enjoyed the cock-tail line enormously. Hamilton's rule has a catch you know too: true words said with bad motives wouldn't have been covered. Some nights it's only for the gasp, and you can tell which ones. The others, the ones with a reason behind them, you'd stand behind anywhere.

4. The cocktail I've made for you starts from Croswell's four things, with a spirit of my choosing: aged rum from Jamaica, a little bitters, and a measure of water, as his definition has it, poured in ice-cold, all served without ice in a glass frozen beforehand. Last comes a bright pinch of orange oil, with its peel dropped in. My one change is the sugar: it arrives as a banana liqueur, a liqueur whose recipes only turn up about a century later. Many aged Jamaican rums already have a taste of stewed banana in them. I put it in plain sight as a nod to Croswell's line: the banana that's often already in the rum, said out loud. Expect something rich, round and smooth, and far better than it sounds when you say "banana" at a dinner party.

5. Keep saying it. A room needs someone who will. But when the gasp next gets smoothed over and the potatoes start going round, don't reach for a bigger line. Ask it as the question it always was, out loud, and then wait. Someone usually answers. Then you get the conversation you came for, there and then, while it still counts.
