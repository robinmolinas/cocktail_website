| price | Croswell, a newspaper editor in Hudson, New York, was convicted of libel for publishing an article alleging that President Jefferson had paid a writer (James Callender) to attack Washington and Adams (MTSU, M1). Whether it was true: not ours to say (C4). No year in guest text (C2). | He printed it about the most powerful man in the country, and paid in court. The reading says "he printed that", never whether it was so. | story test: aimed up, price paid |
| price | At his trial he was not allowed to prove the truth of what he'd said, or his intent in publishing it (MTSU, M2). | He wasn't allowed to show it was true, or why he'd said it. Note: intent was shut out too, so never "they only asked whether he should have said it". | fear: the gasp absorbed, the question never asked |
| turn | On appeal, Alexander Hamilton argued that a free press meant the right to publish the truth "with good motives, and for justifiable ends" (MTSU, M3). A judge, James Kent, agreed that truth should be a defence; the court split and the conviction stood (M4, M6). | Truth told for a reason should count. | gives: turn "how dare you" into "is it true?" |
| counterweight | By Hamilton's own rule, true words said with bad motives could still be punished (MTSU, Vile's gloss, M5). | The rule doesn't cover the nights it's only for the gasp, and this guest knows which nights those are. | hides: some nights it's only the gasp; position: keep the ones you'd stand behind |
| story | In May 1806, after the conviction, his paper printed a mocking ledger of a losing candidate's election drinks, "Gain: Nothing", among them 25 glasses of cock-tail (*Imbibe!* pdf 169, F3). | A joke at the other side's voters, made in print, and partisan: the reading takes no side and can say gently it was for the gasp too. | hides: they enjoy it |
| story | A reader wrote in to ask what a cock-tail was. Croswell's answer: spirits of any kind, sugar, water and bitters. Then he added that people said it suited a candidate of that party, because once you'd swallowed a glass you were ready to swallow anything else (*Imbibe!* pdf 169, F5–F6; Oxford pdf 493, F9). It's the first time we know of that the name and the recipe were printed together (Oxford). | The drink is defined by a jibe: the line he's remembered for is the cheek. Manners as swallowing things is Wren's reading, signposted. | one true thing; story test: the line outlived the price |
| reaction | Eleven days later a rival paper in Pittsfield, Massachusetts, printed a letter attacking him, with a swipe at him for "publishing grog stories" (*Imbibe!* pdf 169, F8). | The gasp is on record, in print, from the other side. | story test: reaction on record |
| legacy | Wondrich calls the answer one of the most famous and often repeated quotations in American drinking; with small changes, the definition described the cocktail for four generations (*Imbibe!* pdf 169, F7). | The jibe outlived the trial: people still quote it. | driver: LEAVE A MARK |
| lineage | Oxford's Cock-Tail: a raw sugar cube muddled with a little water and bitters, a spirit, cold water to taste, stirred, in a small Old-Fashioned glass (pdf 494, F10). The 1806 formula says "spirits of any kind"; our aged rum is ours, not his (C5). | Nothing hidden: four things, named up front, as he printed them. | the drink: the definition itself |
