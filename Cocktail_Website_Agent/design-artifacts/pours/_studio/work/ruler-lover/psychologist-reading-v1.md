## whoYouAre

Someone comes back after a year away, and you ask about the interview they were dreading. You remember their sister's name. You stay with them a second longer than they expect, and for that second nobody else is there. They go home thinking you're simply like that.

You're not simply anything. You know exactly what your attention does, and you give it on purpose, because a lot holds together when you do: the table, the team, the whole evening. People who watch from a distance call it charm, and sometimes mean it as a warning. They've got it backwards. It works because you mean it. You hardly mention what it costs you, the nights you'd give a lot not to be the one everyone turns to, or the question underneath: if I stopped, would they still come? The fear is one particular night, the one where it doesn't land, when someone looks past you and everything quietly drifts. And this is what your attention gives: people leave feeling they were the one who mattered, and around you they feel sure enough to offer something of their own. On an ordinary evening, nobody expects to be given that.

## yours

In about 1885, a sea captain's son from near Naples was put in charge of a small bar facing the casino in Monte Carlo. Ciro Capozzi had actually tended bar in America, and where other bars poured inventions called things like Flip Flaps, he served far simpler drinks, the Manhattan among them.

His regulars included the Prince of Wales, who would show him cocktails he had learned. The prince already liked American drinks when most of his circle thought them a curiosity, so Ciro didn't win him over. The prince kept coming back, with what he knew. I like to think Ciro treasured that: the most watched man in Monte Carlo, at ease enough to teach the barman. Some say Ciro's charm, skill and welcome made the cocktail fashionable in Europe. That might be an exaggeration, but not much of one.

People get you wrong in the same way. They look for the trick, and there isn't one. The plainness is the point: someone paying real attention, and meaning it. And the best of it was never that people are charmed. It's what they bring you once they feel seen, and how much more of themselves they show around you than anywhere else.

The cocktail I've made for you is a Manhattan, the renowned classic Ciro was pouring in Monte Carlo: rye, sweet vermouth and bitters, stirred cold and clear, then brightened with lemon peel. There's one change: a small square of fresh pineapple, pressed in with the rest and strained out again. It's from a cocktail that a book of 1901 credits to the prince, and I put it there as a nod to what a regular brings to the bar. It still looks like a plain Manhattan. You only find the pineapple up close.

Some night soon, don't be "on". Let someone else carry the conversation, and let them show you something they've learned. The ones who still come weren't there for the effort. They were there for you, and I hope you get to feel it.

## Resonance

- **"This is me" line:** *if I stopped, would they still come?* It names the doubt this guest never says, in their own words. Robin's line to hand him: "It works because you mean it."
- **As the guest:** the scene (a year away, the interview, the sister's name) is recognisable without being a role. The warning-charm line answers the thing they've heard said about them. The gift is said out loud. The ending answers the hidden question with an affirmation, and asks for one small act.
- **As a sceptic:** "how much it takes" is true of anyone who works hard, but "the one everyone turns to" plus "if I stopped" pins it to someone whose attention holds things together. No Barnum line survives without the attention frame. Siblings: no crowd or plan (Charismatic), no getting ready or "expected" (Geisha), not "the same you" (Nurse), no handling the pull for others' sake (Siren).
