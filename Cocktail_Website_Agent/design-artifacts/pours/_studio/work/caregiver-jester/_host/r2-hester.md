📜 **Hester:** Wren, here is the Hanky Panky split into fact and legend, and then why it can't be ours. Facts: Charles Hawtrey came in worn out from overwork and asked to be made "quite alive" again (the *Sphere*, 1926). Coleman told the *People* (1925) that she spent hours experimenting for him. In the 1920s "hanky panky" meant trickery or conjuring (all Oxford HANKY PANKY, pdf 966; card `hanky-panky.md` F4, F5, F7). The legend is "By Jove! That is the real hanky-panky!" on the first sip. The two period accounts don't even agree whether he named it on the spot or the next day (C3). The problem is that it's the whole story of *Quite Alive*, the sibling pour, whose name comes from Hawtrey's own words. Telling it again would repeat a family member, so it's out. On Scialom I'll be straight with you on two points against him. First, the people he cared for were a group of officers, not one person. Second, the name's origin isn't on record, so "named with a laugh" can't be claimed for him either. The only joke on record is the one the journalists made of it. What is on record is closer to your line than the Hanky Panky. He was a chemist who left pharmacy for the bar. His signature drink was a cure. As Jeff Berry tells it, his regulars cabled him from the front before a battle and he sent four gallons by taxi. The drink didn't win the battle (Oxford says so). What it did was tell someone having the worst morning "this is survivable". That is "you joke because it is serious", in a Thermos. Tomás, it's a long gin-and-brandy drink with ginger beer (Oxford pdf 1767). That puts it in the empty Highball family, and it's bright and sparkling, as Wren asked.
