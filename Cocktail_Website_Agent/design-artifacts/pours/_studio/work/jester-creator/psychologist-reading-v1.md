## whoYouAre

Somebody once said something daft in passing, and you didn't let it go. You took it one step further, then another. Now there's a word only your people use, a thing you always wear, a little ritual nobody remembers agreeing to. It started as a joke, and it's still a joke. You just never saw a reason to stop.

At every age, someone has expected you to grow out of it. What you don't say is that you could. Normal isn't a mystery to you: you could do it tomorrow, and nobody would stop you. That's the part that frightens you, more than any look across a room: how quietly it could happen. Meanwhile, next to you, other people get to be a bit ridiculous too, and they are. They feel freer around you than they'd ever think to tell you. That's a bigger gift than being taken seriously.

## yours

1. The way his bar's own book tells it, it started in 1924, when a New York columnist called the regulars of the Paris bars barflies: the ones who never seem to leave. His name, and this is true, was Oscar Odd McIntyre.

2. Then Harry McElhone, a Scot who ran Harry's New York Bar, took it one step further. He called a meeting of barflies at his bar, and that Christmas the International Bar Flies was founded there. McIntyre was made president, "Big Blue Bottlefly". The book lists Harry as "Little Blue Bottlefly". The badge was a housefly on a sugar lump. By 1927 there were over five thousand members, in branches called traps, from Shanghai to Pittsburgh.

3. The joke wasn't his. He just wouldn't let it end where anyone else would have, which is exactly what you do with a joke. And he never stopped. In 1940, with the war closing in on Paris, he locked his papers in a safe, buried it and left. After the war he returned to the bar and picked up where he left off, and his family still runs it. I like to think the silliness was one of the things he kept safe.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is Cameron's Kick, a forgotten classic that first appeared in Harry's 1922 book and again in his bar's 1927 one. It's a Scotch whisky and an Irish whiskey shaken together in equal parts, with lemon and orgeat, a sweet almond syrup. Two countries' whiskies in one glass sounds like it shouldn't work, and it plainly does. Then I added the flies: three coffee beans floated on top, the way sambuca, an Italian anise liqueur, is often served "with the fly", because with a little imagination the beans look like houseflies. Here they're for the Bar Flies. They bring the smell of roasted coffee to every sip, and if you bite one, a bitter crunch against the almond.

5. Some stretches of your life will need you to be serious, and when one comes, you'll manage it. Just don't throw the silliness out with everything else. Put it somewhere safe. When it's over, pick up where you left off: the word, the ritual, the thing you always wear, all of it. Everyone who ever felt a bit freer next to you will be glad to have it back.
