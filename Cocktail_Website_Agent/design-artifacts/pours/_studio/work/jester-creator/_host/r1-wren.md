🪞 **Wren:** Hester, Tomás, here's who's coming to the bar. The full card is in `work/jester-creator/psychologist-persona-card.md`.

**The one true thing.** The Wacky One doesn't do a joke; they adopt one. A made-up word, a hat, a daft ritual, kept up for years, long after anyone's laughing, until it's part of the furniture and other people use it without knowing where it came from. And they know exactly how normal goes. They could do it tomorrow. They choose not to, again and again. No sibling has that: the oddness isn't noticed, planned for others, performed for a reaction or stumbled into. It's *kept*.

**Shows, hides, fears, gives.** They show a person completely at home in being ridiculous (Popeye's "I yam what I yam": no apology, no wink). They hide two things. First, that it's a choice made every day, and some days it costs something (the room that goes quiet, the "are you ever serious?"). Second, the fear under the boredom: not a boring room but a boring *self*. Normal would be easy, and nobody would stop them. Being worn down into it without noticing, above all by the stretches of life that really are serious, is the fear. And at every age someone expects them to grow out of it. The gift, said out loud: next to them, everyone else is free to be a bit ridiculous too. That's not because they planned it for anyone. It's because they went first and were fine.
Line to aim for: *"You could be normal tomorrow. Nobody would stop you. That's the part that scares you."* Tagline direction: *"You made it up. Now everyone says it."*

**Siblings and neighbours.** The Surrealist (*The Other Berry*) notices strangeness, apologises first, and is stung when it's taken as only a joke. This one never apologises and is happy to be the joke. So we don't use "it was never a bit", "the sensible version" or a strange ingredient hidden in a familiar seat. The Morale Booster (*Anyway*) makes fun *for* a group to lift a gloom and keeps a list of flops. Here the freedom others get is a side effect. So we don't use "took it seriously" or "anyway". The Trend Setter (*Still Yours*) likes a real thing early and goes off it when the crowd arrives. This thing is too daft to copy for status, and they're delighted when people take it up. So we don't use "permission" or "keep it". The Naïf doesn't know the rules, and this one knows them perfectly. The Alternative Comic wants a reaction, and this one doesn't need one. The Experimentalist never makes the same thing twice, and this one makes the same odd thing for years.

**Traps.** The sad clown: never suggest the silliness hides sadness. It isn't a mask; it's the face, and that misreading would insult them. No labels ("class clown", "quirky", "random"), only scenes. No flattery. Nothing about drinking, even though the story is a drinkers' society. No sales words ("stunt", "branding") and no motive without "I like to think". No gag drink, because pleasure is the goal. Frames other pours already own: "What nobody sees…", "What you can't stand…", "What you give people…", "Go back to it", "You're allowed to…", the taxi.

**Story test.** Someone who made something plainly ridiculous on purpose (a word, a society, a ritual) and kept it up under their own name for years. It became part of the world, used by people who never knew it started as a joke. Done in the open, never a trick. When life forced seriousness on them, they came back to it.

**Verdict on the plan's lead: accept McElhone. It beats my own sketch.** On the page: the International Bar Flies, "an organization for serious drinkers", with over five thousand members by 1927 and posts called "traps" all over the world (Oxford pdf 1272). He sent "self-deprecating postcards", so he laughed at himself first (pdf 1271). In 1940 he buried his papers in a safe and fled. After the war he returned and "picked up where he left off" (pdf 1272), and his family still runs the bar (pdf 972–973). My sketch wanted someone to push back. The page gives something truer for this guest: no critic, just a stretch of life that had to be serious, and then he came back. My guards: the war gets one plain clause and nothing else from it (no bomb, no son taken prisoner, no Café de Paris). "Picked up where he left off" is the hinge, and the traps and postcards carry the joke. Hester, one check: Oxford prints the co-founder as O. O. "Odd" McIntyre. If Odd really was his name, the Wacky One deserves that coincidence in one plain clause. The backup is Leland's "mixologist", but nobody kept that joke going. The readers did.

**Position (direction only):** some seasons of life will need you serious. When one comes, don't throw the silliness out: put it somewhere safe and pick it back up after. The lighter backup is plain affirmation: don't grow out of it.
