## whoYouAre

Somebody once said something daft in passing, and you didn't let it go. You took it one step further, then another. Now there's a word only your people use, a thing you always wear, a little ritual nobody remembers agreeing to. It started as a joke, and it's still a joke. You just never saw a reason to stop.

At every age, someone has expected you to grow out of it. What you don't say is that you could. Normal isn't a mystery to you: you could do it tomorrow, and nobody would stop you. That's the part that frightens you, more than any look across a room: how quietly it could happen. Meanwhile, next to you, other people get to be a bit ridiculous too, and they are. They feel freer around you than they'd ever think to tell you. That's a bigger gift than being taken seriously.

## yours

1. The way his bar's own book tells it, it started in 1924 with a gloomy magazine piece by a New York columnist, who called the regulars of the Paris bars barflies: the ones who never seem to leave. His name, and this is true, was Oscar Odd McIntyre. Then a nightclub owner advertised that his Christmas Eve takings would go to the "needy barflies".

2. Harry McElhone, a Scot who ran Harry's New York Bar, answered with an advert of his own, calling a meeting of barflies at his bar, and that Christmas the International Bar Flies was founded there. McIntyre was made president, "Big Blue Bottlefly". The book lists Harry as "Little Blue Bottlefly". The badge was a housefly on a sugar lump. By 1927 it had over five thousand members, from Shanghai to Pittsburgh, and its branches were called traps.

3. The joke wasn't his. He just wouldn't let it end where anyone else would have, which is exactly what you do with a joke. And he never stopped. In 1940, with the war closing in, he locked his papers in a safe, buried it and left. After the war he returned to the bar and picked up where he left off. A later booklet of the society lists its president as "Exalted Blue Bottle Fly": Harry. He'd been promoted. I like to think the silliness was one of the things he kept safe.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is Cameron's Kick, a forgotten classic, a whisky sour that first appeared in Harry's 1922 book and again in his bar's 1927 one. It's a Scotch whisky and an Irish whiskey, half each, shaken with lemon and orgeat, a sweet almond syrup. Two countries' whiskies in one glass sounds like it shouldn't work, and it plainly does. I've given it a little more lemon than his book does, and a touch more almond, so it tastes brighter. Then I added the flies: three coffee beans floated on top, the way sambuca, an anise liqueur, is often served "with the fly", because with a little imagination the beans look like houseflies. Here they're for the Bar Flies. They bring the smell of roasted coffee to every sip, and if you bite one, a bitter crunch against the almond.

5. Some stretches of your life will need you to be serious, and when one comes, you'll manage it. It doesn't get to take the silliness with it. When it's over, pick up where you left off: the word, the ritual, the thing you always wear, all of it. You won't be the only one who missed it.

## Resonance

**As the guest (the Wacky One).** *For Kicks* is how I'd explain any of it if you asked: no reason, just for kicks. And the drink's own name is hidden in it, which is my kind of joke. The tagline gives me the moment I secretly love most, hearing my made-up word from someone who doesn't know it was mine. The epigraph stands cold. It's a drink with flies on it and a bartender who says it's a long story, so I want the story. whoYouAre ¶1 is my life with no apology in it: the daft remark I didn't let go of, and "it's still a joke". Nobody asks me to mean it more seriously than I do. ¶2 names the fear I've never said out loud, which isn't the look across the room but how quietly I could turn normal. Then it says plainly what I give, and that it's worth something. The line I'd quote: **"Normal isn't a mystery to you: you could do it tomorrow, and nobody would stop you."** The story is the best kind of daft: a gloomy article, a joke advert, then Harry calling an actual meeting and turning up in the minutes as "Little Blue Bottlefly". After the war he came back, and by the later booklet he's "Exalted". "He'd been promoted" made me laugh out loud. y5 tells me something hard, that some stretches will need me serious, then hands the silliness back and tells me I won't be the only one who missed it. The closing line makes that a habit I can keep: three flies, and start again at three.

**As the sceptic.**
- *Horoscope:* one generic sentence ("At every age, someone has expected you to grow out of it"), narrowed at once by the next three. Everything else is specific. Passes.
- *Swap, same family.* The Morale Booster (*Anyway*): no planning for a group, no gloom to lift, no list, no "took it seriously". The meeting is one clause, and no rules are quoted. The other nine are unauthored. By their tests: no reaction sought (the Alternative Comic), no rule unknown (the Naïf), no trick (the Elf/Imp), no target (the Scandalmonger).
- *Swap, cousins.* The Surrealist: no "this is going to sound weird", no "I meant it", no hidden swap; the fly is on top, in plain sight. The Trend Setter: **near miss owned.** The tagline's "Now everyone says it" sits near *Still Yours*'s "you notice other people doing it too". "Made it up" is the difference: invention, not taste arriving early, and there's no eye-rolling or going off it. The Experimentalist: the same thing kept for years. The Visionary: nothing handed on down a chain.
- *4-grams* against every pour, including Tomás's closing line: clean after eight rewrites (v2 added "just don't let it", which was Down the Line's).
- *Voice:* one "I", no "we/our/us", the handover phrase for the drink, no guest pronouns, no archetype names. The drink isn't in the guest's hand. The coincidence is owned in one clause, the romance is signposted ("I like to think…"), and the source is labelled ("The way his bar's own book tells it"). No "strong" and no "kick" as a strength claim.
- *History:* the one soft spot. It's about 235 of yours' 429 words (55%) and 41% of the whole reading (578 words). I took the later booklet because "He'd been promoted" is the story's best line for this guest and it brings the society back as well as the bar. If the host holds the rule to yours alone, the first cut is y2's badge sentence (9 words); its tie then moves into y4's "Here they're for the Bar Flies, whose badge was a housefly".
- *Robin's rules:* the kindness is said aloud ("That's a bigger gift than being taken seriously"). The twist is tied to the story in words ("Here they're for the Bar Flies"). The drink sounds worth having ("a forgotten classic", "it plainly does", "tastes brighter"). The change to the recipe is owned. The ending is on what the guest gains. The legend (the dead-fly badges) isn't used.
- *Punch:* the whoYouAre opening is the strongest. The tagline is the weakest of the three, because of the Trend Setter near miss, but I'd keep it.

**Why I conceded the name.** Tomás conceded to *Not a Phase* in his draft, and in the same round I conceded to *For Kicks*. On the merits I'm taking his. (1) "For kicks" is the persona's motive, nonsense for the pleasure of it, and the reading otherwise leans on the fear, so the name brings the pleasure back. (2) "It's not a phase" is the line many people have heard when they came out. Borrowing it for silliness could sting a guest for whom it isn't funny, and guest dignity comes first. (3) *Not a Phase* only repeats what the tagline and whoYouAre ¶2 already say, while *For Kicks* adds something and carries the drink's name.
