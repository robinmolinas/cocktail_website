# Persona card: The Wacky One (jester-creator)

Wren, round 1, 2026-10-01. Working notes for the room; nothing here ships.

## Core truth
The Wacky One doesn't do a joke, they adopt one: a made-up word, a hat, a daft ritual, kept up for years, long after anyone is laughing, until it stops being a joke and becomes part of the furniture, and other people use it without knowing where it came from. And they know exactly how normal goes. They could do it tomorrow. They choose not to, again and again.

## Shows / hides
- **Shows:** completely at home in being ridiculous. No apology, no wink (Popeye's "I yam what I yam" is exactly the stance).
- **Hides:** (1) that it's a choice, made daily, and some days it costs something: the room that goes quiet, the "are you ever serious?". (2) The fear under the Jester's boredom: not a boring room, a boring *self*. Normal would be easy, and nobody would stop them.

## Hidden fear -> hidden gift
- **Fear:** being worn down into normal without noticing, above all by the stretches of life that really are serious; and, at every age, people expecting them to grow out of it.
- **Gift (said out loud, per Robin's batch-caregiver rule):** next to them, everyone else is free to be a bit ridiculous too, and is. Not because they planned it for anyone. Because they went first and were fine.

## The "real me" line to aim for
- "You could be normal tomorrow. Nobody would stop you. That's the part that scares you."
- Tagline direction (invites, positive): "You made it up. Now everyone says it." Alternatives: "People keep waiting for you to grow out of it."

## Truth vs. legend
They want the true story told with a straight face, and the absurd parts left absurd. They don't need it explained or softened. Legends told as his stories (the lions) are fine as colour, never as fact.

## Tone
Witty and playful (the Jester line), with the Creator's pride in the made thing. The bartender plays along: deadpan, a little silly, enjoying it. Funny is allowed here, and I'll fight for it. The fear paragraph can go quiet for a moment, then the reading comes back up.

## Distinct from siblings and neighbours
- **The Surrealist** (*The Other Berry*, creator-jester): notices strangeness, apologises first ("this is going to sound weird"), is stung when it's taken as only a joke. The Wacky One never apologises and is happy to be the joke. Owned there: "None of it is a performance… you meant it", "the sensible version… sanded down", "a second look", a strange ingredient hidden in a familiar seat, "What you can't stand…", "What you give people…".
- **The Morale Booster** (*Anyway*): makes fun for a group, on purpose, to lift a gloom; keeps the list of flops. The Wacky One makes nonsense for its own pleasure; the freedom others get is a side effect. Owned there: "Half the fun is that you took it seriously", "There's a list", "anyway".
- **The Trend Setter** (*Still Yours*): likes a real thing early, "a word" among them, and goes off it when everyone arrives. The Wacky One's thing is too daft to copy for status, and they're delighted when others take it up. Owned there: "permission", "wear it… anyway, out loud", keep it.
- **The Naïf** doesn't know the rules; the Wacky One knows them perfectly and declines. **The Alternative Comic** breaks a form once for a reaction; the Wacky One doesn't need one. **The Elf/Imp** plays a trick straight; the Wacky One has no trick. **The Experimentalist** (*Off-Label*) never makes the same thing twice; the Wacky One makes the same odd thing for years.

## Traps
1. **The sad clown.** Never suggest the silliness hides sadness. It isn't a mask; it's the face. That misreading would insult them.
2. **Labels:** "class clown", "the office joker", "quirky", "random", "free spirit". Scenes only.
3. **Flattery** instead of recognition.
4. **Alcohol:** the story is a drinkers' society. The reading is about nonsense, never drinking; no drunk-patron tags.
5. **Sales:** Oxford frames McElhone as branding. No "stunt", "branding", "publicity" in guest text, and no motive either way without "I like to think".
6. **A gag drink.** The goal is pleasure: it must taste plainly good. No Popeye, no spinach.
7. **Owned frames and motifs:** "What nobody sees…" (Overnight), "What you can't stand…" / "What you give people…" (The Other Berry), "Go back to it" (Off-Label; so "come back for it" needs care), "You're allowed to…" (Four Shares), "There's a list" and "anyway" (Anyway), taxi (*Worth the Trip*), "permission" (Still Yours).

## Story test
Someone who made something plainly ridiculous on purpose (a word, a society, a ritual), kept it up under their own name for years, and it became part of the world, used by people who never knew it began as a joke. In the open, never a trick or a secret. When life forced seriousness on them, they came back to it.
Out: mistakes, hoaxes, one-off stunts, strangeness merely noticed (Surrealist), fun planned to cheer a group (Booster), taste that was simply early (Trend Setter).

## Verdict on the plan's lead (Harry McElhone and the IBF): accept
Read on the page (Oxford MCELHONE pdf 1271-1272; HARRY'S NEW YORK BAR pdf 972-973):
- The IBF, "an organization for serious drinkers", 1924, with O. O. "Odd" McIntyre; by 1927 over five thousand members, with posts, or "traps", all over the world, and it "kept growing for years" (pdf 1272).
- "Self-deprecating postcards" among his paper jokes (pdf 1271): he laughed at himself first.
- 1940: locked his important papers in a safe, buried it outside Paris, fled. After the war he returned and "picked up where he left off", running the bar until his death (pdf 1272). The family still runs it (pdf 972-973); the bar still holds a straw poll for every US presidential election (pdf 973).
- My sketch asked for push-back. The page gives something truer for this person: no critic, just a stretch of life that had to be serious, and he came back. That answers this guest's real fear.

**Guards I add:** the war gets one plain clause and nothing more (no bomb, no son a prisoner, no Café de Paris). "Picked up where he left off" is the hinge, not the war. The traps and the postcards carry the joke; the lapel tags stay out. For Hester: check what "Odd" was (Oxford prints it in quotes). If it was his given name, it's a coincidence to own in one plain clause; if a nickname, still usable, told as a nickname. Precision for me too: five thousand *members* whose branches were called traps, never "five thousand people using his word".

**Backup:** Leland's "mixologist" (a joke word that everyone ended up using in earnest) fits "invent a word", but nobody kept it up: readers did. Only if McElhone can't be kept light.

## Position (direction, not ruled)
Not "keep going anyway" (Booster), not "stop apologising" (Surrealist), not "keep it" (Trend Setter).
- **Lead:** some seasons of your life will need you serious. When one comes, don't throw the silliness out. Put it somewhere safe, and pick it back up after. Honest, tender, and it answers the fear with the story's own shape. Watch the gain (Robin: end on what the guest gains) and Off-Label's "Go back to it".
- **Lighter backup:** plain affirmation: don't grow out of it.

## For Tomás (person-side only)
The oddness should be out loud and on the label, a mash-up that obviously shouldn't work and plainly does. Cameron's Kick (two countries' whiskies in one sour, first printed in his 1922 *ABC*, per the plan) is that, and it's the story in the glass. The spark should add one daft touch that's still delicious; this guest would rather see it than find it. The Boulevardier without the nut veto is fine by me if it's an equal drink.
