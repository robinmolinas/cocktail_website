## whoYouAre

On any walk, you're a few steps behind. Something stopped you: frost that's stayed only in the shadow of a fence, a bee climbing into a flower the wrong way round, a sign you've passed a hundred times and have only now read properly. Somewhere up ahead, someone says "come on". You've been hearing "come on" for as long as you can remember.

You were never dawdling. You wanted to see what it would do. You don't often say how much a small thing can make your day, because it can sound naive, and there's an old, faint guilt about holding everyone up. It isn't being slow that scares you. It's getting busy, and sure, and one day walking straight past something without feeling the tug, and not even missing it. The people who walk with you see more than they would on their own. The ordinary comes back to them new. That's what your slowness gives them, and every so often the thing you stopped for turns out to matter to everyone.

## yours

1. Most of us drink a Manhattan within minutes of it being made. Around 2004, in London, a bartender named Tony Conigliaro started putting some aside instead: Manhattans, and a Cuban classic called the El Presidente, in glass, for years. His question came from wine. Wine changes slowly as it ages, and he wondered whether a mixed drink could be helped the same way, if it was given the time. For years he tried to interest his customers in the idea. Nobody paid it a bit of attention, he said.
2. In 2009 he opened a bar of his own, a small one on a side street in Islington. That October, a bartender from Portland, Oregon, Jeffrey Morgenthaler, was in town, and was told to ask for the Manhattan that wasn't on the menu. It was five years old. Conigliaro said the years had brought its flavours together. Morgenthaler, by his own account, thought it tasted like a Manhattan. He went home and tried the idea anyway, in a small wooden barrel, where it takes weeks instead of years. His first batch was gone in a week or two, and before long bars across the world were ageing cocktails in barrels.
3. I like to think Conigliaro gave that bottle its years because he trusted there was something in it, while nobody else was interested yet. That's you. You give an ordinary thing more time than anyone else would, before there's any sign it'll be worth it. And look at what travelled. Not the taste: the man who took it home could hardly tell. It was the question. What happens if you don't hurry? You hand people that question all the time, and some of them take it further than you'd ever have guessed.
4. The cocktail I've made for you is an El Presidente, a classic from Havana, and a stirred one: white rum, a sweet white vermouth, with orange curaçao and grenadine, a spoonful each, for colour and roundness. My change is in the wine. I've swapped part of the vermouth for Madeira, a wine the law won't let anyone sell until it has had years, because Conigliaro's question came from wine. So some of the time he was asking about is already in the glass. I like to think it's a nod to Morgenthaler too: his first barrel had held Madeira. It's stirred cold, poured into a small stemmed glass, and it's ready the moment it's made. You're usually the one giving things time. This once, somebody else did the waiting for you.
5. Busy comes for everyone, and the stopping goes first. You won't hear it go. So have one thing going that can't be hurried: a cutting in a jar on the windowsill, a question you haven't looked up yet. Look in on it now and then. And when someone up ahead says "come on", take the minute. It's usually the best one of the walk.
