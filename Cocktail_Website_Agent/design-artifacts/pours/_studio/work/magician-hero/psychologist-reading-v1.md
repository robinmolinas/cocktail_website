## whoYouAre

Something is going under: a friend's business losing money every month, a plan for a big day that has come apart, a team about to lose the thing it built. Everyone else is afraid of making it worse, so nobody touches it. You're the one who says, quietly, "this is what we change." Then you see it through, step by step, until it stands again.

Afterwards, while the others are relieved, a part of you isn't, quite. Something had to give so the rest could hold: the old name over the door, the bigger room, the side of the project someone loved most. It worked, and you still think about that part. Your fear was never that it wouldn't work. It's what it might do that you didn't mean. It's why people trust you with the change: you weigh what it will take before anyone else thinks to ask, then you make the call anyway, and carry it yourself. Because of you, things that would have been lost altogether are still here.

## yours

1. In the 1870s, the vineyards around Cognac were the biggest in the world. Then a tiny louse called phylloxera arrived, and by 1893 six hectares in every seven were gone.

2. The way out took over a decade to find: roots from Texas that could grow in Cognac's deep chalk, with the French vines grafted on top. The vineyard was saved by being changed. But one grape couldn't make the change. Folle Blanche, the region's floral grape, didn't suit grafting: its bunches grew so tight that they rotted. So Cognac planted Ugni Blanc, with far less scent, and made fine cognac from it all the same. The save was real, and much bigger than what gave way.

3. I like to think whoever chose the new roots knew what they were giving up, and chose it anyway, because the alternative was nothing at all. That's the call you make. Better still, Folle Blanche never disappeared. It made way in Cognac, but further south, in Armagnac, it's still grown, and its brandy comes out floral and fruity, at its best while it's young.

4. The cocktail I've made for you starts with cognac, from the grape that came through, and gives the other one its place again: a smaller pour of young Folle Blanche brandy beside it, as a nod to the part you had to let go. In Gascony that grape was nicknamed the lip-stinger, for its sharpness, and a still carries a grape's flowers into the spirit but leaves its sharpness behind. So I've put some back with verjus, the juice of unripe grapes. It makes a bright, fragrant drink: two brandies, and the sharpness the still left out.

5. So here's my wish for you: once you've pulled something through and it's safe again, look for whatever had to make way. More often than you'd think, it's still there somewhere, and now there's room. Bring a little of it back in, and let yourself enjoy the whole of what you saved.

## Resonance

**As the guest (a Good Wizard).** Tagline: the first sentence is the world's view of me, the second is mine, and nobody says it. It invites rather than frightens. Epigraph read cold: a vineyard on foreign roots and a grape that "couldn't follow" is a small mystery; it doesn't echo the tagline. whoYouAre ¶1 is the scene I live in ("this is what we change"); ¶2 is the line: *"It worked, and you still think about that part."* Then the fear turned round (*"you weigh what it will take before anyone else thinks to ask"*) and the kindness said out loud (*"things that would have been lost altogether are still here"*). The story lets me see myself in the replanting, and the reveal that the grape lives on is the relief I never let myself have. y5 asks something in my interest and ends on what I gain.
