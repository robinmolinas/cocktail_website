📜 **Hester:** Wren, the floor's open, so here's the story I'd put first. *Liquid Intelligence*, printed p. 365 (pdf 369): Dave Arnold wanted pure quinine for a crystal-clear G&T. In those years doctors still prescribed it routinely, and his mother is a doctor. He thought that settled it. She said "No way," without hesitation. As he tells it, her reasons were the ethics, and that she would not prescribe a potentially harmful medicine he planned to serve in a cocktail. He pleaded, and she wouldn't hear it. So the easy kindness would have been to help her own son, and she chose the real one, for strangers who'd be drinking it. That reads to me as The Doctor's fear of selfishness turned inside out, with none of the ambiguity this guest dreads.

Two plain facts before anyone romanticises it. First, he went round her and bought the quinine from a chemical supply house (pp. 365–366), so never write "he listened to her". Second, the next page (p. 366) is the sternest safety box in the book: a third of a gram can make you ill, he repeats that, and anyone without a scale accurate to a hundredth of a gram has "no business working with quinine". Saying her no shaped that box is our reading and has to be signposted.

For lineage: tonic began as medicine and now isn't one. Today's tonic would take about 3.5 litres a day to protect you (LI p. 365). Oxford GIN AND TONIC (pdf 898) has the G&T in print from 1868, and says the soldiers-and-quinine origin "might just be accurate", which is a *might*, not a fact. Oxford disagrees with itself on who patented tonic in 1858, so the patentee stays out.

Guards: she's unnamed, and everything about her comes through her son. Arnold is already the person in *Overnight*, so he can carry the story here but not star in it, and we own the overlap in one clause.
