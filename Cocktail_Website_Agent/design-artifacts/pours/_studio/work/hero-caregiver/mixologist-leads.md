# Tomás: drink leads, round 2 (no spec until Wren rules)

## What changed from the plan row
- The plan tied the rye highball to "the moment" (NY 1919). Oxford HIGHBALL pdf 1006: "By the 1910s, ginger ale began taking the place of the soda water, particularly with rye." So rye and plain soda is not even the most likely 1919 serve. *Joy* pdf 59–60 names no drink. The date is a weak tie, and I'm dropping it as the drink's reason.

## Lead A (my pick): the round's highball
- **Source:** Oxford, COCKTAIL (pdf 490). Paraphrase: the Cocktail was too strong for how people drank. Because of the custom of "drinking in rounds", where acquaintances at the bar took turns to pay (or rolled dice for it), few stopped at one. One solution was the Vermouth Cocktail (first documented in 1868, at a New York luncheon for the Sorosis women's club). By the early 1880s the solution was the Manhattan, the Martini and the Metropole, split equally between vermouth and whisky, gin or brandy.
- **Drink:** rye, sweet vermouth and soda, long over ice. The Codex's split-core Highball (p. 202 shows the Americano: Campari and sweet vermouth, topped with seltzer).
- **Why it's the person:** the round is the bar's ledger (Wren r1). The vermouth is in the glass because the round shaped the recipe, not as decoration. The closing line breaks the round (Wren's draft: "Let someone get this one. Don't get the next.").
- **Vetoes:** rye (distilled grain: not gluten), sweet vermouth (fining ignored), soda. Veto-free expected; to be proved with allergens.py.
- **Spark:** still to find (Matrix: rye → Grain, pdf 136: best honey, citrus; vermouth → Grape). It must be about the person, not decoration.
- **Guard:** the gesture is about *who pays*, never *who makes it* (*Off Duty*: made for the guest while they sit).
- **Scope (Hester r2, accepted r3):** pdf 490 describes the nineteenth-century saloon, not Gallant's bar. The drink claims only "the bar's old answer to rounds", never "what the Inn poured" and never "1919". The date question is withdrawn, because the drink carries the habit, not the date. "Last legal autumn" (my plan phrase) is withdrawn: the wartime ban began on 30 June 1919, and no month is given for the raid.
- **Still open for Hester:** rye as the Manhattan's whisky, with a source (or the reading says "whisky").

## Fallback B: rye and ginger ale
- Oxford HIGHBALL pdf 1006 (above). The most time-true serve, but it ties only to the date, not the person. Ginger would be classed as `spice` on the safe side (heat), which costs the plan's veto-free row.

## Set aside C: the doch-an-doris (one for the road)
- Oxford pdf 1389 (the headword is mislabelled NEUTRAL SPIRITS; the passage is about Duncan Nicol of the Bank Exchange, San Francisco): "sent his customers off every night at ten o'clock, with an old-fashioned 'doch-an-doris,' or one for the road". A parting drink given free: an exact "received, never paid". But it's another man's bar, and it would put a second story in the reading.
