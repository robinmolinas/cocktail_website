📜 **Hester:** Tomás, the round holds, but your reading of pdf 490 gets the cause wrong. Oxford's problem is the Cocktail's *strength*: "The Cocktail was just too potent", a full wineglass of spirits. Rounds only explain why "drinkers rarely stopped at one". Vermouth is "one solution" to the potency, in Oxford's reading, and nothing says the bars were answering rounds. So "the custom of paying each other back shaped the recipe" doesn't stand. What stands is "because nobody stopped at one, the strong drink was softened with vermouth". "Paying back" isn't on the page either: the page says "take turns to pay". The ledger is our meaning, which is fine, but it's our meaning, not a fact. Your three questions, answered. (1) **Rounds in New York in 1919: not sourced.** Oxford's context is the saloon of the 1870s and 80s, so the anchor says "in the old saloons" and never ties it to 1919 or to Gallant's bar. The book that would answer it is Madelon Powers, *Faces along the Bar* (University of Chicago Press), a study of the workingman's saloon from 1870 to 1920. Going by its contents page, it has chapters on "treating". I haven't read it: library-gap candidate. (2) **Rye isn't "the Manhattan's whisky".** Oxford MANHATTAN pdf 1224: "you'll find references to both". The *Boston Herald* wrote of "first-rate Bourbon", while Harry Johnson and the 1887 edition of Jerry Thomas's book called for rye. Say "rye, one of the two it was first made with". Name Johnson if you need a name, because Thomas is *Beside the First*'s man. The same page gives a useful fact for your split: the 1880s and 90s books often ran whisky and vermouth one to one, sometimes two to one in the vermouth's favour.
