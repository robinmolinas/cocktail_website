## whoYouAre

Something goes wrong for someone near you: the bill nobody can split, the call nobody wants to make, the mistake that was only half theirs. You don't rush. You weigh it up, quietly, and then you say it: "Put it on me." And they go home lighter. That's what you give people, and it's worth far more than you ever let them tell you.

What you can't do is the other half. Someone does you a small kindness, a lift, a meal, an evening of their time, and it's paid back within the week, with a little extra. Owing anyone, even briefly, feels like weakness to you, like leaving a door open that someone could walk through. So your books are always in credit. Plenty of people owe you something. You've never asked for any of it back.

## yours

1. In 1919, months before Prohibition reached the whole country, a wartime law had already made selling drink illegal. That year, the Greenwich Village Inn in New York, which a man named Barney Gallant ran, was raided, and he was arrested along with half a dozen of his waiters. Barney agreed to plead guilty on one condition: that his waiters were let go.

2. He was sentenced to thirty days in jail, and he served far less. Yes, his name really was Gallant. You could say it was his place to answer for, and maybe it was simply good business. But I like to think it was more than that: that he looked at six people who'd only been carrying the trays and decided the cost was his.

3. You'd have made the same decision, and you'd have called it only fair. What I've always wondered is how it felt for those waiters, going home owing someone that much. You've stood where Barney stood more times than you can count. You've never let yourself stand where they did.

4. The cocktail I've made for you comes out of the old saloons, where friends drank in rounds, taking turns to pay, and hardly anyone stopped after the first. A strong drink was too much for that, and one answer was to split the whisky half and half with vermouth, a wine flavoured with herbs: that's where the Manhattan, a renowned classic, and its family come from. So I kept the old equal parts, rye and sweet vermouth poured to the same line twice, with two dashes of bitters, then let it run long over ice with soda and a twist of lemon over the top: bittersweet, bright and easy to linger over. I kept the split for what you won't allow yourself: something else carrying half the weight, so everyone can keep going.

5. So when someone does something for you, don't pay it back. No dinner in return, no bigger present, no "I'll get you back for this". Every time you square it straight away, you take something from them: the feeling you get every time you say "put it on me". Let them keep that. And for once, you get to be the one who's let off.

**closing line (Tomás's, spec v1):** *Let someone get this one. Don't get the next.* Read against the reading: it agrees with the position (y5), not the creed; it's bar-sized where y5 is life-sized, so they don't repeat each other; and it answers the name.
