## whoYouAre

Something goes wrong for someone near you: the bill nobody can split, the call nobody wants to make, the mistake that was only half theirs. You don't rush. You weigh it up, quietly, and then you say it: "Put it on me." And they go home lighter. That's what you give people, and it's worth far more than you ever let them tell you.

What you can't do is the other half. Someone does you a small kindness, a lift, a meal, an evening of their time, and it's paid back within the week, with a little extra. Owing anyone, even briefly, feels like weakness to you, like leaving a door open that someone could walk through. So your books are always in credit. Plenty of people owe you something. You've never asked for any of it back.

## yours

1. In 1919, months before Prohibition reached the whole country, a wartime law had already made selling drink illegal. That year, the Greenwich Village Inn in New York, which a man named Barney Gallant ran, was raided, and he was arrested along with half a dozen of his waiters. Barney agreed to plead guilty on one condition: that his waiters were let go.

2. He was sentenced to thirty days in jail, and he served far less. Yes, his name really was Gallant. You could say it was his place to answer for, and maybe it was simply good business. But I like to think it was more than that: that he looked at six people who'd only been carrying the trays and decided the cost was his.

3. You'd have made the same decision, and you'd have called it only fair. What I've always wondered is how it felt for those waiters, going home that night owing someone that much. You've stood where Barney stood more times than you can count. You've never let yourself stand where they did.

4. The cocktail I've made for you comes out of the old saloons, where friends drank in rounds, taking turns to pay, and hardly anyone stopped at one. A strong drink was too much for that, and one answer was to split the whisky with sweet vermouth, half and half: that's where the Manhattan and its family come from. I kept that split ⟨rye and sweet vermouth in equal parts⟩, and let it run long over ice with soda ⟨+ the spark, if Tomás finds one, tied to the person in one clause⟩. I kept it for what you won't allow yourself: something else carrying half the weight, so everyone can keep going.

5. So the next time someone does something for you, don't pay it back. No dinner in return, no bigger present, not this week, not next. Every time you square it straight away, you take something from them: the feeling you get every time you say "put it on me". Let them keep that. And for once, you get to be the one who's let off.

**closing line (Tomás's; my suggestion, his call):** *Let someone get this one. Don't get the next.*

## Resonance

**As the guest (a Saviour).** Tagline: "You'll take anything on for anyone. Except a favour." I'd smile, then stop: the second sentence is the part I'd never say out loud. whoYouAre: "You don't rush. You weigh it up" is right. I'm not the one who runs in, I'm the one who decides. "Paid back within the week, with a little extra" is the "how did they know?" line, because it's a habit, not a virtue, and nobody has ever named it to me. "Like leaving a door open that someone could walk through" names why, without calling me controlling. The story: I'd enjoy that he wasn't a saint ("maybe it was simply good business"). The turn in y3 ("You've never let yourself stand where they did") is what I'd resist, which is why it's right. Proposal: "you take something from them: the feeling you get every time you say 'put it on me'" reframes receiving as giving, the only argument a Saviour would accept.
