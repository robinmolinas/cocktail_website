# Persona card: The Saviour (hero-caregiver)

Wren, round 1, 2026-09-30. Sources: `persona.py hero-caregiver` (both sheets), my family sketch (`plans/work/hero/psychologist-sketches.md`), the Hero plan row, *Joy* pdf 59–60 re-opened on the page today, and a grep of every pour's guest text (Cocktail to Dossier) for the frames below.

## Core truth
They'll take the cost in someone else's place, calmly and on purpose, and they can't stand to be on the other side of that deal. Owing anyone anything feels like weakness. So their private books are always kept in credit: plenty of people owe them, they never collect, and they settle every kindness done to them within the week.

## Shows / hides
- **Shows:** "Put it on me." The bill, the harder half of the job, the phone call nobody wants to make, the mistake that was only partly theirs, the late shift so a colleague can get to the school play. Steady, unfussy, a little stern with themselves. They call it duty (the sheet's own word) or "only fair".
- **Hides:** how humiliating it feels to need anyone. The speed of the repayment: a lift to the airport paid back with a dinner, the dinner with a bigger present. The private sums. And under the sums, a small unadmitted safety: being owed is safe, but owing means someone could come to collect, and then you'd be at their mercy. Also that they're tired, because tired sounds weak.

## Hidden fear, and the gift it becomes
- **Fear:** weakness, specifically being the one who has to be carried, being in debt, being let off.
- **Gift (say it out loud, Robin's caregiver rule):** someone else gets let off: the cost, the trouble, the bad night. Somebody goes home lighter because of them. That's worth something, and the reading must say so plainly, once.
- **The turn the position needs:** when they pay a kindness straight back, they take something from the other person: the feeling they themselves get every time they help. Letting someone help them gives that person the thing they value most.

## The "real me" line to aim for
- *You'll take anything on for anyone. What you can't take is a favour.*
- The scene version: someone does them one small kindness, and it's paid back, with interest, by Friday. They've done a hundred for other people and never asked for one back.
- Title-block direction (positive frame, intrigue): the gift, or the "put it on me" habit. Not the invisibility of the gift: the Angel owns "nobody saw you do anything".

## Truth vs. legend
The true story, plainly, and unsentimental. They distrust anything that makes sacrifice holy or grand. It's a relief to them that Gallant wasn't a saint: a Village innkeeper who took a plea, served far less than his sentence, and opened one of the swankiest clubs in town a couple of months later. Lightness here is a kindness: it takes the martyr out.

## Tone
From the sheet: assertive and empowering (primary), compassionate and kind (secondary). For the bartender: steady, direct, warm and a little dry. No rousing speech and no fuss, because they'd hate being fussed over. Brief, respectful, one dry smile. The position can be firm; they respect firmness.

## Distinct from siblings and near pours
- **The Rescuer, *Off Duty* (closest risk).** Theirs is the emergency: speed, clarity, the shaking afterwards, being made a drink and sitting down. Ours has no emergency. It's a deliberate trade made calmly, often at a desk or a kitchen table, sometimes stretched over years (paying a sibling's fees, taking the job nobody wants). Fear: owing, not standing back. Ask: stay in someone's debt, not "let them do the going". Off-limits wording: "already moving", "by the time anyone else has decided", "the one who comes", "clear", "Nobody sees the part that comes later", hands shaking, sitting down while someone makes it.
- **The Warrior.** Holds a line in front of a group and fears faltering in view. The Saviour gives something of their own away. So Gallant must never read as "the boss standing up for the team". He made a trade: himself instead of them.
- **The Dashing Hero:** shields someone from danger, fears losing them. The Saviour takes on a cost, not a danger.
- **The Firefighter:** a crew, credit handed on. The Saviour acts alone, and it's about cost, not credit.
- **The True Friend, *Four Shares*:** being looked after, keeping a share back. The Saviour is about owing, and the position isn't "you're allowed to be looked after too".
- **Owned frames to avoid (grep of guest text):** "blamed for all of it" (*Making the Calls*: so no "blame" in the title block); "the price… worth paying" and "never once" (*Till Spring*); "it was nothing" (*Not Only the Way*: the Saviour's deflection is "it was only fair", not "nothing"); "Nobody thanks you, because nobody saw" (*Before the Room*); "carry on" (*Down the Line*). Free, checked: owe, owing, debt, favour, in credit, "put it on me", "on the house", "one condition", "IOU".

## Traps
1. **The atonement shape.** One man pleads guilty so the others go free: that's the exact shape of the sheet's own example. The story is still ours (secular, a nightclub, told dry), but the words must never reach for it: no "paid the price", "in our place", "took their punishment", "redeem", "forgive debts", "sacrifice", "martyr", "save/saviour". "Set free" is the page's wording: paraphrase it plainly ("on condition his waiters were let go"). Nothing in the glass or the image that reads as religion: no red wine as blood, no bread, no cross, no rays breaking through darkness (the sheet's imagery would read as a halo), no "thirty" or silver motif, no chains, cell bars or handcuffs either.
2. **Praise.** "Selfless", "generous", "noble". The name coincidence (his surname is Gallant) is owned once, about him, and never pointed at the guest.
3. **Diagnosis.** "Saviour complex", people-pleaser, "need to be needed". Never suggest they help to feel above anyone. The debt is self-protection, not control.
4. **Admiring the overload** (First Law). The reading can't make carrying too much sound noble. The position has to be in their interest.
5. **Inflating Gallant.** Never "served thirty days" (it was the sentence; "he served far less"). "First person in Manhattan jailed under Prohibition laws" is Walker's claim via Regan: attribute it or drop it. One act, not a character: the house rules on pdf 60 tell guests not to overtip their waiter ("Why be a chump?") and to check the bill, so never "he always looked after his staff".
6. **Motive.** Not on the page. It may have been good business. Grant that first ("Maybe it was just good business"), then signpost ("I like to think…"). Don't claim the same waiters worked at Club Gallant.
7. **Outlaw ground.** The raid is the setting, one clause, never glamour.
8. **Hierarchy.** The guest may be nobody's boss. whoYouAre's scenes stay ordinary and level: siblings, friends, colleagues, an ageing parent.
9. **Family rations.** At most one of brave, courage, hero, strong, fight. Never "save".
10. **Gender.** None about the guest. "His waiters" is fine (third parties). Club Gallant's gendered rules stay out.

## Story test, and the verdict on the plan's lead
**Test:** a person gave up something real of their own so that a *specific* other person was spared; it visibly cost them; best if they also refuse help or can't bear to owe. Out: rescue in danger, snow, hot drinks for a helper, dying, medicine.

**Gallant (*Joy* pdf 59): accept, with conditions.**
- Passes: specific others (half a dozen of his waiters); a real cost on his own record (a guilty plea, a thirty-day sentence); deliberate, even a bargain ("providing the waiters were set free"); ordinary working life, no rush and no danger. That puts clear water between this pour and the Rescuer.
- The sceptic's objection helps: "it was his inn, so it was his to take." That's exactly what the Saviour says about themselves ("only fair", "duty"). The reading grants it and lets the guest recognise their own deflection.
- Fails / off the page: the owing half. Nothing says the waiters owed him or tried to repay. So the mirror for that half is signposted wonder, not claim: I've always wondered what it felt like for those waiters to walk out owing someone that much. You know his side by heart. You've never let yourself stand on theirs.
- **Weakest link: the glass.** The plan's rye highball has no tie to the story or the person. The lesson "the story has to be in the glass" applies, so this is round 2's job, not a footnote. What the person needs the drink to prove is the position: something received that can't be paid back. Lead for Tomás: the ledger of drinking is the round (the Saviour always gets the next one in). "Put it on my tab." A closing line in that territory could land: let someone get this one, and don't get the next.
- **Dabney:** I've re-read my ruling with fresh eyes and I stand by it.
- **Haimo** stays the floor backup (fails "specific other" and "visible cost").
- Library search today for alternatives (took the blame, repaid, own pocket, lent money, Toots Shor, the rap, on the cuff): nothing that passes. Gallant is the only clean fit.

## Position direction
Stay in someone's debt. Next time someone does something for them, let it stand unpaid: no dinner back, no bigger present. What they gain: the other person gets the feeling they get every time they help, and they get to be let off, for once. End on what the guest gains (Robin's rule), not on restraint.

## Round 3 ruling (Wren)
- **Story ruled: Gallant**, with Hester's strikes taken into the card: "the inn he ran" (never his own inn); the sceptic's line becomes "it was his place to answer for"; no "first" of any kind; Club Gallant opened "later", no date; the house rules are the 1924 club's, dossier only; that the waiters went free lives only inside the signposted wonder, one sentence.
- **"Thirty":** the fact may be stated once ("sentenced to thirty days, and served far less": the dry lightness the person needs). My ban is on echoing it anywhere as a motif (no 30 ml, nothing counted in thirties).
- **Drink direction accepted: rye, sweet vermouth, soda, long.** Verified Oxford COCKTAIL pdf 490 myself: rounds (turns to pay, or dice) meant few stopped at one; the answer was vermouth, and by the early 1880s drinks split equally between vermouth and whisky, gin or brandy. Tomás's paraphrase is accurate.
- **What the glass proves for this person:** the split. Half the weight carried by something else, so the round could go on. That is the Saviour's position in the glass (let something else carry half), and the closing line breaks the round. Say it once, in one clause, not as a list.
- **Guards:** the round is the old saloon's custom, never bridged to Gallant's inn or to 1919; no need to claim rye as the Manhattan's whisky (the page says "whisky": rye is Tomás's craft call, never "the original"); Sorosis stays out; history stays under half (Gallant y1–y3, the round one or two sentences in y4); who pays, never who makes (*Off Duty*); the drink must sound worth having (name the family it comes from: the Manhattan's); the spark serves the split or the person, not decoration. Doch-an-doris: dossier only.
