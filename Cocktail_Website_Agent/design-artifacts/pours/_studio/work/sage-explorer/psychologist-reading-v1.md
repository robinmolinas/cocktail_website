## whoYouAre

Over dinner, a friend explains a lovely theory about how some famous thing really began. It's neat, it has a name and a date in it, and you love it at once. Then, once the table's cleared, you're still in your chair, turning it over: all right, and if that's true, then what? People take that for doubt. It's closer to the reverse. You fall for ideas faster than anyone you know, which is exactly why you check the ones you love.

Privately, you want the neat answer to be true as badly as anyone. That's why it's the one you trust least. Liars don't worry you much. Your own delight does: a lovely idea, passed on with confidence, that turns out to be wrong. And when a favourite doesn't survive a closer look, you let it go without a fuss. It still costs you something. Nobody sees that part.

Here's what people get from you, though few ever tell you: you take their ideas as seriously as your own. Someone floats a half-thought at the table, and you don't nod and move on. You follow it further than they dared, and they leave feeling cleverer than when they arrived. Being thought about that hard is rare. It's respect, and it matters more than you know.

## yours

1. There's a famous answer to who invented gin, repeated so widely that drinks historians despair of ever shifting it: a chemist in Holland called Sylvius. It goes back to 1824, when Samuel Morewood wrote that a German traveller, Engelbert Kämpfer, had credited the spirit's discovery to "Professor Sylvius of Leiden". Morewood was nobody's fool. He's been called the founder of the field, and his history of distilling was the first full one. He was probably right, too, that the spirit Kämpfer meant was genever, the Dutch spirit gin grew out of.

2. Maybe Sylvius did make a fine one. Kämpfer seems to have meant just that: Sylvius's own version of a spirit the Dutch were already making. Morewood read it as the invention of the spirit itself, and most of the writers who came after him took his word without question. Historians have only recently begun to correct it. Genever goes back at least to the late 1400s, more than a century before Sylvius was born, to makers whose names are mostly lost.

3. I like to think the answer lasted because it was such a good one: a name, a title, a city. It's exactly the kind of answer you'd love. And you're also the one who'd go back to the traveller's words late at night, and find the claim smaller than it looked, and the spirit under it older and bigger. I see both of those people in you: the one who loves the neat answer, and the one who goes back anyway.

4. So the cocktail I've made for you is built on the older spirit. It's that classic of classics, the Old-Fashioned, made the way people were asking for one by the 1880s, when Holland gin and brandy were the favourite spirits for a Cocktail: a little sugar, Angostura, lemon peel, ice. The spirit is oude genever, the old style (the word names the style, not years in a barrel): rich, malty, closer to a whisky than to the gin you know, which took its short name from it. Under it, poured into the glass first, is a little French grape brandy, a nod to the wine Dutch distillers made their spirit from before they turned to grain. Once it's stirred you can't find the layers. The older one is in every sip.

5. So keep loving the neat answers. Don't change that. Just, next time one delights you, don't save the going back for midnight and your own chair. Go back with the friend who brought it: "I love that. Want to see what's underneath?" Most of the time, what the two of you find is older and bigger than the story you started with, and it's theirs to keep too. Going back stops being the lonely part, and becomes what the evening was for.

## Rulings and choices
- **Coolhaes (A10), Hester's call to me:** dossier only. A second Leiden professor in guest text would make the guest read him as "the real answer", which is Morewood's step again (C3), and it would push the history over half. "Names we mostly don't have" stays true without him.
- **Hester's two gifts:** "gin" as a shortened name is used twice: in the epigraph, read cold, and once in y4. Oude ≠ aged is used as a plain-words gloss only, never as a second easy-answer story (one wrong-label story per glass).
- **Hester's three shorthand fixes:** all applied. "A chemist in Holland" in my voice; Leiden only inside Morewood's quoted words; no "never said", no "made it up"; never "the same spirit".
