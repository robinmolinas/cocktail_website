| story | In 1824 Samuel Morewood wrote that the traveller Engelbert Kämpfer had credited the spirit's discovery to "Professor Sylvius of Leiden". Through that essay the attribution entered mainstream drinks history. (A1; F2-F3; Oxford SYLVIUS pdf 1968) | The neat answer arrived complete: a name, a title, a city. | grant | Oxford calls Morewood the founder of the field and his essay pioneering; it held the first comprehensive history of distilling, and some of its sections haven't been entirely replaced since. (A2; F2, F4, F7; pdf 1968, SPIRITS WRITING pdf 1884) | The answer came from the most careful person in the room, who got the hard part right. Grant him before anything else (Wren b). | lineage | Genever goes back at least to the late 1400s. | ingredient | That early spirit was distilled from wine; by 1588 grain had replaced grapes. Today's genever is built on malt wine, a grain spirit distilled at least three times. (A6; F12, F17; pdf 570, 873-874) | The glass holds the grain descendant, not the 1495 recipe: never "the same spirit" or "the original" (Tomás's fence). | Explorer: the older road |
| name | After a Dutch king took the English throne in 1688, English distillers copied genever without its grain or skill, made up the flavour with more botanicals and sugar, and shortened the name too: "geneva", from jenever, became "gin". (A7; F13; GENEVER pdf 873, GIN pdf 890) | The short, familiar word sits on top of the older spirit; the guest's glass holds the elder. Not "stripped" (*Half a Rim*). | Wren r1: the older, bigger thing under the neat name |
| ingredient | Oude ("old") genever must hold at least 15% malt wine, at most 20 g sugar a litre, at least 35% ABV; no genever has to be aged, whatever it's called. After 1945 the lighter jonge style almost killed oude off; the cocktail revival since the 1990s brought it back. (A8; F15-F16; pdf 874-875) | Even "old" is an easy answer: it means the older style, not years in a barrel. Never "aged" (G6). Tomás's to use or not. | fear: the easy answer |
| lineage | Holland gin and brandy were by far the most popular spirits for the early Cocktail (the page sits beside an 1833 recipe). By the 1880s drinkers were asking for cocktails the "old-fashioned" way, with spirit, lump sugar and ice and nothing added (the 1882 Chicago report names whisky). In 1895 the New York bartender George Kappeler printed it as sugar, two dashes of Angostura, ice, lemon peel and spirit, with a Holland gin version among others. Dutch gin began to disappear from American bars with the Dry Martini in the 1890s. (A9; F14, F18; *Imbibe!* pdf 68, 175, 185-187; GENEVER pdf 874; v2: the early-Cocktail popularity and the 1880s requests are two eras, never one sentence) | The plain build is how this spirit was drunk before it went out of fashion. | drink: the build isn't a bridge |
