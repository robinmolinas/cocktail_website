## whoYouAre

You catch it before anyone has a word for it: a sound, a look, an idea a few people keep circling. And you're the one who gives it a shape, a name, a place, a first version people can point at and join.

People see the rebel. They don't see the plan, and you prefer it that way, because wanting to steer sounds like the very thing you were all against. So you steer quietly. Catching on was never the worry. What it turns into afterwards is: run by people who never got it, smoothed out for everyone, your hands nowhere near it.

And here's the good of it: most new things die young, talked out of it or copied badly. The ones you start tend to grow up, because someone was steering while they were fragile. That's rarer than you'd think.

## yours

1. In 1965, when factory beer ruled America, Fritz Maytag, from the washing-machine family, bought a failing San Francisco brewery called Anchor. He liked its beer, though he admitted it wasn't good: it had gone sort of sour. He brought in a microscope and, in his words, spent his whole nest egg on the brewery. It took ten years to make money.

2. It caught on, and then it caught on everywhere: a San Francisco paper later called his beers the first drops of a tsunami. Others opened the breweries that followed. His own, he kept small. And in 1993, in a corner of the brewery few people knew about, he set up stills and made rye whisky. The Oxford drinks companion calls it an early, "perhaps too early", shot across the bows of American whisky.

3. When he sold, in 2010, the man who'd steered it for forty-five years said he'd do whatever the new owner said, to help them do well. Thirteen years later, under other owners, the brewery went quiet. A new owner has promised it back, though so far it hasn't poured a drop. That's your fear, come true long after he'd let go. But look at what didn't stop: the small breweries everywhere, and a rye revival the same book says began with his. I like to think that's why he could offer to take orders. He'd never needed the whole wave. Only to start it, and keep one part small enough to steer himself.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a rye Sidecar, after the renowned classic of brandy, orange liqueur and lemon, shaken hard over ice, then strained into a chilled stemmed coupe. The rye is Old Potrero, the name his first rye was sold under, though today's bottles are aged longer than that first one, and there's more of it than anything else. In place of sugar syrup goes a little quince paste, loosened with hot water. Quince is a fruit hardly anyone eats raw, so hard and mouth-puckering it takes real work, and then it's wonderful, perfumed and deep. I chose it as a nod to a sour beer he took on, and put right.

5. So let me steer, just once. When something you started takes off, don't try to keep your hands on all of it. Choose the one part you'll steer, and steer it properly. Let the rest run in other people's hands. That isn't it getting away from you. It's how you'll know it was real. And it leaves you free for the next thing nobody has a name for yet.

## Rulings (round 3)
- **Quince: kept.** It's a fruit almost nobody can eat raw, made good by someone who takes the work on, which is the sour beer and the microscope. Taming would mean smoothing something out for everyone, and quince paste isn't that. Fence: never "soften", "tame", "smooth" or "early" about the quince. The tie is to the sour beer, in one clause. The rye isn't given a nod of its own.
- **Keeping it small: kept, one clause** ("His own, he kept small"), as N3 asks. The link to the distillery and the "one part small enough to steer" sit under "I like to think" (y3). It's never a moral about choosing not to grow (*Kept*).
- **The sale line: kept, paraphrased** ("he'd do whatever the new owner said, to help them do well"). The reason for selling is never given.
- **"Wheel" struck everywhere:** *Who's In?* (ruler-jester) owns "hand on the wheel" and "you won't lose the wheel".
