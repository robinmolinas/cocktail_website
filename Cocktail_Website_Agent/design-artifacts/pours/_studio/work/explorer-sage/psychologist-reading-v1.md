## whoYouAre

Years ago, someone gave you an answer that was perfectly true and told you nothing. You nodded. Then you took the question home, and it has come along ever since: in the queue at the post office, on the walk back from the station, somewhere at the back of a perfectly ordinary week. Every so often another piece falls into place. Nobody around you would guess that one of your questions is older than some of your friendships.

You've stopped mentioning it. People expect a question to be settled or dropped by now, and "still?" is a hard look to get. Some take you for a know-it-all. It's nearly the opposite: you can't rest inside something you aren't allowed to understand. What frightens you is being stuck for good with an answer you can't get behind, or one day settling for one yourself. And once you do understand, you don't keep it. You lay all of it out, plainly, so that nobody else has to take it on trust. Things everyone else had shrugged at get seen properly, and anyone can use them. People get the whole answer from you, free, and every year it cost you was worth it.

## yours

1. In 1979, at twenty-one, Jeff Berry started going to tiki bars on his own. At the Tiki-Ti on Sunset Boulevard he asked the old barman, Ray Buhen, what was in his drink. Whatever the drink, Ray gave the same reply: "Rum and fruit juice." Berry heard it in bar after bar. Ray wasn't being difficult. He had once worked for Donn Beach, whose Hollywood bar created the Zombie, and Beach had told his staff never to reveal a recipe, in case rival bars copied it. So by the time Berry started asking, many of those recipes, the Zombie's among them, were a mystery to anyone outside.
2. Berry had a day job in films. His off-hours went on the question. He read microfilm in libraries, tracked down retired barmen, and their children when the barmen were gone, and learned to read recipes written in code. In 1998 he put it in print that the Zombie's recipe was still unknown. Nailing it down took ten years and three books, he said. He found an early recipe in a self-published cookbook from 1950, and in a former headwaiter's notebook, what he calls the original. He printed it, every part named. Two hugely successful bars, in San Francisco and Chicago, owed him a debt, and the Chicago one is named after a lost drink whose recipe he uncovered.
3. I like to think the answer that kept him going wasn't a no. He could have lived with a no. It was "rum and fruit juice": polite, true, and empty. You've been handed that answer too, more than once. A shrug doesn't end a question for you. It starts one. And his narrowed the way yours do, from every drink on the menu to the one nobody could explain, and then he stayed with it for years, beside an ordinary job, until he could write the whole thing down.
4. The cocktail I've made for you is the Zombie, the first famous tiki drink and still one of the great ones. Every part Berry printed is in it, and your recipe names each one. The old menus hid two of them, grapefruit and cinnamon, under a single name, Don's Mix. Here they're written out. The balance is mine: a little more lime and sweetness, so it's brighter and fresher. And I make the falernum myself, a lime and clove syrup with rum in it, as a nod to all the years he spent asking what was in things: with a bought bottle, you'd have to take the maker's word for it. It's shaken, then poured into a tall Zombie glass full of crushed ice, and topped with mint. It's a big drink and a strong one, made to be taken slowly.
5. One more thing from Berry's story. On his own, he got "rum and fruit juice". When he went in with Ted Haigh, a loud, sociable friend whom one writer called Berry's opposite, he left with a recipe. "The first Don the Beachcomber recipes I got were because of Ted," Berry said. So take the question you've carried longest, and ask it out loud to the person you know who's least like you, the one who'd ask a stranger anything. Ask before you're sure it's a good question. You may get there years sooner, and on the day it all falls into place, you won't be the only one who's glad.

## Resonance

- **As the guest (a Genius).** The tagline invites: it's the shape of my oldest questions, and it ends on what I give, not what I lack. The epigraph, read cold, is a joke I get at once and want explained. "Perfectly true and told you nothing" is the line I'd quote back. "Older than some of your friendships" and the "still?" look are the things I'd never say. The fear is mine (stuck for good with an answer I can't get behind), and the gift is said out loud: I lay it all out so nobody has to take it on trust. I see myself in the story twice: in the polite, empty answer, and in the question narrowing from every drink to one. The drink ties back in words: the falernum made at home is a nod to his years of asking. y5 gives me something, an answer sooner and someone to share it with, and asks for the one thing I don't do: ask before I'm sure.
- **As a sceptic.** Barnum watch: the opening scene needs its "true and told you nothing" to hold it to this person. Without that it's anyone with a curiosity. Sibling swaps: *The First Guess* is held off with no "nobody's sure", no label, no "because I said so", and the Genius wants the question closed, not kept open for fun. *Serviceable*: no "that's just how it is", no loving the chase, no hospital. *For Good*: no irritation, no "stays with you", no "hearing yourself". *In a Minute*: time is background, no stopping. *The Long Answer*: no bogus recipes, no grenadine. *Left Standing*: no "take someone", no "alone" in y5. *Instead* and *Beside the First*: no "keep asking", no "tell someone". *Worth the Trip*: no Scialom, no "as he tells it". Fences: day job one clause, no Home Alone; code one clause; no "secret" as romance; no villain (Ray "wasn't being difficult"); no "brilliant"; no medicine; Haigh never gets him the Zombie, and no Corpse Reviver. "What he calls the original", the binder "an early recipe", no order between the two finds; "ten years and three books… he said". Never "his recipe": "every part Berry printed… the balance is mine". The guest is never in a bar or holding the drink; y5 points to life.
- **Closing line (for Tomás):** *Write everything that's in the falernum on its bottle. Then ask your oldest question out loud.* Tomás's seed with "jar" and "label" moved off (*Just Knew* owns "the jar"; "label" is *The First Guess*'s word), and its second half carries y5.
