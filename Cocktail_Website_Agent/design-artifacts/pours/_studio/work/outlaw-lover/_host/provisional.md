# Pour — Rent-Free · The Fugitive (outlaw-lover)

## Cocktail

- **name:** Rent-Free
- **tagline:** You love like it's a getaway. Even where you stop is a hideout.
- **glassware:** a tall stemmed wine glass, filled with ice
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 135 ml | dry California white wine (crisp and unoaked, a dry Riesling style), with a bay leaf left in the bottle overnight | he tasted one sold as "Hock" in a Napa cellar; kept cold in the dark, the way they kept theirs in the tunnel |
| 1 | dried bay leaf (kitchen bay) | goes into the wine bottle, and stays there |
| 45 ml | soda water, cold | |

**method**
1. The evening before, push one dried bay leaf into a bottle of dry California white, put the cork back in, and stand the bottle in the coldest, darkest corner of the fridge. The leaf stays in the bottle.
2. Put the soda water in the fridge too.
3. Fill a tall stemmed wine glass with ice.
4. Pour in 135 ml of the cold wine.
5. Top it with 45 ml of cold soda, poured gently so it keeps its bubbles, and give it one slow turn with a spoon.

**closingLine:** *Ice first, soda last. Then pour it where you mean to land, and unpack.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*Its wine spends a night in the cold, as two newlyweds kept theirs: in a mine.*

**whoYouAre**
A one-way ticket. A job left on a Friday. A family dinner where you finally told them, and the table went quiet. You've chosen people, places and whole lives like that: all at once, and usually against advice. And every time, choosing and getting free felt like the same thing. Whoever you fell for was also the way out.

You'd never tell anyone how much you'd like to land somewhere. It feels like giving in. And the road running out has never scared you. Being parked does: someone else deciding where you stop, and the life you ran towards slowly picking up the rules of the one you ran from.

And here's what that wildness gives whoever you choose: they don't have to guess. They know they were picked over everything else. Next to you, love looks like courage, and anyone watching starts asking what they'd leave for theirs. Not many people can give that.

**yours**

1. In 1879 a young Scottish writer, Robert Louis Stevenson, crossed the Atlantic and then the whole of America to reach Fanny, an American, in California. The next spring, they married. His parents had objected, because she was "an American and a stranger", and when they saw his mind was made up, they gave in completely.

2. After the wedding, they moved into an abandoned miners' bunkhouse high on a mountain above the Napa Valley, with Fanny's son and their dog, and lived there as squatters. What had tempted him was the idea of going "rent-free, into a ready-made house". He crowned the two of them "the King and Queen of Silverado", and his first job, to take possession, was cutting out the poison oak that had grown up through the floor. They kept their wine in the old mine tunnel, the one place on that hot hillside that stayed cool.

3. His book about that summer ends at night, with a single candle stuck in the neck of a bottle. From outside, he wrote, the old cracked house seemed to burst with light, and anyone out on the far hills would have stopped to watch. I like to think that's what stopping looks like for you: not being caught, but choosing the place, and making it as wild as the road. Years later, in Samoa, they bought land, set about clearing it and building a house, and called the place Vailima. Fanny's sister wrote that it was there "home" first came to mean something to them, and he was still bringing her each morning's writing to judge.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a wine highball: white wine and soda, one of the long, light drinks the highball is traced back to. In a Napa cellar he tasted a wine sold as "Hock", the English name for German white wine, so here it's a crisp, dry California white. The evening before, one bay leaf goes into the bottle, and the bottle goes into the coldest, darkest corner of the fridge, as a nod to the tunnel, and to a canyon he wrote smelled of sweet bay at sunrise and late at night. Then it's poured over ice into a tall stemmed wine glass, and topped with soda straight from the fridge.

5. So my proposal is this. When the pull to go comes again, ask yourself what you'd be leaving. If it's someone deciding for you, go. If it isn't, stop, on purpose, in a place of your choosing, and make that the adventure. Being caught means someone else chose where you'd end up. Stopping means you did.

Seasoning: the bay, steeped in the bottle. No sugar or acid is added: the wine brings both. |
| Balance | balance.py `highball`, wine only, by the room's choice (Wren r3: "the band, never the brandy"; the brandy would replace the thing chosen). **135 wine / 45 soda:** 9.4% ABV, 0.30 g sugar/100 ml, 0.49% acid. Strength is an **EDGE** under the 10% floor, which is built from the Whisky Highball (a spirit lengthened 1:3). That's by nature: a wine core at 12.5% can't reach a spirit highball's strength unless it's barely lengthened (150/30 is 10.4%, in range, but at that point it's hardly lengthened). Sugar and acid are in band. Sweeps: 130/50 is 9.0% (edge), 140/40 is 9.7% (edge); a wine at 11.5% gives 8.6% and one at 14% gives 10.5%; sugar 0 to 0.9 g stays in band. A high-acid wine (0.8%) puts acid at 0.600, the band's top, so the style asks for a crisp wine, not a sharp one. The room chose to own the edge rather than add a spirit. The ice's melt isn't modelled (`highball` takes 0%), so the real glass sits a little under these numbers; that cold wine and soda keep the melt small is my craft call. |
