🪞 **Wren:** Hester, Tomás, here's the Fugitive. My full card is in `_studio/work/outlaw-lover/psychologist-persona-card.md`.

**The one true thing.** For this person, choosing someone and getting free are the same act. Every sibling rebels *against* something. The Fugitive rebels *towards* someone, and brings them along. It's freedom for two, and it isn't a single jump: it's a life, with no way back.

**What they hide.** How much they'd like to land somewhere. Said out loud it sounds like giving in, so they don't say it. Under that is a quieter doubt: whether they know how to love on a night when nobody is against it.

**What they fear.** Someone else deciding where they stop. Being caught here isn't the police. It's the life they ran towards slowly taking on the rules of the life they ran from, with the car parked for them. That's the Outlaw's powerlessness meeting the Lover's fear of love going cold.

**What they give.** The people they love always know they were chosen over everything else. Around them, love looks like courage, and other people remember they're allowed to choose their own life. Once in the reading we say that plainly, as Robin wants.

**My seed for the position.** Stopping isn't the same as being caught. Being caught is when someone else picks the place. Stopping is when you pick it. So stop somewhere of your own choosing, and make it as wild as the road was.

**My verdict on the plan's row (Stevenson and Fanny): accept, with corrections.** I read the primary texts myself.

- **"Steerage" is wrong.** Stevenson says so himself in *The Amateur Emigrant* (*Essays of Travel*, Gutenberg #627, "The Second Cabin"): "I was not, in truth, a steerage passenger." He went second cabin. Hester, please change the row.
- **Why they went up the mountain is medical.** Sanchez says it was for "freedom from the fog which we dreaded for the sick man". So the reading gives no reason for the mountain.
- **The parents.** Sanchez's account holds: they objected to "an American and a stranger", and once his mind was "irrevocably made up" they "yielded without reserve", with an allowance and a welcome. One clause in the middle, never the ending.
- **Two things never go in.** First, the Gosse letter: "the woman whom I have loved for three years and a half" dates the love to her first marriage. Second, the whole Kelmar chapter of *Silverado*: its portrait of the storekeeper is antisemitic.
- **The stopping is on the page, and it's very Fugitive.** They squatted "rent-free, into a ready-made house", and the first job "by which we took possession" was cutting out poison oak. He called them "the King and Queen of Silverado", and the getaway was four: him, Fanny, her son Sam and the dog Chuchu. They kept their wine "in the tunnel that once rang with picks". At night there was one candle "in the neck of a pint bottle", "and yet the old cracked house seemed literally bursting with the light".
- **This is the line that lands for me:** from across the valley, a stopped life looked like a fire.
- **Stevenson's own epigraph to the book** is Cicero (*De Officiis* I.xx): to obey no one, to use their liberty, "whose mark is to live as you please". He put it over people living quietly in the country, so his own book frames stopping as freedom. That's our position, in his hand. Hester: we need a sourced English translation, or we use ours and signpost it.
- **Past the page:** the plan says they married on 19 May 1880. The father's help was "always amply and ungrudgingly given" (Colvin). If the reading claims the love lasted, we need a fetched page for "together until he died". His illness and his death are never named.

**For Tomás, for round 2:** the tunnel kept their wine cold in the dark, and the candle stood in a bottle. Those are story beats, not a spec, and the Disrupter's Napa red stays far from this glass.
