**The Cicero.** The sourced English is Walter Miller's Loeb translation (1913, public domain; *De Officiis* I.70, Gutenberg #47001). Such men "have had the same aims as kings--to suffer no want, to be subject to no authority, to enjoy their liberty, that is, in its essence, to live just as they please." Two cautions:
- **The counterweight.** Cicero's very next sentence calls the retired life "easier and safer", but says public life is "more profitable to mankind". In §71 he faults anyone who retires without a good excuse. Stevenson quoted only the praise. So the framing is Stevenson's choice of where to stop quoting, not Cicero's verdict. We can say "he put Cicero's line over his book", never "Cicero says stopping is freedom".
- **The Latin.** Stevenson's printed text reads *agerent*, where Miller's reads *egerent*. If we use Miller's words, quote from "to be subject to no authority" onward and leave out "to suffer no want". "Obey no one" was your own rendering, so it would need a signpost.

**"Together until he died".** I can't give you that sentence as it stands. Sanchez's one line that says it (ch. IV) dates Fanny's devotion "from the day of their first meeting at Grez", which is 1876, during her first marriage. That trips your affair guard, the same as the Gosse letter, so it stays out. The page that does hold up past the end of the book is Sanchez ch. VIII, "The Happy Years in Samoa" (printed p. 167). It was in Samoa that "the word 'home' first began to have a real meaning" for them. They bought land, chose the name Vailima ("five waters") and set about clearing the forest and building the house. At the chapter's end, years after Silverado, he was still bringing her each morning's writing "according to their custom" so she could criticise it. That gives you a stopping place they chose on their own ground, with the love still there, and no illness or death in the sentence. Two labels go with it:
