## whoYouAre

There's someone in your life that everyone has quietly made up their mind about. You haven't. You noticed something once: the evening they stayed behind to fix a thing nobody would ever have checked. Since then you've talked about them as if their turn is coming. Usually it comes. And when people ask how you knew, you can't really say.

That's the part nobody hears. Your reasons are so small that, said out loud, they'd sound like nothing, so you hand over the conclusion and keep the reasons. It's also why you can seem a little mysterious. Underneath sits a worry you seldom admit: that what you saw in someone was only what you hoped to see, and they went ahead because you said so.

And here's your gift to whoever is near you. In your eyes, nobody is ever finished. People grow into the place you kept for them, and plenty never find out who kept it. Most people only get that kind of faith from someone who loves them. You hand it out on far less.

## yours

1. Grappa is the spirit Italy distils from the wine press's leftovers, once the juice has run off. Italians have made it out of thrift since the 1700s or earlier. By tradition the pressed grapes, every kind together, were buried until the wine was made, and only then went into the still. Outside Italy, people laughed at it for years as rough "moonshine" made from the "dregs".

2. In 1973, Giannola and Benito Nonino, of Friuli in Italy's north-east, did something small and new. They distilled the leftovers of one grape, the picolit, on their own, and used them only fresh and lightly pressed: the first grappa from a single grape. In Giannola's words, she and Benito "selected the best Friulian winemakers" to get it. One early doubter, she says, came round to be one of their strongest supporters. And six years before, the family says, a first single-grape bottling had their working printed on its label: the year, how much they'd distilled, how many litres came out, the bottle's number.

3. Nothing was rescued. Grappa had always been made. They only looked at it differently, one grape at a time, and I like to think Giannola could already smell what was in the picolit that nobody had kept apart long enough to find. Abroad, the laughing went on for nearly twenty more years. And not everything went the way the new grappa promised: most of it is still drunk at home in Italy, and the rough old style has come back. I recognise you in that. Not right about everything, but right about the part that mattered, years before most people could see it.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Martini, that renowned stirred classic, with Nonino's grappa from a single grape in place of the gin. It's stirred with a pale, sweet French vermouth that has spent an hour with a fresh fig leaf. A fig tree is grown for its fruit, not its leaves, and yet the leaves are wonderfully fragrant. I chose it as a nod to the Noninos looking twice at what everyone else used without a second thought. There's nothing on top: the grape reaches you first.

5. One thing to try, then. When you next see where someone's heading, show them what you saw, not just where you think they'll end up, the way the Noninos printed their working on the bottle: the evening they stayed behind, the small thing they fixed. Then they aren't carrying your hope. They're holding your reasons, and they can check them for themselves. If you're right, they'll know why. If you're wrong, nobody was steered by a guess. And you won't be the only one who can see it anymore.
