## whoYouAre

The medal's still round your neck, or the last exam's handed in, or the record you hunted for two years is finally on the shelf. Everyone's pleased for you. And by that evening you're quietly reading up on the next one.

People call it restless. It isn't, quite. You've never claimed to be fearless, and the climb was never what stopped you. What you dread is finished: a comfortable life that asks nothing of you, a map with nothing left on it. Going after something far away and hard to get is how you find out what you're made of, and once you know, you need something new to find out about. And you never return empty-handed. You turn up with things nobody near you could have got, and you make far away look like somewhere people can actually go. Everyone you know lives a bigger life because you went. No prize on your shelf is worth as much.

## yours

1. Eric Seed was a student in Austria when he first came across the pine liqueur of the Alps, and he thought it would sell in Colorado's ski towns. One writer calls him perhaps the most mild-mannered person in the cocktail business, soft-spoken among loud characters, and then adds that there was "nothing safe or boring about his business". Seed went back to Austria to find a pine liqueur he could bring over, and returned with Zirbenz, which the Hofer family distillery has made since 1797.

2. He could afford one pallet: sixty cases. None of the big distributors were interested, so a one-man wine business carried it, sold as a drink for after skiing. It struggled, though in some places it did well, and a Denver bartender took it on. Years later Seed admitted the conifer "is hardly an essential bar ingredient". Then, with that first bottle behind him, he walked into the best liquor store in Aspen and asked what they'd like to carry. That became his way of working. He asked bartenders on both coasts what he should bring over, and went and found it: one asked for Batavia arrack, a rum-like spirit from Indonesia. For a decade, by one writer's account, a visit from Seed to a cocktail bar was an occasion, because nobody knew what rare bottle he'd pull out of his bag.

3. I like to think the pine was never the whole point. It took him somewhere, it showed him he could, and then came the question you know well: what now? His way out was to stop waiting for something new to find him, and to ask other people what they couldn't get. That way, the map never runs out. You've had that flat evening more times than you'd admit, and his way out is one you can borrow.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a toddy, the classic warm winter drink, for the end of a cold day. The pine goes in first, Zirbenz or any Alpine pine liqueur, three parts to one of aged rum, a pairing its importer suggests for toddies. Then a teaspoon of allspice dram, one of the bottles Seed became known for pulling out of that bag. I added it for the find that comes next, so the first prize and the one after share a glass. A little lemon, a touch of brown sugar syrup, hot water, all in a glass mug warmed first, and last, lemon zest twisted above the steam, for its scent. It's a warm reddish amber, and it smells of pine, with spice underneath.

5. So the next evening you arrive and it goes flat, don't go looking for a bigger mountain straight away. Ask a friend what they've stopped hoping to find: the book that's out of print, the song they half remember, the place they always meant to see. Then go and get it. You'll never be short of somewhere to go, and a friend gets the thing they'd given up on. I hope your list stays long.
