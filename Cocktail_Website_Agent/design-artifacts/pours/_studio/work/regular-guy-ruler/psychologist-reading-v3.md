## whoYouAre

Your neighbour's parking fine came back cancelled, because you knew which sentence the appeal needed. A friend got their money back from the airline in one call, with you beside them saying what to ask for. You know which form actually gets read, and that you're allowed to ask for a name. All of it matters on the day a letter arrives and someone you love goes pale.

You'd rather not admit that you like knowing. It's a kind of power, though you wouldn't use the word. And when someone offers you a seat where things get decided, you come up with a reason to say no. Knowing this much already puts you near the side of the counter that says no. One step further, and you're afraid your own people would start to see you as one of them.

So I'll name it. Because of you, people who'd have paid full price, signed the wrong thing or taken no for an answer didn't. You put what you know on the side of the people nobody told. That counts for a great deal more than you let it.

## yours

In the spring of 2014, limes in America suddenly got scarce and expensive. A drinks magazine blamed a bacterial disease and bad weather, and asked bartenders for advice. One of them was Bobby Heugel, who owned a bar called Anvil, in Houston.

He gave away a trick of the trade. Limes are packed by size, he explained, each case holding fruit that matches. The odd sizes left over, some as big as your fist, go into what the trade calls a packer's case, and by his account it sells for 30 to 40% less. Almost every distributor has them, he said, though maybe not so many in Houston any more, because he'd been spreading the word. Then he said one of the biggest troubles with that year's limes was their quality, and asked why bars didn't recommend a better drink made with lemon, and tell the customer why.

He wasn't a newcomer. He owned the bar, and he gave the saving away anyway: to his own city first, by his account, then to anyone who read the magazine. Of where he started, he once said there'd been nobody at all he could talk to about drinks. I like to think he remembered that. I think you would. You know what it's like to be the one nobody told, and you've made sure your own people never are.

The cocktail I've made for you is a Collins, among the most famous mixed drinks, built here on vodka, fresh lime, sugar syrup and soda water. Plenty of bartenders look down on vodka. One bar book's authors admit they were among the worst, and call the sneering inhospitable: lots of people love it. I chose it partly for that, because you'd always side with them, and because vodka leaves the lime tasting clean. The balance between lime and sugar is narrow, so both are measured, and the lime is never counted, as a nod to Heugel's trick: measured out, it makes no difference what size the lime was. The soda waits in the glass, and the shaken drink lands on it and mixes itself. It's tall, cold and bright, sweet and tart at once. And if the lemons are better that day, use lemon, and tell whoever's drinking why.

Say yes when you're next offered a seat where things get decided. You're afraid it would put you behind that counter, on the side that says no. It needn't, as long as you keep telling your people everything you know. From there you'll have more to share, and more people to share it with.
