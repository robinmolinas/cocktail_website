# Pour — Word Gets Round · The People's Champion (regular-guy-ruler)

## Cocktail

- **name:** Word Gets Round
- **tagline:** Nobody near you pays extra for not knowing.
- **glassware:** highball glass (about 350 ml), filled with crushed ice
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 50 ml | bourbon | any straight bourbon, ideally about 45% |
| 15 ml | fresh lime juice | measured, not counted: any size of lime will do, so buy the odd-sized ones if your shop sells them cheaper. Or use lemon, if the lemons taste better that day |
| 100 ml | soda water | cold, added last |

**method**
1. Fill the glass to the top with crushed ice. A clean tea towel and a rolling pin will crush ordinary cubes.
2. Squeeze a lime and a lemon. Taste a drop of each juice. Use whichever tastes better today, and if anyone asks, tell them why.
3. Measure 15 ml of that juice into the glass. Don't go by halves or wedges: measured out, it makes no difference what size the lime was.
4. Add the bourbon, then top up with the soda water. Stir once, gently, from the bottom.
5. No sugar, and nothing on top. Drink it while the ice is still crisp.

**closingLine:** *Juice the odd-sized limes. Then take the chair they keep offering you.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*Limes are packed by size, a bartender said. Odd ones go cheap. Measures don't mind.*

**whoYouAre**
Your neighbour's parking fine came back cancelled, because you knew which sentence the appeal needed. A friend got their money back from the airline in one call, with you beside them saying what to ask for. You know which form actually gets read, and that you're allowed to ask for a name. All of it matters on the day a letter arrives and someone you love goes pale.

You'd rather not admit that you like knowing. It's a kind of power, though you wouldn't use the word. And when someone offers you a seat where things get decided, you come up with a reason to say no. Knowing this much already puts you near the side of the counter that says no. One step further, and you're afraid your own people would start to see you as one of them.

So I'll name it. Because of you, people who'd have paid full price, signed the wrong thing or taken no for an answer didn't. You put what you know on the side of the people nobody told. That counts for a great deal more than you let it.

**yours**

1. In the spring of 2014, limes in America suddenly got scarce and expensive. A drinks magazine blamed a bacterial disease and bad weather, and asked bartenders for advice. One of them was Bobby Heugel, who owned a bar called Anvil, in Houston.

2. He gave away a trick of the trade. Limes are packed by size, he explained, each case holding fruit that matches. The odd sizes left over, some as big as your fist, go into what the trade calls a packer's case, and by his account it sells for 30 to 40% less. Almost every distributor has them, he said, though maybe not so many in Houston any more, because he'd been spreading the word. Then he said one of the biggest troubles with that year's limes was their quality, and asked why bars didn't recommend a better drink made with lemon, and tell the customer why.

3. He wasn't a newcomer. He owned the bar, and he gave the saving away anyway: to his own city first, by his account, then to anyone who read the magazine. Of where he started, he once said there'd been nobody at all he could talk to about drinks. I like to think he remembered that. I think you would. You know what it's like to be the one nobody told, and you've made sure your own people never are.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Rickey, a classic that spread across America in the 1890s and is now the official cocktail of Washington, DC: bourbon, fresh lime and soda water. It's built over a glassful of crushed ice. It's long, sharp and very cold, with the bourbon's warmth underneath. A classic Rickey has no sugar, so the lime has nowhere to hide, and you taste exactly how good it is. One bar book says the secret is the amount of lime, and that too many bartenders stop at a single squeeze. So the lime goes in measured, not counted, as a nod to Heugel's trick: measured out, it makes no difference what size the lime was. And if the lemons are better that day, use lemon, and tell whoever's drinking why.

5. You're afraid the seat would put you behind that counter. I don't think it would. What keeps you with your people was never where you sat. It's that you tell them everything you know. Next time you're offered a seat where things get decided, say yes. From there you'll have more to share, and more people to share it with, and I hope you enjoy every bit of it.
