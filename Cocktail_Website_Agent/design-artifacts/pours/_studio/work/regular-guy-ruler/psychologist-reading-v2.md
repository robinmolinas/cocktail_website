## whoYouAre

Your neighbour's parking fine came back cancelled, because you knew which sentence the appeal needed. A friend got their money back from the airline in one call, with you beside them saying what to ask for. You know which form actually gets read, and that you're allowed to ask for a name. All of it matters on the day a letter arrives and someone you love goes pale.

You'd rather not admit that you like knowing. It's a kind of power, though you wouldn't use the word. And when someone offers you a seat where things get decided, you come up with a reason to say no. Knowing this much already puts you near the side of the counter that says no. One step further, and you're afraid your own people would start to see you as one of them.

So I'll name it. Because of you, people who'd have paid full price, signed the wrong thing or taken no for an answer didn't. You put what you know on the side of the people nobody told. That counts for a great deal more than you let it.

## yours

In the spring of 2014, limes in America suddenly got scarce and expensive. A drinks magazine blamed a bacterial disease and bad weather, and asked bartenders for advice. One of them was Bobby Heugel, who owned a bar called Anvil, in Houston.

He gave away a trick of the trade. Limes are packed by size, he explained, each case holding fruit that matches. The odd sizes left over, some as big as your fist, go into what the trade calls a packer's case, and by his account it sells for 30 to 40% less. Almost every distributor has them, he said, though maybe not so many in Houston any more, because he'd been spreading the word. Then he said one of the biggest troubles with that year's limes was their quality, and asked why bars didn't recommend a better drink made with lemon, and tell the customer why.

He wasn't a newcomer. He owned the bar, and he gave the saving away anyway: to his own city first, by his account, then to anyone who read the magazine. Of where he started, he once said there'd been nobody at all he could talk to about drinks. I like to think he remembered that. I think you would. You know what it's like to be the one nobody told, and you've made sure your own people never are.

The cocktail I've made for you is a Rickey, a classic that spread across America in the 1890s and is now the official cocktail of Washington, DC: bourbon, fresh lime and soda water. It's built over a glassful of crushed ice. It's long, sharp and very cold, with the bourbon's warmth underneath. A classic Rickey has no sugar, so the lime has nowhere to hide, and you taste exactly how good it is. One bar book says the secret is the amount of lime, and that too many bartenders stop at a single squeeze. So the lime goes in measured, not counted, as a nod to Heugel's trick: measured out, it makes no difference what size the lime was. And if the lemons are better that day, use lemon, and tell whoever's drinking why.

You're afraid the seat would put you behind that counter. I don't think it would. What keeps you with your people was never where you sat. It's that you tell them everything you know. Next time you're offered a seat where things get decided, say yes. From there you'll have more to share, and more people to share it with, and I hope you enjoy every bit of it.

## Resonance

- **The "this is me" line:** *"You put what you know on the side of the people nobody told."* It names the gift without praising cleverness, and it's the line I'd hand Robin.
- **As the guest:** three everyday scenes anyone can check against their own life (the appeal, the airline call, the form that gets read). Then the hidden pair: liking the power, and refusing the seat for fear of losing your own people. The tagline intrigues and the reading earns it. The position is in the guest's interest and ends on what they gain.
- **As a sceptic:** "you like knowing" alone would fit many people. Here it's tied to the refused seat, which only this Ruler-shaped Everyman carries. Swap-tested against the Scoundrel (charm, being let off), the Legend (unheard, then calm in a crisis), the Firefighter (practice hours), the Mechanic (fixing) and the Hidden Talent (a hidden skill): none of their lines would fit here.
- **The story mirrors the person:** an owner, already in the seat, who gave the trade's saving away and said, with a "maybe", that it might now be harder to find in his own city. And someone who, where he started, had nobody to talk to.
- **The drink proves it:** nothing in the glass charms you. It's know-how with no show: the measure that makes the size of the fruit not matter, and the guest's own call between lime and lemon. On Tomás's plainness question: not too plain for this person. Robin's "interesting" is met by the choice the guest makes and the trick they can pass on, and y4 now says what it tastes like (long, sharp, cold, the bourbon's warmth).
- **Verdict:** *ready*, pending Hester's audit v2.
