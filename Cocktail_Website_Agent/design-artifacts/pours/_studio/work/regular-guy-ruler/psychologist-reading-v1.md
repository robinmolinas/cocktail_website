## whoYouAre

Your neighbour's parking fine came back cancelled, because you knew which sentence the appeal needed. Your aunt got her money back from the airline in one call, with you beside her saying what to ask for. You know the day the market marks things down, which form actually gets read, and that you're allowed to ask for a name. None of it is glamorous. All of it matters, usually on the day a letter arrives and someone you love goes pale.

You'd rather not admit that you like knowing. It's a kind of power, though you wouldn't use the word. Underneath, there's something quieter. When someone's offered you a seat where things get decided, on the committee, behind the desk, at the head of the table, you've found a reason to say no. Knowing the system this well already puts you close to the side of the counter that says no. One step further, and you're afraid the people you did it all for would start to see you as one of them.

So I'll name it. Because of you, people who'd have paid full price, signed the wrong thing or taken no for an answer didn't. You put what you know on the side of the people nobody told. That counts for a great deal more than you let it.

## yours

In the spring of 2014, limes in America suddenly got scarce and expensive. A drinks magazine blamed a bacterial disease and bad weather, and asked bartenders how they were coping. One of them was Bobby Heugel, who owned a bar called Anvil, in Houston.

He gave away a trick of the trade. Limes are packed by size, he explained, each case holding fruit that matches. The odd sizes left over go into what the trade calls a packer's case, and by his account it sells for 30 to 40% less. Some of the limes are as big as your fist. Almost every distributor has them, he said, though maybe not so many in Houston any more, because he'd told everyone. Then he said the real trouble with that year's limes was their quality, and asked why bars didn't recommend a better drink made with lemon, and tell the customer why.

He wasn't a newcomer trying to get noticed. He owned the bar, and he gave the saving away anyway: to his own city first, then to anyone who read the magazine. Of his early days, he once said there'd been nobody at all he could talk to about drinks. I like to think he remembered that. I think you would. You know what it's like to be the one nobody told, and you've made sure your own people never are.

The cocktail I've made for you is a Rickey, a classic that spread across America in the 1890s and is now the official cocktail of Washington, DC: bourbon, fresh lime and soda water. I build it in a tall wine glass heaped with crushed ice. It's tall, sharp and very cold. A classic Rickey has no sugar, so the lime has nowhere to hide, and you taste exactly how good it is. One bar book says the secret is the amount of lime, and that too many bartenders stop at a single squeeze. So the lime goes in measured, not counted, as a nod to Heugel's trick: measured out, a fist-sized lime from the odd case does the job as well as a perfect one. And if the lemons are better that day, use lemon, and tell whoever's drinking why. The man the drink is named after took his with lemon anyway.

You're afraid the seat would put you behind that counter. I don't think it would. What keeps you with your people was never where you sat. It's that you tell them everything you know. Next time you're offered a seat where things get decided, say yes. From there you'll have more to pass on, and more people to pass it to, and I hope you enjoy every bit of it.

## Resonance

- **As the guest:** the scenes are ordinary and checkable (the appeal, the airline, the markdown day), and the hidden pair, enjoying the power and turning down the seat, is the "how did they know". The line I'd hand Robin: *"You put what you know on the side of the people nobody told."* The tagline intrigues without frightening.
- **As a sceptic:** "you like knowing" could be anyone's. It's saved by its turn into power and the refused seat, which only a Ruler-shaped person carries. The scenes stay off the Scoundrel's (no charm, no counter laughs), the Legend's (no crisis, no being unheard) and the Firefighter's (no practice hours).
- **Story:** the guest sees themselves in *"because he'd told everyone"* and in the owner who stayed on the drinkers' side. Point 5 is waived as story fact. Its opposite on the page (he already had the seat) carries the position.
- **Verdict:** *nearly*. It waits on Hester's audit and the lint, and on Tomás confirming the method lines in y4.
