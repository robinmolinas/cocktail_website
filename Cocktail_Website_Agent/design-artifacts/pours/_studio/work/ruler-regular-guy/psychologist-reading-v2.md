## whoYouAre

A family stops speaking over a seating plan. A team splits down the middle over who did the work. Two friends each want you to say the other one's wrong. Everyone waits to see which table you'll sit at. You go and sit with the people who expected you not to, and you have whatever they're having.

You don't do it with a speech. You've never won anyone round with an argument, and you don't try. You just turn up on their ground and mean it. From outside it can look like sitting on the fence. It isn't. You have a side, and you know your own mind. The hardest question you get doesn't come from the other side. It comes from your own: whose side are you on? What you fear is being made to choose, and the day a row stops being a row and becomes the way things are. Here's what all that is worth: because of you, nobody has to pick a table. Both halves end up in one room, and neither had to give itself up to get there.

## yours

1. Before the American Civil War, New Orleans was a brandy town. Its juleps were made with brandy, and that was how the city drank. Then the city changed. By 1885, a local bartender was telling a newspaper that brandy drinking there was over: whiskey had taken its place, in whiskey cocktails and in the new Manhattan.

2. By the 1930s, the Hotel Monteleone's head bartender was Walter Bergeron. He'd worked that bar before Prohibition and returned when it ended. The drink he created there became the hotel's signature. It's a Manhattan at heart, but where a Manhattan has whiskey, his has two spirits: American rye whiskey and French cognac. They go in at equal measures, level with the sweet vermouth, plus a dash each of two bitters and a touch of a herbal liqueur from France, Bénédictine. Its name is the old quarter's: Vieux Carré, "old square".

3. Why he put the two together, and who chose the name, we can't say. But I like to think it's the city's whole story in one glass: the brandy it drank first and the whiskey it turned to, together at last, neither poured a drop less than the other. That's what you do. When people split, you don't rule on which half was right. You bring both in, at the same weight, and you call it by a name everyone already shares.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is the Vieux Carré, a renowned New Orleans classic, with rye, cognac and sweet vermouth in three equal measures, none cut back for another. I didn't change either spirit. I steeped the vermouth overnight with green cardamom, as a nod to the ground the two sides share: the vermouth is the one part of the glass that belongs to neither, and cardamom goes with both the grain behind the rye and the grape behind the cognac. It's stirred, rich and deep, with the spice of the rye up front, the softer fruit of the cognac underneath, and the warmth of the cardamom running through both.

5. One thing, though. When you go over to the other side, you tend to leave a little of yourself at the door, so they feel safe. Your own side notices, and that's where the question comes from. Don't pour less of yourself. Go over as all of you. It was never your quietness they trusted. It's that you came as you are and stayed. And when your own side asks again, you'll have your answer: both, and still me.

**closing line (Tomás, draft v1):** *Put the cardamom in the vermouth, never in either spirit. And when you cross over, keep your own colours on.*

## Resonance (v2, against draft v1)
**This is me:** "The hardest question you get doesn't come from the other side. It comes from your own: whose side are you on?"

**As the guest.** The name stops me first: *One Table* is the thing I'd fight for at every family dinner. The tagline makes me smile because it's true: everyone recruits me, and I end up on both sides. whoYouAre ¶1 is three scenes I've stood in, and "you have whatever they're having" is my exact move. The question from my own side is the line nobody has ever said to me, and it's the one I carry. The story doesn't flatter me: nobody in it is a peacemaker. It's a city and a glass, and that suits me, because I'd squirm at a hero story. The drink feels made for me, not matched. Neither spirit is touched, and the change goes into the part that belongs to neither side, which is exactly where I put myself. y5 names the habit I'd deny, turning myself down to be welcome, and hands me an answer to the question I dread. The closing line puts it in my hands at the bar.
