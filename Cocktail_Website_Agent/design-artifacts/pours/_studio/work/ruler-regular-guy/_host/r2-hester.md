Before anyone writes it, though, four fences, because your summary already goes further than the pages do. They're on the new card, `_studio/fact-cards/vieux-carre-walter-bergeron.md`.

**C1, his side.** Oxford pdf 2102 gives Bergeron's job, his dates (1889–1947) and his return at Repeal. Nothing I read says which side he was on. So "the side that won pours the side that lost back in" can't be said of him. Tell it through the city's drinks: the brandy it drank, and the whiskey that "displaced it" (*Imbibe!* pdf 182).

**C2, the gap.** That switch happened between the Civil War and 1885. The drink came in the 1930s, about fifty years later. No page links the two, and no page says cognac was out of favour by then. The link is our reading. It gets signposted, and it's never a quarrel still going at the bar.

**C3, the name.** Oxford says only that the drink is "named for the French Quarter". It doesn't say who chose the name. So "he named it for the old square" is out. "It carries the old quarter's name" is fine.

**C4, three equal parts, not two.** Your "30 ml of rye with 30 ml of cognac" comes from Oxford's modern recipe. Arthur's 1937 recipe (archive.org reprint, OCR highlight) gives ⅓ jigger each of rye, cognac and *Italian vermouth*, with ½ tsp Bénédictine and one dash each of Peychaud's and Angostura. Tomás, that helps your "full strength": each one is at full measure, not two halves. But "the two spirits share the glass equally" would be false.

One thing for the dossier only. The hotel's own story, relayed by Seidman and by Pearce (2017), is that the drink was "an homage to the different ethnic groups of New Orleans". That's brand-tier, with no 1930s source behind it, and it lands right on your race trap. It stays out of guest text, but a guest who looks the drink up will find it. Arthur's line about Bergeron breaks off at "takes special pride…", so no motive is on record. Also: guidebooks say "1938", which can't be right if the recipe was printed in 1937, so we say "the 1930s".
