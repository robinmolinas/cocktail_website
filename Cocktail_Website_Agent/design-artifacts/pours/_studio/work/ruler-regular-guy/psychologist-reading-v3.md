## whoYouAre

A family stops speaking over a seating plan. A team splits down the middle over who did the work. Two friends each want you to say the other one's wrong. Everyone waits to see which table you'll sit at. You go and sit with the people who expected you not to, and you have whatever they're having.

You don't do it with a speech. You've never won anyone round with an argument, and you don't try. You just turn up on their ground and mean it. From outside it can look like sitting on the fence. It isn't. You have a side, and you know your own mind. The hardest question you get doesn't come from the other side. It comes from your own: whose side are you on? What you fear is being made to choose, and the day a row stops being a row and becomes the way things are. Here's what all that is worth: because of you, nobody has to pick a table. Both halves end up in one room, and neither had to give itself up to get there.

## yours

1. Before the American Civil War, New Orleans was a brandy town. Its juleps were made with brandy, and that was how the city drank. Then the city changed. By 1885, a bartender was telling a New Orleans newspaper that brandy drinking there was over, and that whiskey had taken its place. The city drank it in whiskey cocktails and in the new Manhattan.

2. By the 1930s, the Hotel Monteleone's head bartender was Walter Bergeron. He'd worked that bar before Prohibition and returned when it ended. The drink he created there became the hotel's signature. It's a Manhattan at heart, but where a Manhattan has whiskey, his has two spirits: rye whiskey and French cognac. They go in at equal measures, level with the sweet vermouth, plus two different bitters and a touch of a herbal liqueur from France, Bénédictine. Its name is the old quarter's: Vieux Carré, "old square".

3. He said he made it to honour the old quarter. Why he put those two spirits side by side, he didn't say. But I like to think it's the city's whole story in a single drink: the brandy it drank first and the whiskey it turned to, together in one glass, neither poured a drop less than the other. That's what you do. When people split, you don't rule on which half was right. You bring both in, at the same weight, and you call it by a name everyone already shares.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is the Vieux Carré, a renowned New Orleans classic, with the proportions of its first printed recipe: rye, cognac and sweet vermouth in three equal measures, none cut back for another. I didn't change either spirit. I steeped the vermouth overnight with green cardamom, as a nod to the ground the two sides share: the vermouth is the one part of the glass that belongs to neither, and cardamom goes with both the grain behind the rye and the grape behind the cognac. It's stirred, rich and deep, with the rye's spice and the cognac's softer fruit each as clear as the other, and the cardamom warm in the vermouth between them.

5. One thing, though. When you go over to the other side, you tend to leave a little of yourself at the door, so they feel safe. Your own side notices, and that's where the question comes from. Don't pour less of yourself. It was never your quietness they trusted. It's that you came as you are and stayed. And when your own side asks again, you'll have your answer: both, and still me.

**closing line (Tomás, r4):** *Put the cardamom in the vermouth, never in either spirit. Next time you cross over, bring all of yourself.*
