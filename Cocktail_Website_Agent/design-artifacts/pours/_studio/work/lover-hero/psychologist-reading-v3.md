## whoYouAre

Someone you love says it's a shame you can't make it. It's three hundred miles away, in the middle of the week. You don't argue. On the night, you're there, on time, in your good coat, and the whole room turns round. That's how you love. Not a card, not a call. You said you'd come, so you come, the whole way, with everyone watching. Anyone you love never has to wonder whether you'll be there, and everyone around them can see how loved they are. Nobody forgets being loved like that.

It was never the distance. It's the second before you see their face, when you don't know yet whether they're glad you came, or whether they'd have been fine without you. You'd never admit it, but somewhere along the way, being needed started to feel like the proof that you're loved. It isn't the only proof. Most of the time, it isn't even the best one.

## yours

1. In November 1889, a reporter called Nellie Bly set out from New Jersey to go round the world faster than the hero of Jules Verne's novel, who took eighty days. She had promised seventy-five. When her ship reached England, she heard that Verne and his wife had written to ask her to visit them at home in Amiens, in northern France. It was out of her way, and she'd assumed she would have to say no. It could be done, she was told, if she was willing to go without sleep for two nights. She asked only whether she'd miss any connections. If not, she said, "don't think about sleep or rest."

2. It nearly came to nothing. It all hung on a special mail train that night, and when they pulled into Amiens they were locked in their compartment and thought they'd be carried past. Then she stepped onto the platform, and the Vernes were standing there, waiting for her. What she wrote about that moment isn't the miles or the lost sleep: "I wondered if my face was travel-stained, and if my hair was tossed." A second later, she wrote, the welcome had made her forget all about it.

3. That's the second I mean. You'd make the whole journey without a thought and still feel it on the platform. And look at who was waiting. They had only asked her to stop and see them. I like to think they wanted nothing but her company. When it was time to go, they came out into the cold courtyard without hats, though everyone protested, and stood at the gate waving until she was out of sight, the wind blowing their white hair about. Nobody there was worried about anyone's hair.

4. Before she left, there was wine and biscuits on the table, and Verne, against his own rules, took a glass so they could all drink to her success. The glasses clinked, and he managed it in English, for her: "Good luck, Nellie Bly." Her book never says what the wine was, so the wine in the cocktail I've made for you is my choice: a young red Bordeaux, and it's most of the glass, because the toast was the wine. With it goes straight rye. In the 1880s, American bartenders floated red wine on whiskey sours, so rye and red wine already belonged together in her decade. Then a little dark sugar syrup, Angostura bitters, a splash of water, and the scent of orange peel. Ice never comes near it. It's made and served at room temperature, soft, deep and warming: a good red with a spine of rye. I made it warm for the fire Verne's wife lit with her own hands when they arrived, and for the two of them standing bareheaded in the cold courtyard to wave her off.

5. So go and see someone who's doing perfectly well without you. No occasion, nothing to fix, nobody to rescue: just because you'd like to see them. You'll feel that second at the door, the same as always. Let it come. When their face lights up, it won't be for anything you've done. That's what being wanted looks like, and you've had it all along. I hope there's someone on the platform every time.

## Resonance

Read twice: as the guest, on a phone, with no drink in hand, and as a sceptic who has read every Lover reading and the Hero-family neighbours (*Off Duty*, *Next One's Mine*, *Nothing to It*, *Anyone Would Have*).

- **The "this is me" line:** *"It was never the distance. It's the second before you see their face."* Bly's own sentence in y2 then proves it: the woman who gave up two nights' sleep without blinking worried, on the platform, about her hair.
- **Horoscope check (whoYouAre):** S1 "says it's a shame you can't make it" is specific. S2–S4 (three hundred miles, "You don't argue", there in your good coat, the room turns round) are specific: a scene only this person lives. S5–S6 "That's how you love. Not a card, not a call" is specific: it separates them from the Rough Diamond's deeds and the Geisha's getting ready. S7 "you come, the whole way, with everyone watching" is specific. S8–S9, the gift said plainly (they never have to wonder; everyone can see how loved they are), are specific. P2 S1–S2, the second at the door, is specific. P2 S3, needed felt like the proof, is specific to a Lover who's also a Hero. P2 S4–S5 is *generic on its own*, but it's the turn the paragraph exists for, and it stays short. One generic: passes.
- **Swap test.** Against the Geisha: she gets ready and hopes without asking. Here the guest goes, in public, and the fear is at the door, not in the empty room, so no swap. Against the Rough Diamond: unseen, wordless deeds. Here love is said and seen, so no swap. Against *Off Duty*: crisis, "already moving", the quiet weeks. Here y5 deliberately keeps the guest in the going role, and the reason is being wanted, not rest. Watch "nobody to rescue" next to *Off Duty*'s "nobody needs you": different words, a different act (go vs. be cared for). Against *Nothing to It*: scared of the going. Here "the miles have never frightened you" is the opposite. Against *Hoping You'd Come*: the Vernes waiting on a platform touches its ground, so the reading stays with the one arriving, and the waiting couple appear only as what she found.
- **Punch:** the tagline is strong, a little proverb with a turn. whoYouAre's first line, "Someone you love says it's a shame you can't make it", is a scene and hooks. y5's last line, "I hope there's someone on the platform every time", is a warm wish, and it's the weakest of the three. I'd keep it: it's earned by y2 and lands softly after the hard truth.
- **Family colour:** the plan gives the Lover family's red/pink to the Flirt, so this reading names the wine only as an ingredient ("red wine", once) and never dwells on its colour. That's why the epigraph is about the toast, not the layer. (Host: the image will show it; that's the drink as it is.)
- **Registry fixes made before handing over:** "gives the people you love" (*Any Day*), "where everyone can see" (*Fair Measure*), "in front of everyone" (*Nothing to It*), "a rare thing to give" (*In Your Own Hand*), "it rests on top" / "spoon so it rests" (*Till Spring*), "back of a spoon" (five pours), and "It's the first thing" plus the arrives-first motif (*Before the Room*'s "The basil gets there first") are all gone.
- **Rule check:** no drink in hand (y4 describes the drink, and y5 points at life). Romance: none needs a signpost, since every motive given is hers, on her page. The one interpretation, "They had asked for her, not for anything she could bring", rests on the letter itself. One familiar touchstone (Verne). No gendered words about the guest ("good coat" is neutral). Her "as any other woman would" stays out. Clever-for-its-own-sake check: the hair at the gate. I kept it because it's on her page and it carries the point (nobody at the door cares how you arrive), told as a smile, not a pun.
- **Drink as proof (v2):** one connection, said in words (Robin's rule): the wine is most of the glass because the toast was the wine, and it's warm because they came out bareheaded into the cold for her. No spark from the *Flavor Matrix* (Tomás consulted it and set it aside: honey is *Any Day*'s). The drink is interesting in itself: a cocktail with no ice at all, mostly wine, which most guests have never been served. y4 says it appealingly ("soft, deep and warming: a good red with a spine of rye").
- **Verdict (v3): ready.** The "this is me" line is unchanged; every audit fix makes her more the Knight, not less (X2: the cost chosen, the promise guarded; X7: wanted for her company, signposted). History is about 48% of the reading (counted by sentence), under Robin's half. Closing line: Tomás's v1, as written.
