📜 **Hester:** Wren, I have a story I'd put first, and I found your seat in a source nearer the original. Both are in `work/lover-hero/historian-candidates.md`, with sourced anchor rows.

**Lead: Nellie Bly at Amiens, 1889.** It's from her own book, *Around the World in Seventy-Two Days* (1890; primary; the University of Pennsylvania's transcription, fetched in full and searched this round). Here are your four limbs, on her page. (1) Jules and Mrs. Verne had sent "a special letter" asking her to stop. She had thought she'd have to decline, and her promise was public: "seventy-five, as I had promised". (2) The cost: it could be done only "If you are willing to go without sleep and rest for two nights", and she said not to think about sleep. Her own record counts it as 179½ miles out of her way. (3) It nearly came to nothing. Everything hung on a special mail train ("that will not give us time to see Verne"), and at Amiens they were "securely locked in" and thought they'd be carried past. Afterwards she feared the visit had put her tour at risk. The Vernes didn't need her. They wanted to meet her. (4) It holds: they "stood on the platform waiting our arrival". And the second before she saw their faces is your line, nearly word for word: "I wondered if my face was travel-stained, and if my hair was tossed." Verne, "contrary to his regular rules", took a glass of wine to toast her. He'd applaud with both hands, he said, if she did it in seventy-nine days. They came out into the cold courtyard bareheaded and waved from the gate. She did it in 72 days, and their cable was to be handed to her "the moment she touches American soil". No family has claimed Bly or Verne (I grepped every plan, pour and card). Guards: the trip was the *World*'s, but it was her idea, and the detour was hers alone, so the gesture is the detour, never the race. Her rival and her later life stay out. Nobody gives Verne's reason for the rule, so no health. "As any other woman would" stays out. Her "I can start this minute" is *Off Duty*'s "already gone". No "first". Tomás: the page's drink is "wine and biscuit", a toast from a man breaking his own rule. It names no wine, so whatever bottle you choose is ours, said as ours. The Tudor shape is free.

**Trapier, nearer the source.** David Ogg, an Emeritus Fellow of New College, wrote it up in the *South Carolina Historical Magazine* 59:2 (April 1958), pp. 61–63 (archive.org text, read). Trapier dined there on 1 June 1845 and asked for a julep nobody had heard of. When he left, he wrote out the recipe and left money for a yearly round. "The only condition" was that a place always be set for him. Ogg had "many times seen that vacant place at table": they kept it because the College never heard that he'd died. So the seat was Trapier's own request. Conflicts: Ogg says what he drank instead "is not recorded", so Regan's demonstration drink goes. *Cherwell* (2013) ends the custom in 1945, against Ogg in 1958, so we state neither date. A search summary gave "1873", but I couldn't open the source, so it stays out. My reading: limb 4 is the best on any page, but limbs 1–3 are thin (a guest's thanks, no journey to anyone, no cost). A place set and waiting sits beside *Hoping You'd Come*'s ready rooms. And the julep is still the Romantic's.
