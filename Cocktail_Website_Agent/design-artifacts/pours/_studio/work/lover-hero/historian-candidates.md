# Hester: candidates for lover-hero (round 2, 2026-10-01)

Wren overturned Tudor (r1). Test: (1) went to someone in particular, after saying so or where all could see; (2) at a real cost; (3) a real chance on record it lands on nobody or wasn't needed; (4) it holds: someone was waiting for *them*. Out: contracts/trade, strangers rescued, crisis, war, helpless beloved, death. The 1833 book stays out.

Claims checked before proposing: `_studio/plans/*.md` Claims (all nine), sibling pour files, fact cards, registry. "Bly", "Verne", "Amiens": no hits anywhere. Quotations below were read on screen this session; each is under 15 words.

## A. Lead: Nellie Bly at Amiens (1889)

**Source (primary):** Nellie Bly, *Nellie Bly's Book: Around the World in Seventy-Two Days* (New York: Pictorial Weeklies, 1890), chs. III–V and XVII–XVIII. Transcription: *A Celebration of Women Writers*, University of Pennsylvania Digital Library, https://digital.library.upenn.edu/women/bly/world/world.html (fetched with curl, full text grepped). Chapter, not page, numbers: the transcription has no page breaks.

| kind | fact (as on the page) | meaning | speaksTo |
|---|---|---|---|
| story | Ch. III: the London correspondent tells her "Mr. & Mrs. Jules Verne have sent a special letter asking" her to stop. She had thought she'd have to decline. | They asked for her, not for anything she could bring. | being wanted, not needed |
| promise | Ch. IV: Verne doubts she can do it "in seventy-five, as I had promised." | The promise was public and hers. | keeping your word where it's seen |
| cost | Ch. III: it can be done only "If you are willing to go without sleep and rest for two nights"; she answers: "don't think about sleep or rest." | She paid for the detour herself. | the cost doesn't frighten them |

| risk | Ch. III–IV: at Amiens they were "securely locked in" and "began to think that we would be carried past". | Even at the station the door wouldn't open. | the second at the door |

| the doubt | Ch. IV: "If you do it in seventy-nine days, I shall applaud with both hands". | A friend who doubted her and wished her well anyway. | |

| holds | Ch. XVII: Verne's cable, to be handed to her "the moment she touches American soil", reached her in Chicago (it had missed her at San Francisco). | They kept her in mind all the way round. | someone waiting at the far end too |
| holds | Ch. XVII–XVIII: Hoboken 14 Nov 1889 to Jersey City 25 Jan 1890, "72 days, 6 hours and 11 minutes"; she wanted to yell with the crowd "because I was home again." | She kept the promise, and beat it. | |

**Guards.** The trip was the *New York World*'s assignment, but on her page it was her idea ("I want to go around the world!"), and the detour went beyond it, at her own cost: the gesture is the detour, never the race. Never the rival (Elizabeth Bisland), never her later life (marriage, business, bankruptcy). Why Verne had a rule against wine isn't given: no health, no motive. Her "as any other woman would have done" stays out (gender). Her "I can start this minute" (ch. I) echoes *Off Duty*'s "already gone": keep it out. No "first" (*Nothing to It*). The visit's exact date isn't on my screen: say "in 1889" or nothing. Sherard (a Paris journalist) and the London correspondent were with her: two men in the room, not a private visit. The page names no wine: any bottle in the glass is ours, labelled ours.

## B. Second: William Heyward Trapier and the place set at New College (1845), nearer the source

**Source (secondary, near-witness):** David Ogg (Emeritus Fellow of New College), "New College, Oxford, and South Carolina: A Personal Link", *South Carolina Historical Magazine* 59:2 (April 1958), pp. 61–63 (archive.org `sim_south-carolina-historical-magazine_1958-04_59_2`, djvu text read).

| kind | fact (as on the page) | meaning | speaksTo |
|---|---|---|---|
| story | Trapier, of Winyah, Georgetown, SC, dined with the Junior Fellows on 1 June 1845, asked for a mint julep; they had never heard of it. "What Trapier had instead is not recorded." | He brought something nobody had asked for. | |
| gift | On leaving he wrote out the recipe and left "a small sum of money" for a free round every 1 June. | | |
| the condition | "The only condition was that on this annual occasion (June 1) a place should always be set for him." | He asked to be expected. | someone waiting for them |
| holds | Kept in Ogg's nearly fifty years, "as the College has never had intimation of Trapier's death"; Ogg had "many times seen that vacant place at table". | They kept waiting for him, not for the drink. | they were waiting for you |
| object | He had a silver cup of about 1740 engraved, dated 1845, for the Junior Fellows' use. | | |

**Conflicts and gaps.** Regan (*Joy* pdf 342–343) and Daily Info say he showed them how / made a demonstration drink; Ogg says what he drank "is not recorded". Ogg wins (nearer). Harwell (via Regan) calls the bourbon casks "pure assumption": out. End date: *Cherwell* (28 Nov 2013, fetched) says juleps ran "Between 1845 and 1945" and reports a JCR motion to revive them; Ogg, writing in 1958, says the condition "has always been fulfilled". Contested: state neither. A search summary credited *The Oxford Companion to the Year* with "1873"; I couldn't open it, so it's out. Willard Connelly, *Adventures in Biography* (1960), has "the same seat is kept for Heyward Trapier, 'in case he should appear'" (archive.org OCR highlight, lending-only; incomplete).
**Fit (my reading):** limb 4 is the best on any page. Limbs 1–3 are thin: a guest's thanks, no journey to anyone, no cost, and the "risk" is only ours. The place set for someone who may come sits beside *Hoping You'd Come*'s ready rooms. And the julep is still reserved for the Romantic (lover-sage, unauthored): the host's call.

## C. Tomás's lead, checked (round 3): Lafayette's return, 1824–25

**Sources.** Auguste Levasseur (Lafayette's secretary, on the tour), *Lafayette in America in 1824 and 1825*, trans. J. D. Godman (Philadelphia, 1829), vol. 1 (archive.org `lafayetteinamer01godmgoog`) and vol. 2 (`lafayetteinamer00godmgoog`), djvu text, primary (an eyewitness, and an admirer). *Joy* pdf 24 (Regan citing Erdoes; secondary on secondary). Wikipedia, "Visit of the Marquis de Lafayette to the United States" and "Gilbert du Motier, Marquis de Lafayette" (fetched; leads only). archive.org full-text OCR highlights (incomplete). monticello.org blocked us (403).

| claim (Tomás) | on the page? | where |
|---|---|---|
| Monroe and Congress invited him | **Yes.** Monroe's letter: Congress passed a resolution expressing the nation's "ardent desire is once more to see you amongst them"; the timing "left entirely at your option"; a government vessel offered. | Levasseur vol. 1, opening chapter (letter quoted in full) |
| He crossed in August 1824 | **Yes, in part.** He refused the frigate and sailed on the *Cadmus*, an American merchant ship, from Le Havre in July, with only his son and Levasseur. Arrival at Staten Island 15 August is Wikipedia's (lead). | Levasseur vol. 1 |
| Jefferson came out and they embraced in tears | **Partly.** Levasseur arrived after it, and found Jefferson "still deeply affected by the pleasure of having embraced his ancient friend". The run and the tears ("Ah Jefferson!" "Ah Lafayette!") are Thomas Jefferson Randolph's later recollection, in print by 1879 (*Friends' Intelligencer*, 11 Oct 1879, OCR highlight), relayed by Wikipedia and Crawford, *Twilight at Monticello* (2008). A grandson remembering, and its first printing isn't found yet. | Levasseur vol. 1, ch. on Virginia |
| He lost his Chamber seat in 1824 | **Thin and conflicting.** Wikipedia contradicts itself ("left the French legislature in 1824" vs "until 1823, when new plural voting rules helped defeat his bid for re-election"). An OCR highlight from Sherman Kent, *The Election of 1827 in France* (1975) points to his loss at Meaux in the 1824 election, but the highlight is cut off. Levasseur: nothing. Not usable as stated. | |
| A fortune already gone | **Only as a panegyric.** Levasseur says he had "sacrificed his fortune and repose" in the French Revolution. And on the other side of limb 4: Congress voted him $200,000 and a township of land at Monroe's request (Wikipedia, lead). | Levasseur vol. 1, ch. I |
| Lafayette Punch and Lafayette brandy | Only *Joy* pdf 24, no recipe, no date beyond "1824". | |
| (new, for Tomás) a humble welcome with a drink | A country landlord's room with "Welcome Lafayette" written in charcoal on the wall, and a small table with decanters of "brandy and whiskey", bread, and glasses, "tendered with a kindness and cordiality". | Levasseur vol. 2, pp. 30–31 |

**Against Wren's limbs.** (1) Passes broadly, but he went to a nation, at its invitation, across 24 states. Jefferson is one stop of many (eleven days, per Wikipedia), and the reading would have to choose that door. (2) Partly: his age and a year on the road. The fortune was lost decades earlier, not on this journey. (3) **Fails on the page.** Everything says he was wanted before he sailed: a President's letter, a resolution of Congress, crowds from the first day. The only "they'd have been fine without me" is the Chamber seat, which is thin and is about France, not the arrival. (4) Passes, overwhelmingly. But the nation also paid him. **Traps:** the war is the engine of every welcome (veterans, the Brandywine scarf "still retained traces of his blood"); crowds and military pomp as the point; slavery at Monticello (Levasseur's own passage on Jefferson's slaves); the Fish House Punch is Regular Guy's. **My verdict:** a beautiful reunion, but the risk limb is missing, and the war carries everything.
