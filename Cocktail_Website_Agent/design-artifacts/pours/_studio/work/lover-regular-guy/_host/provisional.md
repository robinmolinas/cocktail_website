# Pour — Any Day · The Rough Diamond (lover-regular-guy)

## Cocktail

- **name:** Any Day
- **tagline:** Nobody you love ever has to ask you. They only have to mention it.
- **glassware:** rocks glass, one large plain cube (the biggest cube your freezer makes; not clear, not cut)
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 ml (2 oz) | bourbon | Elijah Craig Small Batch recommended, or any straight bourbon between 40% and 50% |
| 22.5 ml (¾ oz) | fresh lemon juice | squeezed the same day |
| 15 ml (½ oz) | honey syrup | three parts light runny honey (wildflower, or a light clover) to one part water, shaken in a closed jar until smooth |

**method**
1. Make the honey syrup: three spoons of light runny honey and one of water in a jar, lid on, shaken until it's smooth.
2. Put one large ice cube in a rocks glass.
3. Pour the bourbon, lemon and honey syrup into a shaker and fill it three-quarters with ice.
4. Close it and shake hard for thirty seconds.
5. Strain it over the cube. Nothing on top.

**closingLine:** *Thirty seconds of shaking. The cold's done by twelve. Say it in the other eighteen.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*Ordered after a long shift: the usual, with honey instead of sugar. Now it's a classic.*

**whoYouAre**
Someone you love mentions that the car's making that noise again. That's all. They'd never ask, and they don't need to: you heard it, because it's exactly how you'd have said it. By Saturday you're flat on your back underneath it, and neither of you makes a thing of it. A mention is enough. What comes back isn't a card or a speech. It's the thing done, for as long as it takes.

The sentence you've never quite managed is the other one, the one that says why. The feeling's there, full size. Said out loud, it sounds like someone else's words, so it stays put, and you tell yourself they know. Mostly they do. But now and then, on a long drive home, you think about the day it'll be too late to check. Here's what your way gives the people you love: they never have to find the words to ask, and they never owe you a thank-you. They always know where they can lean. That's worth more than any speech.

**yours**

1. In the late 1990s, Sasha Petraske was turning a tiny former tailor's shop in New York into a bar, and he ran out of money. He told T. J. Siegal, one of the few friends he'd made at school, that the plumber was coming and he didn't have any money. Siegal was clear, years later: it wasn't a request to invest. It was a friend saying he'd run out.

2. So for months, Siegal handed over all the cash he made at the restaurant where he worked. When the *New York Times* wrote the bar up, Petraske was furious; he thought it made him look bad. Siegal's answer, as he tells it, is four words: "I bought thirty copies." Maybe it was just good for business. I like to think it was the closest he came to saying he was proud of his friend.

3. You'd have heard it too. Not a money problem: help, from someone who hadn't asked. And you'd have answered in the same language: no speech, just months of turning up. And when you're proud of someone, you'd rather buy thirty copies than say so.

4. Sitting at the bar one night after a long shift, Siegal asked for his usual bourbon sour, with the honey syrup Petraske had started making instead of the sugar. "It was as simple as that," he said. He named it the Gold Rush, and years later found it chalked up under classic cocktails, last on a list that began with the Old-Fashioned and the Manhattan. It made him "a little bit giddy". The cocktail I've made for you starts from his order, as the recipe prints it: bourbon, lemon and honey syrup, shaken hard, over one large cube of ice, no egg, nothing on top. I've used a third less honey syrup than the printed recipe, so the bourbon gets a word in, the way I'd like you to. It's shaken for thirty seconds, as the recipe says. Thirty again: a coincidence, nothing more. After about twelve seconds, the drink barely changes.

5. Forget the speech. Nobody who loves you is waiting for one. You need one line, said while you're busy with something else. Under the car. Halfway through the job. On the drive home, eyes on the road. "I'd do this for you any day" is plenty. They've always known where they can lean. Now they'll know why.
