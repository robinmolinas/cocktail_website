## whoYouAre

At a wedding or a birthday, someone asks what you're up to these days. You tell them: the same place, the work you like, your usual walk in the morning. Then you watch them wait politely for the rest. There isn't any rest. Years ago you stepped out of the race everyone else is running, quietly, and you don't miss it.

Living with less was easy. Being loved by people who think you're wasting yourself is harder. "You could do so much more." They mean it kindly, which is why you don't argue. And under that sits a quieter fear. No one will drag you back in. But someone kind might ask, with a job you'd be good at or a favour "just for a while", and once you're back indoors, the part of you they liked would turn sour.

Your way does something for people, and someone should tell you. After an afternoon with you, they go home wanting a little less, and lighter for it. You make "enough" look like a choice, not a defeat, and you show them someone can say no and stay kind.

## yours

1. In late autumn, the writer Henry David Thoreau went into old pastures for the small, sharp apples farmers left out as unsalable. To someone out walking, he wrote, they were the choicest fruit. Indoors, he wrote, it tasted like another fruit: one he took out of his desk was "sour enough to set a squirrel's teeth on edge". There, he wrote, you miss the November air, "which is the sauce it is to be eaten with".

2. He knew he was describing himself: "one thought for the field, another for the house". His friend Emerson couldn't help "counting it a fault in him that he had no ambition": instead of engineering for all America, he was "the captain of a huckleberry party". Emerson meant it. In the same sketch, Emerson also wrote: "He chose to be rich by making his wants few, and supplying them himself."

3. As Thoreau tells it, nothing in the apple changes when it's carried in. Only the room does. You know that about yourself, so you keep turning down the bigger room, however kindly it's offered. Emerson wrote that it cost Thoreau nothing to say no. It costs you something every time, because you like the people asking. You're not hiding from anyone. You simply know where you're at your best. And I like to think the ones asking are curious about the world as you see it, and don't know how to say so.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Stone Fence, a drink from colonial America, on record under another name by 1750. I went back to its older pairing of rum and hard cider, and kept it sharp: aged rum and a dry, still farmhouse cider, the sharpest you can find, over two or three small lumps of ice, with no sugar to soften it, because sharp was never a fault that needed fixing. Thoreau wrote that wild apples like his were thought to make the best cider, though he still begrudged them to the cider mill. So, for the apples he'd rather have eaten in the field, a thin slice of raw, sharp apple goes in at the end.

5. You don't owe anyone a bigger life, or an explanation of this one. Thoreau once kept a smooth face while tempting a companion with one of those sour apples. By winter, when frost and thaw had turned them sweet, the two of them were filling their pockets together. So stop defending your life to the ones who worry about you. Have one of them come along. Some will taste what you taste, and you'll have someone to fill your pockets with.
