|---|---|---|---|
| story | Thoreau wrote that the wild apples of old pastures, the ones a farmer leaves out as unsalable, are the walker's choicest fruit in late October and early November (W5, W6). | What everyone else passes over as not worth gathering is, to the one who walks there, the best thing in the field. | yours: story |
| fear | He wrote that the same apple, brought into the house, often tastes harsh and crabbed, and that one he took from his desk indoors was unexpectedly crude, sour enough to set a squirrel's teeth on edge; indoors you miss the November air, which is its sauce (W7). Thoreau's claim, not a fact about apples. | The fear of being brought indoors and turning sour, in someone else's words: nothing in the apple changed, only the room. | whoYouAre (fears); yours: reveal |
| reveal | He applied it to himself: one thought for the field, another for the house; his thoughts, like wild apples, were food for walkers and not warranted palatable in the house (W9). He also wrote that "our wild apple is wild only like myself" (W2). | The mirror is his own, on the record, not ours: what he was is right in its own air and wrong in someone else's room. | yours: reveal |
| no fight | Thorny pasture apple bushes, he joked, have "no malice, only some malic acid"; when the tree finally rises, it lets the cows that cropped it into its shade (W3, W4). Same pages also use fighting words ("foes", "defeats"). | Sharp, not angry. Something that held its ground for years and, once grown, let them in to shade and fruit. | whoYouAre (gives); yours: why it's you |
| hidden cost | His friend Emerson wrote that Thoreau kept his freedom "at the cost of disappointing the natural expectations of his family and friends" (E2), and counted it "a fault in him that he had no ambition": instead of engineering for all America, "the captain of a huckleberry party" (E6). | The cost spoken by someone who loved him, and he meant it ("if, at the end of years, it is still only beans!"). | whoYouAre (hides); yours: why it's you |
| love | Emerson's next sentence calls these "foibles, real or apparent", and says Thoreau's study of nature made his friends curious to see the world through his eyes (E7). He was always ready to lead a huckleberry party with the young people he loved (E8). | The disappointment and the love sit in the same paragraph; the friend half grants it may only seem a fault. | yours: why it's you |
| gift | Emerson: "He chose to be rich by making his wants few, and supplying them himself." (E4). Emerson also hedged it: "Perhaps he fell into his way of living" (E5, C3). | Enough, called riches by the friend who doubted him. Quote Emerson's line as his; "chose on purpose" stays the guest's, not a fact about Thoreau. | whoYouAre (gives) |
| position | He once tempted a companion, keeping a straight face, with one of the sour apples; once frost and thaw had turned them sweet, "Now we both greedily fill our pockets" (W11, W12). The page doesn't say who brought whom, or when the tease happened. | He didn't carry the apples in to convince anyone: the companion was out there with him, where they taste right. (Winter, not October; "cheated" is his word, so it's a tease, not a lesson; never "he brought/took" the companion.) | yours: proposal |
| lineage | The Stone Fence (also Sampson, recorded from 1750) is spirit and cider in roughly equal parts, "originally rum, and then whisky", hard cider preferred (S1). Jerry Thomas's 1862 version: bourbon, sweet cider, "2 or 3 small lumps of ice" (S2). | The old country drink the bar made from the apple: the apple arrives as cider. Ours goes the older way, with rum and hard cider (never "the original recipe", C1). | yours: the drink as proof |
| ingredient | Wondrich notes that as late as 1869 a bar manual said a tart, full-flavoured crab-apple cider was often preferred to ordinary cider in the Stone Fence (S3, a relay). Thoreau wrote that small, high-coloured wild apples "are thought to make the best cider" (W10), and Oxford says today's best cider apples are high in tannin, sugar and acid (S5). | The sharp apple the market skips is what the cider and the old drink wanted, and still want. That's the clause that keeps "still best where it was left" true today (S5 is present tense). | yours: the drink as proof |
| lineage (two versions) | Wondrich: by the Civil War the Stone Fence was "a ghost of its former self"; Jerry Thomas's 1862 version (if his testimony is to be believed, Wondrich's hedge) was bourbon with sweet, non-alcoholic cider, which Wondrich calls "suave and smooth, but comparatively feeble"; the older drink was hard cider and New England rum (*Imbibe!* pdf 160, S2). **Dates:** the tart crab-apple cider (S3) is 1869, seven years *after* Thomas's 1862 book. | The sharp version didn't come before the soft one and vanish: it carried on beside it. Someone in 1869 still preferred the tart cider while the fashionable glass had gone smooth. Never "before it was softened" (wrong order); "feeble" is Wondrich's judgement, credited to him. | yours: the drink as proof |
| owned | Right before "best cider", Thoreau wrote: "I still begrudge them to the cider-mill" (W10). He drank no wine (E10); nothing says he drank cider. | Own it in one clause if the reading names cider at all: he'd rather have eaten them in the field. Never "Thoreau's drink". | yours: the drink as proof (fence) |
