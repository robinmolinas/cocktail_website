# Pour — Good as It Is · The Bumpkin (regular-guy-innocent)

## Cocktail

- **name:** Good as It Is
- **tagline:** Impressing you is hard. Delighting you is easy.
- **glassware:** rocks glass (double old-fashioned, about 300 ml), filled with cracked ice
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 ml | unaged cachaça | white (prata or branca), any bottle of 38–45%: the cane spirit itself |
| 1 | medium lime | cut across the middle, then each half into quarters; 6 of the 8 pieces go in |
| 2 heaped tsp (about 10 g) | granulated white sugar | grains, not syrup: they grind the oil out of the lime skin, so you taste the skin as well as the juice |
| | cracked ice | to fill the glass |

**method**
1. Cut the lime in half across the middle. Lay each half cut side down and cut it into quarters. Pick out any seeds you can see, because they make it bitter.
2. Put 6 of the pieces in a sturdy rocks glass and add the sugar. Keep the last 2 pieces for the next one.
3. Press each piece firmly with a muddler, or the end of a rolling pin, giving it a slight twist, until every piece is crushed and the sugar has mostly gone. Then stop.
4. Fill the glass with cracked ice. To crack it, wrap ice cubes in a clean tea towel and give them a few knocks with the rolling pin.
5. Pour in the cachaça and stir from the bottom for about ten seconds, so the sugar comes up into the drink.
6. Leave the lime pieces in. They keep flavouring the drink, and the peel can add a little bitterness, which is part of the pleasure.

**closingLine:** *Leave the lime in. Then let the whole table see you enjoying it.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*In Brazil, the law spells out what goes in a caipirinha. It's a short list.*

**whoYouAre**
At the wedding with seven courses and a little card for each, what you remember is the bread. Warm, with too much butter. You said so with your mouth still full, and you meant it. You know what the foam on the fish was meant to be. You've heard the words, and you can use them if you have to. You just liked the bread more.

What stings isn't being called plain. It's the half-smile that says you liked it because you don't know any better. So in some rooms you turn yourself down a notch, and more than once you've ordered the clever thing just to keep your seat at the table. That costs you more than it shows. What you're really afraid of is being let in as the sweet one, and never as one of them. But watch what happens on the nights you don't turn it down. Someone leans over and admits the bread was the best bit for them too. Then someone else does. It's worth more than it looks: with you there, people can stop performing and just enjoy it.

**yours**

1. Brazilians were already drinking cachaça, their spirit made from sugar cane, with lime by the middle of the 1800s. In the first half of the last century, the city of São Paulo gave it American touches: ice, and in some versions sugar syrup, or egg white and a splash of gin, which the poet Mário de Andrade described in 1944 as "sophisticated". Some drinkers turned the new touches down.

2. As one historian tells it, a third way came out of that: the old country drink, made right in the glass, taking the ice from the city version. It took the name caipirinha, from a word for someone from the countryside, and by the end of the 1960s it was known all over Brazil. Europe took to it later, and still loves it. Back home, the fashionable city crowd found cachaça too rustic and made the drink with vodka instead. Brazil's law, though, defines a caipirinha as cachaça, lime and sugar, and has since 2003.

3. I like to think the people who kept it plain knew exactly what the egg white and gin would do, and took the ice because cold is simply better, and left the rest because they liked it how it was. You choose the same way. You're not against new things. You take what's actually good and leave the show.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is the caipirinha, Brazil's famous classic, made with just three things, the same three its law names: cachaça, lime and sugar, and then the ice. The cachaça is an unaged one. I didn't add anything, as a nod to the people who kept it plain. I built it in the glass with no shaker, which is how I picture that third way. The sugar is crushed into most of a lime until it has nearly gone, and its grains scrub the oil from the skin, so you taste the skin as well as the juice. The sugar stays on the dry side, so the cane and the lime come through. The pieces stay in, peel and all, and if it's left to sit, the peel adds a little bitterness. It's cold, bright and sharp, with the cane spirit's warmth underneath.

5. A small dare, then. Next time a table goes quiet and careful over something clever, don't wait to hear what everyone else thought. Say what you loved, even if it was the bread. It'll be a better evening for it, and you'll learn how many of them were hoping someone would.

- **Glass and drink:** a sturdy rocks glass (about 300 ml), full of cracked ice. The drink is unaged cachaça (white, *Codex* p. 112) with muddled lime and dissolved sugar: pale and almost clear, slightly cloudy, with a green tint from the lime. That tint is my estimate from the ingredients; no page gives the colour. The crushed lime pieces are visible at the bottom, among the ice. No garnish, no straw, nothing on the rim (the recipe has none).
- **Props (four, the story in objects):** the cutting board with the two leftover pieces, the recipe's own (6 of 8 go in) · the bag of plain white sugar with grains spilled, the sugar kept as grains · the sugar cane, because cachaça is made from cane juice (Oxford CACHAÇA, F12) · the torn, half-eaten bread roll, Wren's picture of this person's delight (the persona, not a fact) and the reading's gift: someone admits the bread was the best bit too.
- **One impossible detail:** three extra glass shadows raised in a toast beside the one real glass. These are the people who were waiting for someone to say it first, which is Wren's "what they gain". ### Open items

- **Two accounts in one book:** Wondrich (CAIPIRINHA) tells the caipirinha as a third way beside the Batida Paulista; Smith (CACHAÇA) makes the Batida and the Caipirinha one drink, on the global stage in the 1970s (C1). The reading signposts Wondrich ("As one historian tells it") and gives no decade abroad.
- **The law's scope:** the decrees define the caipirinha as a product made and sold ("industrializada", 2025); they don't govern a bar or a kitchen. "Exclusivamente" is 2003's word only. The reading says Brazil's law defines it, never that our glass follows it. The glass's 17.8% happens to sit in the decrees' 15–36% band: Checks only.
- **The name:** *caipirinha* roughly "little hillbilly" (F7) never reaches the guest; the gloss is Oxford's "a person from the rural hinterlands", said once. Nobody on record named the drink or took the word back, so the guest makes the link.
- **No added spark, on purpose:** any addition would be the kind the story's drinkers turned down; the drink is interesting through the method (granulated sugar abrades the zest, *Joy* pdf 173; pieces left in, *Codex* p. 137). The sugar gets a taste reason, never a history one.
- **Muddled lime is unsourced as a volume:** modelled as 20 ml juice from 6 of 8 pieces; 17.5–20 ml in range, 22.5 an edge, 15 or 25 ml OUT (Tomás's sweeps). Cachaça at 38–45% (48% edges the strength).
- **Dossier only:** cures, the cholera ration, the 1918 flu legend (G1); the enslaved and Burton's grades; Kenya's Dawa (C2); the US; Andrade's tone (Wondrich's English, unknown).
