**The conflict (C1).** Oxford's CACHAÇA entry (pdf 380, Frederick H. Smith) says "the Batida Paulista, is better known by its alternate name, the Caipirinha", and puts it on "the global stage in the 1970s". Wondrich's own entry also says "Batida Paulista" "appears to have been" one of the Caipirinha's original names (pdf 383). So the caipirinha as a separate drink that turned the improvers down is Wondrich's account. We tell it as his ("as one historian tells it"), or after "I like to think". And the decade it took off abroad is disputed (1970s or 1980s), so "the city dropped the cane in the same decade Europe fell for it" can't be said. The Caipiroska's "early 1980s" (pdf 384) is fine by itself. **And (C2):** Kenya's near-national Dawa is "a Kenyan take on the Caipirinha, or rather the Caipiroska", from 1980 (DAWA, pdf 631). The vodka version travelled too, so "the one the world orders" is out. What we can say is "loved in Europe, where it remains popular" (pdf 384), with no date.

**The replacement, which I think is better: Brazil wrote the plain version into law.** Decree 4.851 of 2 October 2003: the caipirinha is "a bebida típica brasileira", made "exclusivamente com Cachaça, acrescida de limão e açúcar", meaning only cachaça, with lime and sugar. The 2009 decree (6.871, art. 68 §5) kept the three, and so does the current one, Decree 12.709 of 31 October 2025, art. 74. All three are read on planalto.gov.br. The fashionable crowd swapped the cane for vodka (pdf 384). Then the country set down in law that the name belongs to the cane, the lime and the sugar. That vindicates it **as plain**, and nobody gets sneered at. **Guards:** the decrees cover drinks made and sold as products (the 2025 text says "industrializada"). They don't cover what a bar or a kitchen pours. "Exclusively" is only the 2003 wording. Never "banned the vodka one". The law says what may carry the name. Safe line: *"Brazil's law defines a caipirinha as cachaça, lime and sugar, and has since 2003."*

**On the name (c):** I'll accept it said once if the gloss stays on the page. Oxford says "a southern Brazilian epithet for a person from the rural hinterlands", so "a word for someone from the countryside" is fair. It's "a word", not "the word", and never "affectionate" or "proud", because the page says neither. Nobody on record named the drink or took the word back, so that stays the guest's own link, as you said. **Three more fences:** *pinga com limão* is "of no great antiquity", on record only from the mid-1800s (pdf 383), so never "centuries-old". Andrade's "sophisticated" comes to us in Wondrich's English, in quotation marks, and we don't know his tone, so it's neither a sneer nor praise. The famous "1918 flu remedy" origin isn't in Oxford: it's a legend, and it's out with the cures.
