## whoYouAre

At the wedding with seven courses and a little card for each, what you remember is the bread. Warm, with too much butter. You said so with your mouth still full, and you meant it. You know what the foam on the fish was meant to be. You've heard the words, and you can use them if you have to. You just liked the bread more.

What stings isn't being called plain. It's the half-smile that says you liked it because you don't know any better. So in some rooms you turn yourself down a notch, and more than once you've ordered the clever thing just to keep your seat at the table. That costs you more than it shows. What you're really afraid of is being let in as the sweet one, and never as one of them. But watch what happens on the nights you don't turn it down. Someone leans over and admits the bread was the best bit for them too. Then someone else does. It's worth more than it looks: with you there, people can stop performing and just enjoy it.

## yours

1. Brazilians have drunk cachaça, their spirit made from sugar cane, with lime and sugar since the middle of the 1800s. In the first half of the last century, the city of São Paulo gave it American touches: ice, sugar syrup, and for some, egg white and a splash of gin, which the poet Mário de Andrade described in 1944 as "sophisticated". Some drinkers turned the new touches down.

2. As one historian tells it, a third way came out of that: the old country drink, built simply in the glass, keeping just one thing from the city version, the ice. It took the name caipirinha, from a word for someone from the countryside, and by the end of the 1960s it was known all over Brazil. Europe took to it later, and still loves it. Back home, the fashionable city crowd found cachaça too rustic and made the drink with vodka instead. Brazil's law, though, defines a caipirinha as cachaça, lime and sugar, and has since 2003.

3. I like to think the people who kept it plain knew exactly what the egg white and gin would do, and took the ice because cold is simply better, and left the rest because they liked it how it was. You choose the same way. You're not against new things. You take what's actually good and leave the show.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is the caipirinha, Brazil's famous classic, with only the three things its law names: unaged cachaça, a whole lime and sugar, and then the ice. I didn't add anything, as a nod to the people who kept it plain. I built it in the glass, with no shaker, the way that third way was made. The sugar is crushed into the cut lime until it disappears, and its grains scrub the oil from the skin, so you taste the whole lime, not only its juice. The sugar stays on the dry side, so the cane and the lime come through. The pieces stay in, and the lime keeps building to the end. It's cold, bright and sharp, with the cane spirit's warmth underneath.

5. A small dare, then. Next time a table goes quiet and careful over something clever, don't wait to hear what everyone else thought. Say what you loved, even if it was the bread. You'll enjoy it properly, and you'll learn how many of them were hoping someone would.
