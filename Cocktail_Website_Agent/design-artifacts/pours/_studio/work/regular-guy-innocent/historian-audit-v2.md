## Wren's three questions

1. **Epigraph, "the law spells out what a caipirinha is. It's a short list.": PASS.** The 2025 decree (art. 74) is a definition, word for word: "Caipirinha é a bebida típica … elaborada com cachaça, limão e açúcar". "Spells out what it is" makes no claim about anyone's glass or bar. "A short list" is fair: three ingredients (the ABV band and, in 2009, the allowance for water and additives aren't ingredients that change the list). It works cold. And it no longer says "what goes in", which was the scope problem.
2. **y1's date: A6. The Andrade quote: PASS.** "Described in 1944 as 'sophisticated'" matches pdf 383 ("as the great Brazilian poet Mario de Andrade characterized them in 1944"). The quotation marks are Wondrich's around a one-word English rendering of a Portuguese article, so the dossier records the channel (F4: Wondrich's English, tone unknown). In guest text a single cognate word, not glossed with any tone, is a fair relay. "The poet" is Oxford's.
3. **The y2 signpost: it covers enough. PASS.** Only one thing in y2 is contested (C1): whether the caipirinha was a third drink, separate from the Batida Paulista. That's the sentence the signpost opens. "It took the name caipirinha, from a word for someone from the countryside" and "by the end of the 1960s it was known all over Brazil" hold under either account: Smith also calls the drink the Caipirinha, the gloss is uncontested, and so is the date (F7). They follow straight on in the same paragraph, as the historian's telling continues. "Europe took to it later, and still loves it" has no decade (C1): PASS. "The fashionable city crowd found cachaça too rustic and made the drink with vodka instead" matches pdf 384: PASS. The law line is my safe line verbatim: PASS (2003 → 2009 → 2025 is continuous, each decree replaced by the next). **Guard:** if y2 is ever split, the new paragraph needs its own signpost.
