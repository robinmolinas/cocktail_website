# Reading v1: The Wise Fool (sage-jester), Wren, round 3

**name:** In Good Part (my pick; Tomás's second). I could take *A Laughing Matter*.

**tagline**
You'll be the silly one, so nobody has to be the wrong one.

**epigraph**
*A club that teased politicians over dinner had a drink of its own. It was pink.*

## whoYouAre

Someone at the table has started to believe their own story. A boss, an old friend who's done well, the relative everyone humours. You don't correct them. Correcting them would cost them something with everyone watching. So you make it funny, carefully, aimed at them and not at the room, and they're the one laughing hardest. Nothing else happens that night. A week later, quietly, they've dropped it. Nobody mentions the joke, least of all you.

It sounded like a quip. It took you most of the evening. Nobody guesses how much the untrue thing bothered you while you were smiling: you can sit with almost anything except a story everyone has agreed to believe. The room that frightens you is the one where nobody's allowed to laugh, because that's where it stays true for good. And there's a cost you rarely mention. When you mean something about your own life, people are still waiting for the funny part.

So here it is, plainly, because it's worth something: you let people be wrong and keep their dignity. It's kinder than honesty on its own, and much harder.

## yours

1. In Philadelphia in the 1880s, some journalists started a dining club. Once a month they had dinner at a Philadelphia hotel, and they were known for good food, plenty of drink and teasing politicians. They called themselves the Clover Club.

2. The politicians were at the table. They'd been invited: in its first two years the club's guests included two former governors and an attorney-general. After the oysters, the president drank to the guests from a silver loving cup, and the guests drank from it standing while the members stayed in their seats. Then came the speeches, and what the club's own little book called a "war of wit". They liked to say it was kindly meant and well received. And every new member was sworn in sitting in a baby's chair, holding a rattle.

3. I recognise you in that. They didn't shout from outside. They asked the powerful to dinner, honoured them first, and they'd already made themselves look ridiculous with a rattle. I like to think that's why a guest could laugh along: nobody at that table had to lose face to hear it. You do the same. You go first as the silly one, so the person it's about can take it home.

4. The club had its own cocktail, but only late in its life: gin, lemon, raspberry syrup and egg white, frothy and pink, first printed in 1901 by the Waldorf-Astoria's head bartender. In 1934 *Esquire* named it one of the ten worst cocktails, and it lay forgotten until the late 2000s, once a new bar took its name. It's a classic again, and the cocktail I've made for you is a Clover Club, with a syrup of my own: I make the raspberry syrup with red wine instead of water, as a nod to those dinners, where the wit came with the wine. It looks like the least serious thing you could order, pink under a soft cap of froth. Underneath is a full measure of gin, and the raspberry tastes deeper, of fruit and wine rather than sweets. You only find that out by tasting it. I like to think your best jokes work the same way.

5. Keep telling the truth like that. The people who've been wrong around you are luckier than they know. But every joke of yours is working for something: the truth, the face, whoever it's about. Now and then, make one that carries nothing at all, daft from start to finish, just because it makes you laugh. The club bought itself a rattle, after all. I hope you get to be silly for the fun of it, and that somebody laughs with you.

## Rulings (round 3)
- **Hester's question, the motto ("While we live, we live in Clover…"):** dossier, as she proposed. It's about living well, not about truth told upward, and it would spend the reading's one quotation. The quotation I use is "war of wit" (three words, her fence A4).
- **"A. Jin Rickki":** out, agreed. Her reason convinced me (fashion, not truth told upward).
- **The hotel:** "a Philadelphia hotel" only (C1). "In the 1880s", no year (C2). Founders: "some journalists started" (C3: founded by journalists, never "a club of journalists").
- **The club's claim (A4):** told as "They liked to say it was kindly meant and well received", never as an outcome. y3's "a guest could laugh along" sits under "I like to think". No guest is named and no roast is described.
- **"Sworn in":** Hester's anchor wording (A5). Hester: the book says the chair and rattle were bought "for the newcomer" after a few meetings. If "every new member was sworn in" in it overstates, give me the fix and I'll land it.
- **The wine tie (y4):** "where the wit came with the wine" rests on the speeches beginning before the coffee (F8) and the members' wine (F10). If Hester confirms Crockett's "dined and wined" on a primary page, it can stay as it is. If not, and she thinks the clause overreaches, I'd change it to "as a nod to those long dinners". Never "tannic" or "bite" (Tomás).
- **Position (P1), with Tomás's closing line:** *"Then tell one joke tonight that's for nobody's good"* agrees with y5. y5 says "carries nothing at all" so the two don't echo. I'd keep his line.
- **Lear:** not used. Hester hasn't read the text, and the reading doesn't need a touchstone.

## Resonance
- **As the guest:** "Correcting them would cost them something in front of everyone" is the motive nobody credits them with. People think the joke is how they get away with it. It's how they spare someone. "It took you most of the evening" is the hidden cleverness. "People are still waiting for the funny part" is the cost they'd never say. The gift is said out loud, and the position gives them something back rather than asking them to hold back.
- **As the sceptic:** could this be the Subverter (*With the Bite In*)? No. There's no nodding table, no head of the table laughing late, nothing "gets past" anyone, and nobody's nerve is the point. Here the joke is aimed at one person, for that person's sake, and the story is insiders who invited the powerful in. The Prankster (*Got You*)? No: nobody's being taken in, and there's no belonging test. The Stand-up? There's no "everybody", no confession. The Mentor? There's no coaching: they drop it, and nobody gets credit. The Prodigy? Nobody is hiding what they know to be accepted. Barnum check: "you can sit with almost anything except a story everyone has agreed to believe" is Sage-wide in feeling, but the scene around it (the joke aimed at one person, the cost of being taken for the funny one) isn't. Weakest beat: w1's three examples are general on purpose.
- **Verdict:** ready for audit. "This is me" line: *"You don't correct them. Correcting them would cost them something with everyone watching."*
