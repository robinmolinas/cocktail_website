# Pour — In Good Part · The Wise Fool (sage-jester)

## Cocktail

- **name:** In Good Part
- **tagline:** You'll be the silly one, so nobody has to be the wrong one.
- **glassware:** coupe, chilled in the freezer, no ice in the glass
- **contains:** `["egg-white"]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 ml | Plymouth gin, recommended | as Oxford's Clover Club recipe prints it; or any soft, earthy dry gin, less juniper-led than a London dry, about 41-44% |
| 15 ml | fresh lemon juice | squeezed the same day |
| 35 ml | raspberry and red wine syrup | homemade: 100 g fresh raspberries, 100 g white sugar and 100 ml dry young red wine (a Cabernet Sauvignon style), blended cold and sieved; makes about 250 ml, enough for seven drinks; keeps a week in the fridge |
| 15 ml | egg white | about half a large white, loosened with a fork first so it pours |

**Glassware:** coupe, chilled in the freezer, no ice in the glass.
**Contains:** egg-white.

**method**
1. Up to a week ahead, make the syrup. Put the raspberries, sugar and wine in a blender and blend on low until you can't feel any sugar grains, about a minute. Leave it 30 minutes, then press it through a fine sieve to take out the seeds. Jar it and keep it in the fridge.
2. Put the coupe in the freezer.
3. Crack an egg, keep the white, and whisk it lightly with a fork for a few seconds so it pours. Measure 15 ml.
4. Pour the gin, lemon juice, syrup and egg white into a shaker. Close it and shake hard with no ice for about 10 seconds, so the white turns to froth.
5. Open it, fill it with ice, and shake hard again for 12 to 15 seconds, until the outside is too cold to hold comfortably.
6. Strain it into the cold coupe through a small tea strainer held over the glass, so the froth comes out smooth.

**closingLine:** *Froth it before the ice goes in. Then tell one joke tonight that's for nobody's good.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*A club that teased politicians over dinner had a drink of its own. It was pink.*

**whoYouAre**
Someone at the table has started to believe their own story. A boss, an old friend who's done well, the relative everyone humours. You don't correct them. Correcting them would cost them something with everyone watching. So you make it funny, carefully, aimed at them and not at the room, and they're the one laughing hardest. Nothing else happens that night. A week later, quietly, they've dropped it. Nobody mentions the joke, least of all you.

It sounded like a quip. It took you most of the evening. Nobody guesses how much the untrue thing bothered you while you were smiling: you can sit with almost anything except a story everyone has agreed to believe. The room that frightens you is the one where nobody's allowed to laugh, because that's where it stays true for good. And there's a cost you rarely mention. When you mean something about your own life, people are still waiting for the funny part.

So here it is, plainly, because it's worth something: you let people be wrong and keep their dignity. It's kinder than honesty on its own, and much harder.

**yours**

1. In Philadelphia in the 1880s, some journalists started a dining club, the Clover Club. Once a month they had dinner at a hotel there, and they had set out to enjoy good food, plenty of drink and teasing politicians.

2. Politicians were at the table. They'd been invited: in its first two years the club's guests included two former governors and an attorney-general. The guests were toasted from a silver loving cup, then drank from it standing while the members stayed seated. Then came the speeches, and what the club's own book called a "war of wit". The book claims it was kindly meant and well received. And after a few meetings, the club got a baby's chair and a rattle for welcoming each new member.

3. I recognise you in that. They didn't shout from outside. They asked the powerful to dinner, honoured them first, and they'd already made themselves look ridiculous with a rattle. I like to think that's why a guest could laugh along: nobody at that table had to lose face to hear it. You do the same. You go first as the silly one, so the person it's about can take it home.

4. The club had its own cocktail: gin, lemon, raspberry syrup and egg white. Its recipe first turns up in 1901, when the Waldorf-Astoria's head bartender gave it to a paper, the *New York Press*. In 1934 *Esquire* named it one of the ten worst cocktails, and it was all but forgotten until a new bar named itself after it, late in the 2000s, and poured it as its own. It's a classic again, and the cocktail I've made for you is a Clover Club, with a syrup of my own: I make the raspberry syrup with red wine instead of water, as a nod to those dinners, where the wit came with the wine. It looks like the least serious thing you could order, pink under a soft cap of froth. Underneath is a full measure of gin, and the raspberry tastes deeper, of fruit and wine rather than sweets. You only find that out by tasting it. I like to think your best jokes work the same way.

5. Keep telling the truth like that. The people who've been wrong around you are luckier than they know. But every joke of yours is working for something: the truth, the face, whoever it's about. Now and then, make one that carries nothing at all, daft from start to finish, just because it makes you laugh. That club kept a baby's rattle, after all. I hope you get to be silly for the fun of it, and that somebody laughs with you.
