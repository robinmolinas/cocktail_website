## whoYouAre

Two hours into a polite evening, you lean over and whisper, "Coming?" Twenty minutes later the two of you are sitting on a kerb with chips, laughing too loudly. Then they tell you something they've never told anyone. You weren't getting them into trouble. You were getting through to them.

People call you a bad influence, and you let them, because it's half true and it makes a better story. The rest of it: polite feels lonely to you. It keeps everyone at arm's length, you included. You don't want an audience. You want accomplices. Under every "come on" there's a "please come", and you'd rather raise the stakes than say that plainly. What you dread is being kept for the noise: loved as the wild one and never really known, or made sensible and quietly dropped. That's your gift: around you, people drop the act. They say the honest thing, the funny thing, and they like who they are when they're with you.

## yours

1. Early in 2004, Dushan Zaric and Jason Kosmas left Schiller's, a New York bar, and took its bar manager with them. Two more colleagues joined them. The owner, Kosmas said, wasn't happy with them. That autumn the five opened Employees Only. From the start they broke the scene's rules together: they poured by eye while the serious bars measured, and they served food.

2. Some of the city's serious cocktail people decided, as Kosmas remembered it, that they couldn't really care about their drinks "because people are having too much fun there". Zaric didn't argue. They weren't the best mixologists, he said, and they "chose to play a different card in order to provide experience".

3. And from opening day, anyone still at the bar at closing time got homemade chicken soup, free. Partly that was business: Zaric had seen hungry customers leave the bar he'd worked at before. Partly it was their European background, where eating and drinking went together. And the staff stayed. Very few ever left. Zaric said why: they felt "they had a hand in creating this". I like to think that's why your people stay too. Not for the noise, but because with you, they got to help make it. The dare is what they talk about. What they remember is that you wanted them there.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a rye Manhattan, a classic the whole world knows, with the orange curaçao Employees Only added to theirs. My change is the vermouth. It's steeped for about twelve hours with raw beetroot that you peel and slice yourself, and your fingers will go red. I put it there as a nod to that hand in it: you can't make this drink without it marking you. Stirred over ice, it goes into a coupe, as theirs did. It tastes rich and smooth, earthy and sweet beneath the rye, with orange oil on top.

5. You don't have to keep raising the stakes to keep people. Some time soon, plan something with nothing to dare in it: a Sunday food shop, soup on the stove, a film you've both seen twice. See who's glad to be there. Those are your accomplices, and they always were. They'll still be around when nothing's happening, and you get to be known for more than the noise.

## Names (three or more; pick held)

- **Accomplices** (my pick): the one true thing in a word. People who are in it with you, not watching. It's fond, a little cheeky, and sayable at a bar.
- *Red-Handed* (Tomás): I could take it. For this person it reads as fond, not accusing: you get caught red-handed *with* someone. ## Resonance

**The "this is me" line:** *You don't want an audience. You want accomplices.* Its runner-up is whoYouAre ¶1's last line, *You weren't getting them into trouble. You were getting through to them.*

**As the guest (a Bad Influence).** The tagline makes me grin. It's what I'd say in my own defence, and nobody has ever said it for me. The epigraph, read cold: a bar that put something odd in its Manhattan, and a drink that stains. I want to know why. ¶1 is me, the "shall we go?" and the kerb. And then the last line catches me, because the point was never the leaving. ¶2 names what I'd never admit: polite feels lonely, and "please come" hides under "come on". The fear is exact, and it has two sides, which is how it actually feels. Then it gives me something back: people like who they are with me. The story is a gang that left together and got written off as the fun bar. The soup makes me soften, and "a hand in creating this" is the real reason my people stay. y5 doesn't tame me. It asks for one ordinary plan, as a test I can win, and ends on being known.

**As the sceptic.** Barnum check: "polite feels lonely" is specific (most people find polite safe); "you'd rather raise the stakes than say it" is specific; the two-sided fear is specific to this sheet ("tamed into someone safe, then left for being boring"). "They like who they are with you" could fit a few personalities, but it's anchored by "drop the act" right before it.
