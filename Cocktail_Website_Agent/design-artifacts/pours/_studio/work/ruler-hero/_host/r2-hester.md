📜 **Hester:** Wren, I have a correction that changes your Beam, then your answer on Duffy.

**1. The ownership deal is contested, so it comes out.** Oxford JIM BEAM (pdf 1105, Bryson, from Pacult 2003) says that by August 1934 he had raised capital from the Blums, and "the Beams would manage operations, the Blums would own the business". Chuck Cowdery (bourbon historian, blog, May 2024, secondary) has it differently: "In the 1940s, the family sold the company to a trio of Chicago investors", and Harry Blum was "by then sole owner" by the 1960s. That's two accounts of when the family stopped owning it. So "he takes the Blums' terms" can't carry limb 2, and the plan's "gave up the ownership himself" goes too. That line was in my row, so I'm striking it as mine. Also, Oxford has him taking over "the operation of Old Tub in 1892". It says operation, and it never says he owned it.

**2. What both sources hold:**
- **Place:** at Repeal, aged sixty-nine, he brought back his brother Park, Park's sons Carl and Earl, and his son T. Jeremiah. Oxford says he "gathered his family". Cowdery says the two brothers "with their sons" reopened the distillery, so we never say "he alone".
- **Cost:** Oxford: "To Jim's chagrin, he learned that he had lost the rights to the Old Tub name". Cowdery: "some problems with ownership of the Old Tub brand name". He learned it was gone. He didn't choose it, so never "he gave up the name".
- **Mark:** the whisky took his own name, in both sources. "A mark he never sought" isn't on either page, so signpost it or drop it.
- **They kept their place:** Oxford: after he retired in 1944, Carl and T. Jeremiah ran operations. Cowdery: the new owners "kept the name, and the family". That's now the strongest limb, with two sources.

So limb 2 holds only if the hard decision is that he went on without the old name and kept them working. That's your ruling. Limbs 1, 3 and 4 are firmer than before, and "chagrin" is your wince in the record's own word. If limb 2 must be a sacrifice he chose, Beam fails. Nothing else I found in the library passes all four:
- Employees Only's barback, promised the next bartender's job and given it nine months later (*Proper* p. 156): the promise is kept, but no one person made it and nothing it cost is on the page.
- Alexander Walker's 1885 "more than one head can carry" (Oxford pdf 1111): it ends with him bringing others into the business, which is handing the load over. The Saviour, Auteur and Firefighter own that.
