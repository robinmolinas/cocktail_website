# Persona card: The Noble Ruler (ruler-hero), Wren, round 1

Source: persona.py ruler-hero (essence "the benevolent leader, the heroic figure"; goal Control, fear Revolution; drivers: provide structure / LEAVE A MARK; Ruler fears chaos, vulnerability, failure; Hero fears weakness, timidity). King Arthur is the sheet's image: a legend, never a fact, and never in guest text.

## The one true thing
Their word is the floor other people stand on. They promise people a place, and they'll give up something that's theirs (what they own, their name, their credit, the easy way out) before they let that promise break. Duty is how they love. From the outside it doesn't look like love. It looks like a decision.

## Shows / hides
- **Shows:** steadiness. The call gets made, the plan holds, everyone knows where they stand.
- **Hides:** what it cost. They take the loss in private and present it as the obvious thing to do. The right register is a wince, not grief and not a complaint. They also hide that they decided alone, and weren't always sure.

## Fear
That the promise breaks on their side: the people who counted on their word find out it didn't hold. Keep this apart from the Firefighter, who owns "the one who wasn't enough when someone was counting on you" (*Anyone Would Have*). That fear is about competence. This one is about trust. Showing the cost feels to them like the promise wobbling, which is why they don't (the Ruler's fear of looking vulnerable plus the Hero's fear of looking weak).

## Gift (say it out loud, once)
The people around them can stop worrying. Their place will still be there tomorrow, so they can get on with their lives. That's worth a great deal, and almost nobody thanks them for it, because when it works you can't see it.

## Traps
- Royal, crown, knight, throne, court, Round Table, "reign": archetype puns. "Statue" too (they'd hate being one).
- **"Colonel"** (the page's "Colonel James B. Beam Whiskey") is a military title: never in guest text. Say "his own name".
- Patriarch, head of the family, "Boys": gendered, and they assume a family. The Beam quote stays attributed family memory, paraphrased.
- Martyr talk ("you sacrifice everything"). Gentle on the cost: one wince, not a wound.
- "Duty" read cold. Show it as love, once and plainly.
- The boss or "leader" as a label (the Entrepreneur owns "never wanted to be the boss").
- Envy. Any story built on minding someone else's attention reads as resentment.

## Story test
1. A place promised or held for named others (more than one person).
2. A hard decision taken by the person, giving up something of their own (ownership, name, credit, standing) so the others keep their place.
3. The cost felt in private, **on the page**. The decision and the cost are events and can't be signposted. Only the motive can.
4. The others did keep their place.
5. Out: a rescue in the moment, death or sacrifice of life, war, a legend told as fact, a shared bowl (*Four Shares*).

## Verdict on the plan's lead (Tarling): replace it with the backup (Jim Beam, Oxford JIM BEAM pdf 1105)
I re-read the pages whole, and Tarling fails:
- **The founding is contested.** UNITED KINGDOM BARTENDERS GUILD (pdf 2069) says a group of bartenders who had competed in a European competition founded the guild, with Craddock as first president and Angerosa helping him run it. The Tarling entry (pdf 1988) says "the pair" founded it.
- **The book is contested.** The Tarling entry has him "instigating" *Approved Drinks* "that year". MIXOGRAPHY (pdf 1323) gives the guild's first book as *The Book of Approved Cocktails* (1937), "under Harry Craddock and William Tarling", so Craddock's name heads the very book the reveal says was for everyone. A recognition line can't rest on a contested detail.
- **Only limb 1 passes.** No promise, no hard decision, no cost and nobody protected appear on the page. Those are events, so they can't be signposted.
- **The mirror is wrong for this guest.** A concern about another man's attention reads as envy, and "not one name" sits on the Firefighter's ground.

Beam passes every limb on one page:
- (1) Prohibition had "shut everything down" (a date clause only). At sixty-nine he gathered his brother Park, Park's sons Carl and Earl, and his son T. Jeremiah.
- (2) He raised the money from the Blums on plain terms: "the Beams would manage operations, the Blums would own the business".
- (3) "To Jim's chagrin" he had lost the rights to the family's Old Tub name. That's the private wince, in the page's own word.
- (4) Distilling started 120 days later. When he retired in 1944, Carl and T. Jeremiah ran it.
- Bonus: the drivers' "leave a mark" comes true without him seeking it. The whisky took his own name and became "the familiar 'Jim Beam'". Own the *Nothing to It* overlap in one clause, or keep the reveal on the lost name rather than the gained one.

**Guards:**
- Never "Colonel".
- The "Boys…" line is family memory, attributed and paraphrased ("us" also trips the one-voice lint).
- The Blums are never villains.
- Booker Noe, American Tobacco and Suntory stay out, and so does Boehm (the ancestor's name change, which would make a second name story).
- His motive for accepting the terms isn't on the page: "I like to think…".
- "Best-selling bourbon" is Oxford's, and usable ("let the drink sound worth having").

**The clash:** the plan allows only one Repeal-1933 story in the family, and Duffy (the Legend's lead, which has no backup) is the other.

## Position direction
It's encouragement, and it's in their interest: let them see you mind, once. A promise kept with a wince is still kept, and the people you keep it for would rather know. It stays clear of "tell them you were scared" (fear: Big Sibling, Pioneer) and of handing the load over (Saviour, Auteur, Firefighter). An alternative, as affirmation: what you gave up was never the mark you leave; they are.
