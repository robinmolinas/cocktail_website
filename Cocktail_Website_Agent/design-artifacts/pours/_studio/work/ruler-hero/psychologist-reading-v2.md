## whoYouAre

When you tell people there's a place for them, they stop worrying. They can, because you never do. You keep your word the way other people keep a house: every day, mostly unseen, and without asking whether it suits you this week.

You said there'd be a place for all of them. You never said what it cost you. Something of yours goes missing along the way, a plan, a weekend, the thing you'd hoped to put your name to, and you act as if it were the obvious thing to do. Partly because it is. Partly because you're afraid that if they saw you mind, they'd ask themselves whether the promise still holds. It does. Because of you, the ones who rely on you get to stop worrying about tomorrow, and most people go a whole life without that.

## yours

1. Old Tub was a good name for a bourbon. Before 1900 it had made a name for itself in Kentucky, made by Jim Beam's father and grandfather, and in 1892 Jim took over running it, with his brother-in-law. Then Prohibition shut everything down. When it ended, late in 1933, Jim was sixty-nine.

2. He gathered the family: his brother Park, Park's sons Carl and Earl, and his own son, T. Jeremiah. The family still remembers him telling them it was time they all went back to work. The next summer a new distillery was going up, and four months later its stills were running. Then he found out that the right to the Old Tub name had been lost. He minded. The word used for it is chagrin, which is the polite word for a wince. The whiskey went out carrying his own name instead. You've almost certainly seen it.

3. After he retired in 1944, his nephew Carl and his son T. Jeremiah ran it, and last I read, his descendants still have a big say in how it's made. What he told them about the name is lost too. I like to think he said very little: there was work to do, and he'd promised them a place in it. I recognise that in you. You keep the place you promised, and when something of yours is lost along the way, you carry it quietly and go on.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a New York Sour, a renowned classic. Mine is bourbon, fresh lemon and sugar, shaken cold and poured without ice, sharp and clean. I'd make it with Jim Beam, since it carries the name he ended up with, though any Kentucky straight bourbon will do. Then one thing goes over the top: a float of dry red wine, a thin red band above the gold. Only the wine shows, and it brings a little dry pucker to the bright sour. Back in 1883, a bartender in Chicago said the wine made the drink look good and taste better. I put it there for the one thing in this story that stung.

5. One request, then. When keeping a promise costs you something, say so, once, to someone you kept the place for. The promise won't look any weaker. It'll look exactly like what it is: something you care about enough to pay for. And they'll finally get the chance to thank you, which you so rarely allow.

## Closing line (Tomás owns it; my wording offer)
Tomás's r3: *Float the red so it shows. Then, once, let one of them know it cost you something.* "Let one of them know" is *Night Light*'s closing line ("Let one of them know you were scared too"). My offer, keeping his gesture: *Float the red so it shows. Once in a while, so should the cost.*

## Resonance (v2, Wren): ready, pending Hester's audit

- **"This is me" line:** *You said there'd be a place for all of them. You never said what it cost you.* (whoYouAre, paragraph 2.) It names the hidden price of a kept word, which no sibling carries. The Caring Leader's cost is patience and the Perfectionist's is warmth. The Firefighter fears being the weak link, which is a different fear from the price of a promise.
- **Cold title block:** the tagline is the positive frame and makes sense alone. The epigraph is about the drink, makes sense cold, and teases (why would wine improve a sour?). Tagline, epigraph and name share no word. The name *A Place Kept* echoes whoYouAre's "place" without spending the aim line.
- **Read as the guest:** whoYouAre lands without the story. The story shows a man who minded and went on. The drink makes one thing visible, and the proposal asks for exactly that, and gives back the thanks they're owed (Robin: end on what the guest gains).
