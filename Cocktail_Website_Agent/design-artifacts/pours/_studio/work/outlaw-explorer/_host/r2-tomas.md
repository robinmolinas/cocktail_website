What I'd put in the glass instead is your point 4, the danger turned into a joke. Cynar is an Italian bitter made with artichoke (Oxford, FLOC DE GASCOGNE pdf 822). The *Codex* (p. 260) says it "has a reputation as being extremely bitter", and then that it sits in the middle of the amari. In the *Flavor Matrix*'s artichoke entry (pdf 32), the surprise pairing is plum. The sloe is the plum's wild cousin: blackthorn, *Prunus spinosa* (Oxford, PRUNELLE pdf 1573). The Matrix entry is about the vegetable, so we'd say "artichoke and plum", never that Cynar itself was tested. Artichoke in a cocktail sounds like a dare, and it turns out to be what makes the drink. That's your guest turning people's fear into a laugh. You rule on the risk: it could read as "the danger was smaller than it looked", and that would shrink their choice. If you strike it, the fallback comes from the books: the 1890s Blackthorn Sour, made with sloe gin, pineapple syrup and a splash of apricot liqueur (*Imbibe!* pdf 96).

These numbers are provisional, so they're not a spec yet. Sloe gin turns out to be the syrup. A normal sloe sour with sugar goes OUT: 30 ml sloe gin, 30 ml gin, 22.5 ml lemon and 15 ml syrup finishes at 10.55 g sugar and 1.01% acid, against limits of 8.9 g and 0.94%. With 45 ml sloe gin, 30 ml London dry gin at 47%, 20 ml lemon, 7.5 ml Cynar and no syrup, shaken and served in a chilled coupe, it finishes at 17.0% / 6.70 g / 0.895%. Every number is in range on `shaken`. With a 40% gin it needs 5 ml of syrup to stay above the strength floor (15.2%). The sloe gin's sugar is my own back-calculation from *Liquid Intelligence*'s Blackthorn (p. 131), about 20 g/100 ml.
