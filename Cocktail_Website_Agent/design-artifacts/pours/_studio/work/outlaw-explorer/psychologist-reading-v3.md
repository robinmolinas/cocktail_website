## whoYouAre
The cold lake in March. The rope swing nobody else will try. The plan that sounded mad at dinner and is happening by morning. Your friends groan. Then they come, and it turns into the night they're still telling people about years later.

People think you're chasing the rush. It's plainer than that. In the second you let go, nobody is in charge of you: not the sensible voice, not whoever insists you need a course first, not the life that gets planned for you a little more each year. The drop is the one kind of helpless you choose. The other kind, padded and polite, where everything's decided and nothing's yours, frightens you more than any height. Said aloud, that would come out as a complaint, and you'd rather laugh.

And the laugh is what you give. You take what scares everyone and make it a game they can all be in. Around you, people remember they're allowed to feel alive. That's no small thing to give a person.

## yours
On 1 April 1979, four friends from an Oxford club that called itself the Dangerous Sports Club gathered on the Clifton Suspension Bridge in Bristol, high over the river, and stepped off on elastic rope. Theirs were the first recorded bungee jumps. One of them, David Kirke, in top hat and tails, had covered his face so that his mother wouldn't recognise him in the papers next morning.

The rope hadn't been tested. Clever friends had worked out how far it would stretch, and Kirke said later that for a club with that name, testing it first "wouldn't have been particularly dangerous". What went through his mind as he went over, he said, was "Whoooppeeee". The police had at first taken it for an April Fool's joke. By Kirke's account, the police were very good-humoured about it. They arrested all four, and let them go with a small fine, on the understanding they'd never do it again. Six months later, Kirke and others from the club jumped from the Golden Gate Bridge.

A journalist who met Kirke wrote that the printed invitations had already gone out, so there was no backing out. Who posted them, we don't know. I like to think someone did it on purpose, closing the way back before anyone else could close it for them. That's you: not the jump, the invitations. Kirke once said the club was against "little, important bourgeois instructors" telling people they had to pass exams before they were allowed to try a sport. Not the danger. Who gets to decide. And years later he said he was glad his stunts had given people some fun.

The cocktail I've made for you is a sour, one of the great classic shapes: sharp with lemon, sweet just enough. It starts from sloe gin, made by steeping sloes, small wild cousins of the plum, in gin with sugar. That turns it red and leaves it sweet already, so I add no syrup. I shake it hard with London dry gin and fresh lemon, and pour it into a coupe, chilled first. Then the surprise: a small measure of Cynar, an Italian bitter with artichoke among its many flavourings, and a reputation for being extremely bitter. A cookbook of surprising pairings puts cooked artichoke with plum, and here it gives the sweet sloe the bitter edge it needs. I put it in for you: you're the one who turns what makes people flinch into the part of the night everyone remembers.

So I'd never tell you to stop jumping. I'd point you at a bigger drop. It isn't off a bridge. It's the plan for your life that somebody sensible drew up, a little at a time, that you've half stopped noticing you never chose. You don't have to tear it up. Take one piece of it back, the way you'd step off an edge: decide, tell people, send the invitations, so there's no backing out. Kirke said life is very long without those brief moments. I'd add one thing. The long part can be yours too.

## Epigraph ruling (v2, amended v3 per Hester)
"There's artichoke in this glass" stays: artichoke is one of Cynar's flavourings (Codex p. 260) and Oxford calls Cynar artichoke-based (pdf 822), so the sentence is literally true. It claims presence, not that artichoke makes the drink. "Until they taste it" is about the drink as a whole.
