## whoYouAre

Someone you look up to tries something you've worked hard on and says, "Not quite." Nothing else. You smile, say you'll have another go, and that night you're still turning it over, long after they've forgotten they said it. You'd never admit how much one sentence from the right person can move you. Or why: you're sure there's more in you than anyone has seen so far, and you don't have the proof yet.

What you dread is the day it's for real: being asked for it properly, in front of the very person you wanted to impress, and having nothing to show. So you listen to people who know more as if every word might be the key. It's also your gift to them. People love teaching you, because you take a hard word home and come back with something. But part of you is still waiting for them to hand you the answer. Some answers nobody can hand you.

## yours

1. In the mid-eighties, a legendary New York restaurateur named Joe Baum was opening a new restaurant, Aurora, and he tried out a bartender called Dale DeGroff to run its cocktail bar. DeGroff had spent six years behind a hotel bar in Los Angeles, teaching himself about spirits and cocktails; the way one book tells it, so he could keep a good job he knew he wasn't qualified for. His customers may have noticed. The world hadn't.

2. Baum rarely explained himself. He mostly told DeGroff whether he was going the right way or the wrong way. "Joe never told me anything," DeGroff said. One day Baum tasted his beef-broth drink and said: "There's something wrong with it. Fix it." That was all. DeGroff couldn't work out what. Baum also wanted fresh juice behind the bar, never a ready-made mix, and when DeGroff asked what they'd do if it got busy, Baum said that if he couldn't handle it, he'd find someone who could.

3. The answer turned up somewhere else entirely: at a Chinese restaurant, where DeGroff saw beef with orange on the menu. He took the lemon and lime out of his drink and put orange in, and Baum tasted it and said OK. Baum never told him. I like to think he couldn't have: he could taste that something was wrong, but the answer had to be DeGroff's, and it stayed his. More than thirty years later, the orange is still in the recipe DeGroff gives for that drink. You treat the people you learn from as if they're holding the answer and deciding when to let you have it. The answers that make you who you are come from somewhere nobody pointed you, and they're yours for good.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is built on that drink, the Bullshot: vodka shaken with beef broth, an old steakhouse favourite that most people have never tried. It was once the drink of advertising men and film stars who wanted something different, and one drinks historian calls it surprisingly good when it's made with care. So I make the broth at home the day before, slowly and plainly, then shake it cold with the vodka, a splash of medium dry sherry, a little salt and a little fresh orange juice, and squeeze an orange peel over it at the end. I left out his hot sauce and pepper, and there's no lemon or lime anywhere in it. I kept both touches of orange, because the orange is the answer he found for himself. It comes out a warm amber, savoury and deep, and the orange lifts it at the end.

5. So when someone whose opinion matters to you next says "not quite" and stops there, don't hold out for an explanation. Or for them to tell you you're good enough. Take it home, and let the answer come from wherever it turns up: a conversation on a train, a shop window, something you read for another reason. When you bring it back, it'll be yours, and so will everything you build on it. The things people end up knowing you for will be the ones nobody handed you.
