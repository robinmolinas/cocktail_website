# Pour — Anyway · The Morale Booster (jester-caregiver)

## Cocktail

- **name:** Anyway
- **tagline:** You decide it's going to be a good night. Then you make it one.
- **glassware:** stemmed wine glass (about 250 ml), chilled
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 ml (2 oz) | London dry gin, 43% or stronger | coriander is one of the usual botanicals in a London dry gin |
| 30 ml (1 oz) | sweet vermouth (Italian style) | more of the sweet than Oxford's recipe: it puts back the sugar a pour of orange juice would bring |
| 1 teaspoon (5 ml) | dry vermouth | |
| 1 thin wheel | fresh orange, about ½ cm thick, cut into eight pieces, peel and all | the peel is where most of an orange's flavour is; the thin wheel gives only a splash of juice |
| ¼ teaspoon | coriander seeds, lightly cracked | one of the usual flavourings in London dry gin, with a lemony note of its own; strained out before serving |
| 2 dashes | Peychaud's bitters, recommended (or another bright-red New Orleans-style aromatic bitters) | the Sazerac's bitters, for the guests who'd rather have had a Sazerac |

**method**
1. Put the wine glass in the freezer for ten minutes.
2. Cut a thin wheel from an orange, about half a centimetre, and cut it into eight pieces. Leave the peel on.
3. Put the orange pieces and the coriander seeds in a shaker. Press them firmly with a muddler (or the end of a rolling pin) until the peel smells strongly of orange and the juice runs.
4. Add the gin, both vermouths and the bitters. Fill the shaker with ice and shake hard for about ten seconds.
5. Pour it through the shaker's strainer and a small tea strainer held over the glass, so no seeds or pulp get in.
6. Nothing on the rim. The orange is already in it.

**closingLine:** *Muddle the whole wheel, peel and all. Then tell the story of the one that didn't take.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*People tried everything to fix the orange in the Bronx cocktail. This is one more try.*

**whoYouAre**
It's a grey week, or a long wait, or a birthday nobody's quite in the mood for, and you've decided it's going to be good anyway. So you think of something: a silly game with proper rules, a theme, a time to turn up. Half the fun is that you took it seriously. People who would never start anything themselves find they've joined in, and even the one who swore they wouldn't ends up laughing.

There's a list, though, and only you have it: the ones that didn't take. The quiz nobody got into. The evening that stayed flat however hard you pushed it. Everyone else forgot them by the next morning. You didn't, because under all the fun is the thing you dread most: a gloom nobody could lift, and you the one who couldn't lift it. Here's what those nights gave people, even the flat ones. They could tell that someone wanted the evening to be good for them. That's what your fun was for all along, and it matters more than whether the game worked.

**yours**

1. Early in 1917, on a Sunday afternoon in St. Louis, Clara Bell Walsh had about fifty guests over for something new: an hour of cocktails, from noon until one. A newspaper at the time gave her the credit for the idea. It's the first party on record that was planned as a cocktail party and called one.

2. It wasn't her first idea. She'd tried other party themes before it, and in one of them the guests dressed up as babies and drank from bottles. That one didn't catch on. The cocktail party did, and more than a century later it's still one of the ways people get together. I'd have gone to both.

3. It's the cocktail party people remember. But it might never have come without the ones before it, and I like to think she knew it. It's the same with the list you keep. The flat evenings on it aren't the opposite of the good ones. They're how you learned what people will join in with, and what they won't. And among the drinks that Sunday there were a few Sazeracs, served to the older guests who were set in their ways. I like to think that was someone making sure even the hardest ones to win over had something there for them. That's you, too.

4. The cocktail I've made for you starts from the sensation of her day, the Bronx: gin, two kinds of vermouth and orange. It was on her menu, and it was famously hard to get right. Put in enough orange juice to taste it, and the drink goes weak, so people kept trying new answers. The version I start from doesn't pour juice at all: one thin slice of orange is crushed in the shaker, peel and all, because the peel is where most of an orange's flavour is. You can taste the orange without drowning the gin. Then I added mine. A pinch of cracked coriander seeds goes in with the orange: coriander is one of the usual flavourings in London dry gin, with a lemony note of its own. And in place of the Angostura there are two dashes of Peychaud's, the Sazerac's bitters, for the guests who'd rather have had a Sazerac.

5. So keep the list. Just stop reading it as a record of what went wrong. Every idea on it that didn't take is part of the one that will. When something you've planned falls flat, laugh about it with them, and start planning another. Throw it anyway. I'd come to both.

### Names considered

**Anyway**, the room's pick (all three, round 5): the word the person lives by ("Throw it anyway"), said in the reading before the glass says it; short across a bar ("An Anyway, please"). **The One That Took** (Wren's first) was set aside by Wren and Tomás because on a menu it reads as a claim that the drink fixed the Bronx, which Wren's own condition forbids and the record doesn't support (*Imbibe!* pdf 237). Also considered: *Another Go*, *Noon Till One* (Tomás), *Here's the Plan*, *Game for It* (Wren).

- ***Anyway*** (my pick, Wren's second). It's the word the person lives by: they decided it's going to be good anyway, and after the one that didn't take, they throw the next one anyway. Across a bar it's short and a little mysterious, and it makes the bartender ask ("Anyway?"), which is a party trick of its own. It doesn't repeat the tagline ("You decide… Then you make it one"). The reading says it, so the name recognises and the closing line turns (MEMORY). Registry: no "Anyway".
- ***The One That Took*** (Wren's). It's good on the person, but condition (1) says the drink is my try, never the one that took. On a menu, a name is read as a claim about the drink. That's my case against it as the pick.
- ***Another Go***: plain, warm, the position in two words. It's weaker because "go" sounds like a round of a game, a bit small.
- ***Noon Till One***: the party's own shape, and a nice thing to order. But it captions the story rather than the person.
- Wren's *Here's the Plan* and *Game for It* are on the record.
