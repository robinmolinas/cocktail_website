## whoYouAre

It's a grey week, or a long wait, or a birthday nobody's quite in the mood for, and you've decided it's going to be good anyway. So you think of something: a silly game with proper rules, a theme, a time to turn up. Half the fun is that you took it seriously. People who would never start anything themselves find they've joined in, and even the one who swore they wouldn't ends up laughing.

There's a list, though, and only you have it: the ones that didn't take. The quiz nobody got into. The evening that stayed flat however hard you pushed it. Everyone else forgot them by the next morning. You didn't, because under all the fun is the thing you dread most: a gloom nobody could lift, and you the one who couldn't lift it. Here's what those nights gave people, even the flat ones. They could tell that someone wanted the evening to be good for them. That's what your fun was for all along, and it matters more than whether the game worked.

## yours

1. Early in 1917, on a Sunday afternoon in St. Louis, Clara Bell Walsh had about fifty guests over for something new: an hour of cocktails, from noon until one. A newspaper at the time gave her the credit for the idea. It's the first party on record that was planned as a cocktail party and called one.

2. It wasn't her first idea. She'd tried other party themes before it, and in one of them the guests dressed up as babies and drank from bottles. That one didn't catch on. The cocktail party did, and more than a century later it's still one of the ways people get together. I'd have gone to both.

3. Nobody tells the story of the baby party. But the second idea might never have come without the first, and I like to think she knew it. It's the same with the list you keep. The flat evenings on it aren't the opposite of the good ones. They're how you learned what people will join in with, and what they won't. And among the drinks that Sunday there were a few Sazeracs, served to the older guests who were set in their ways. I like to think that was someone making sure even the hardest ones to win over had something there for them. That's you, too.

4. The cocktail I've made for you starts from the sensation of her day, the Bronx: gin, two kinds of vermouth and orange. It was on her menu, and it was famously hard to get right. Put in enough orange juice to taste it, and the drink goes weak, so people tried every trick they could think of. This is mine. Most of the orange goes in as peel, shaken hard with a few cracked coriander seeds, because the peel is where an orange keeps its flavour, and coriander, already in most gins, tastes faintly of citrus. Only a little juice goes in. So it tastes of orange and stays bright and strong. And in place of the usual bitters there's a dash of Peychaud's, the Sazerac's bitters, for the guests who'd rather have had a Sazerac.

5. So keep the list. Just stop reading it as a record of what went wrong. Every idea on it that didn't take is part of the one that will. When something you've planned falls flat, let it be the story you all laugh about later, and start on the next one. Throw it anyway. I'd come to both.
