## Recipe

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 ml (2 oz) | London dry gin, 43% or stronger | coriander is one of the usual botanicals in a London dry gin |
| 30 ml (1 oz) | sweet vermouth (Italian style) | more of the sweet than Oxford's recipe: it puts back the sugar a pour of orange juice would bring |
| 1 teaspoon (5 ml) | dry vermouth | |
| 1 thin wheel | fresh orange, about ½ cm thick, cut into eight pieces, peel and all | the peel is where most of an orange's flavour is; the thin wheel gives only a splash of juice |
| ¼ teaspoon | coriander seeds, lightly cracked | one of the usual flavourings in London dry gin, with a lemony note of its own; strained out before serving |
| 2 dashes | Peychaud's bitters, recommended (or another bright-red New Orleans-style aromatic bitters) | the Sazerac's bitters, for the guests who'd rather have had a Sazerac |

## Method

1. Put the wine glass in the freezer for ten minutes.
2. Cut a thin wheel from an orange, about half a centimetre, and cut it into eight pieces. Leave the peel on.
3. Put the orange pieces and the coriander seeds in a shaker. Press them firmly with a muddler (or the end of a rolling pin) until the peel smells strongly of orange and the juice runs.
4. Add the gin, both vermouths and the bitters. Fill the shaker with ice and shake hard for about ten seconds.
5. Pour it through the shaker's strainer and a small tea strainer held over the glass, so no seeds or pulp get in.
6. Nothing on the rim. The orange is already in it.

**closingLine:** *Muddle the whole wheel, peel and all. Then tell the story of the one that didn't take.*

## For Wren: y4 against the spec (v1 asks, round 4; see the round-5 section for v2)
- "Most of the orange goes in as peel, shaken hard with a few cracked coriander seeds" → the method muddles **one thin orange wheel, peel and all**, with the seeds, then shakes. Suggest: "The orange goes in as one thin wheel, peel and all, pressed with a few cracked coriander seeds, because the peel is where an orange keeps its flavour."
- "coriander, already in most gins" → Hester's wording: "coriander, one of the usual botanicals in a London dry gin, which tastes faintly of citrus". The "tastes faintly of citrus" is the *Matrix*'s listing it as citrus's substitute (pdf 80). If you want it exact: "which the cookbooks list as a stand-in for citrus".
- "Only a little juice goes in" → true: only what the thin wheel gives, no juice poured.
- **"stays bright and strong": please strike "strong".** At 20.2% it's a light drink, at the bottom edge of Arnold's range, and Oxford calls a balanced Bronx "light and pleasant". Suggest: "So you can taste the orange without drowning the gin." That's true to the numbers: no poured juice, strength kept at the edge rather than under it.
- "in place of the usual bitters" → the page's recipe uses Angostura: "in place of the Angostura" or keep "the usual bitters". Both are true.
- If a sweetness word is wanted: it sits at the bottom of its band (3.71 g). Say "dry-ish", never "sweet".

## Names

- ***Anyway*** (my pick, Wren's second). It's the word the person lives by: they decided it's going to be good anyway, and after the one that didn't take, they throw the next one anyway. Across a bar it's short and a little mysterious, and it makes the bartender ask ("Anyway?"), which is a party trick of its own. It doesn't repeat the tagline ("You decide… Then you make it one"). The reading says it, so the name recognises and the closing line turns (MEMORY). Registry: no "Anyway".
- ***The One That Took*** (Wren's). It's good on the person, but condition (1) says the drink is my try, never the one that took. On a menu, a name is read as a claim about the drink. That's my case against it as the pick.
- ***Another Go***: plain, warm, the position in two words. It's weaker because "go" sounds like a round of a game, a bit small.
- ***Noon Till One***: the party's own shape, and a nice thing to order. But it captions the story rather than the person.
- Wren's *Here's the Plan* and *Game for It* are on the record.
