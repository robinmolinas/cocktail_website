## whoYouAre

It's a birthday, and you've been asked to say something short. You do: one sentence, read slowly from a very long sheet of paper. There's a second where nobody knows whether they're allowed to laugh. You let it run a little longer than is comfortable. Then it goes, and it's a laugh nobody planned, not even you. The evening where everyone laughs on cue, at the punchline they could see coming from the door, bores you to tears.

Here's the part that doesn't show: you're watching every face. You'd never explain one of your jokes, because explaining feels like taking it apart while everyone watches. So when one doesn't land, you keep a straight face and take it home with you. What you'd least like to hear is that it came across as cold, that someone thought it was on them, or felt left outside it. It almost never is. And it's a rare thing you give people: a real reaction instead of a polite one. For a second nobody has to know how to feel, and the night everyone's still describing weeks later is usually one you were at.

## yours

1. In the spring of 2011, a bar opened in Chicago with no bar in it. There was no counter to lean on: the drinks came to the table, from stations at the back that looked more like a laboratory. The chef behind it, Grant Achatz, said they wanted to mix the newest techniques with tradition in a way they thought hadn't been done. One writer picks out an Old-Fashioned as perhaps the finest: the most expected cocktail there is, sealed inside a ball of ice, set in a rocks glass. At the table the ice was cracked, and the drink ran out over it.

2. Chicago's cocktail crowd didn't warm to it at first. A critic that first spring watched the server crack the ice and counted it among the drinks played, quietly, for laughs. The same review found the drinks could feel a little cold, because nobody had time to talk about them. Funny and cold, in the same week. In the summer of 2012 Charles Joly took over the drinks, and he said later it hadn't felt like a bar: it lacked a sense of hospitality. He didn't make the drinks ordinary. They stayed every bit as strange, but now a bartender came with them, to joke and to talk. It had to be, in his words, about "hospitality, the booze and a good time."

3. I like to think that critic put a finger on exactly the thing you worry about. The strange part was never the problem. The joke was there from the first week, and somebody laughed. What was missing was someone staying in the room with it. You know that second better than anyone, the one where people don't yet know whether they're allowed to laugh. It's a better second with someone beside them in it.

4. The cocktail I've made for you brings that idea home. Inside is the renowned classic, a rye Old-Fashioned: rye, demerara sugar, Angostura bitters and orange peel. It's a drink with as many recipes as bartenders, and the whole craft of it is restraint, so it's made exactly. It waits, cold and already diluted, in a shell of ordinary cloudy ice from your own freezer. A home freezer can't freeze a drink that strong, so plain ice holds it like a cup. Whoever made it cracks the shell at the table with one tap of a spoon, while whoever it's for looks on, and the drink runs out over its own broken ice. Nothing in the bottle is strange. The joke is all in how it arrives, which, if you ask me, is you exactly.

5. So keep every strange thing you had planned. Don't explain it beforehand, and don't make it sensible. Just don't disappear straight after. Stay for the second that follows, and for the conversation after that. Nobody needed the joke taken apart. They needed someone in it with them. The ones who weren't sure the first time are the ones who'll come back for whatever you try next.
