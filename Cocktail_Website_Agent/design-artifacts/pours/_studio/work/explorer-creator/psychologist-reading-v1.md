## Reading

**epigraph**
*The best idea behind this glass was found in a convenience store.*

**whoYouAre**
Somewhere in your life there's a thing that used to jam, drip or stick, until one afternoon you'd had enough of it. You happily spent a weekend on it so you'd never have to spend ten minutes on it again. And you regret none of it.

What you keep quiet is how it starts. It sounds like moaning: the queue that's slow for no reason, the form that asks for the same thing twice, the way something has always been done that has never quite worked. You've tried sighing and getting on with it. It doesn't take. It stays with you until you've found a way round, and the way round usually comes from somewhere nobody thought to look. The sentence you can't bear is "it's always been like that". The one that frightens you is hearing yourself say it, and meaning it. And the things you fix stay fixed. People stop having the problem, forget it was ever there, and get their afternoons back. That's what your restlessness gives them, and it's worth far more than the grumbling it started with.

**yours**

1. Some bars were already making the Margarita in a blender by the early 1960s. About ten years later, in Dallas, Mariano Martinez was making frozen ones at his own restaurant, and they weren't working. They came out different every time, and not even cold, his customers complained. His bartenders were threatening to quit. The restaurant was burning out blenders, he said later, as fast as it could buy them.
2. The answer wasn't in a bar. It was at a 7-Eleven, in the Slurpee machine. He took a soft-serve ice cream machine and bent it to the job: through trial and error, as he tells it, they made it spin faster and gave it a bigger compressor, so the drinks came out colder. Within a few years the frozen Margarita machine was a fixture of the age, and many versions came onto the market. The original is in the Smithsonian now. He never meant to invent anything, he said. He was just trying to make his customers happy.
3. I like to think the complaints bothered him long before any machine did. You'd know that feeling. You hear the grumble everyone hears, and you're the one who can't let it go. Then you find the fix somewhere nobody thought to look: a restaurant's answer, sitting in a convenience store. And once it works, you're happy for it to disappear. Almost nobody ordering a frozen Margarita today thinks about Dallas, or burned-out blenders. They just get a cold drink. That's how you look after people: they never have to find out there was a problem.
4. The cocktail I've made for you is a frozen Margarita, one of the most famous mixed drinks there is, with blanco tequila and Cointreau, the orange liqueur Martinez's father always insisted on. It's built the way you'd build it: all the work happens once. A day ahead, you mix the whole batch, water and all, and put it in the freezer. By coincidence, the book I took the method from describes the method as making a 7-Eleven-style frozen treat. After that, every glass is a scoop of the frozen mix and a squeeze of lime, then a few seconds in the blender. There's no ice to guess at and nothing melting into it, so it comes out the same, and properly cold, every time. 5. So trust the annoyance. It isn't moaning, and it isn't ingratitude. It's usually the first sign that you've found something worth fixing. The next time someone tells you it's always been like that, take it as directions: they've just shown you where to start. And when it works and nobody says a word, enjoy the quiet. You made it.
