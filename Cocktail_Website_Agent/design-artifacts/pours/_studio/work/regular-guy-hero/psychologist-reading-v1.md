## whoYouAre

Someone says it in the pub, on the doorstep or in the group chat: somebody really ought to do something about it. The broken bench, the club with no treasurer, the school play nobody will run. Everyone nods. Before you can stop yourself, you've said "I'll do it", and only later, doing the washing-up, do you realise you've no idea how.

Nobody hears about the evenings: the videos, the forums, the cousin who might know. You're out of your depth most of the way through, and you know it. The words you dread are "who asked you?", and worse, the doubters turning out to be right. You go anyway, partly because if you thought about it any longer, you wouldn't. This is what it gives. The thing everyone was waiting for somebody to fix gets fixed. And because you so plainly need a hand, people who'd never have volunteered end up holding the ladder. It counts for more than you know: what was everyone's complaint becomes something they did together.

## yours

1. For about twenty-five years, climbers like Ralph Erenzo on New York's Shawangunk Ridge had camped with no toilets and no running water. So in 2001 he bought an old mill near the ridge to give them a proper campground. The neighbours objected. One of them, as Erenzo remembers it, told him the aim was to stall until his money ran out.

2. When the campground stalled, the town's zoning officer mentioned that a winery counts as farming. So Erenzo and Brian Lee, a broadcast engineer, set out to make spirits instead. Neither had any idea how. Lee joked that the only fermenting he'd ever done was cinnamon buns with his children. By Erenzo's count it took about two and a half years, from knowing nothing, to switching the still on and making alcohol. He said later that he worked from instructions he found online. And when they needed more hands, he called his son, Gable: "I'm building a distillery – I need your help!"

3. Their first bottles held a vodka made from New York apples. Later came their whiskeys, among them a rye from New York grain. By 2015 the state counted more than a hundred craft distilleries, and its agriculture commissioner called Tuthilltown a model for producers across the state. I like to think the best thing Erenzo made there wasn't in a bottle. It was that phone call: what he was about to try, and that he couldn't do it alone, in a single sentence, long before he knew how. You always say the first half. It's the second half you swallow.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Manhattan, one of the great classics: rye stirred with sweet vermouth (a red, wine-based aperitif with herbs) and bitters, then poured over a single large ice cube, and an orange peel pinched over it so its oils land on the drink. It's built for Hudson's rye, the one that began in that mill, if you can find it. Beside the rye there's a little apple brandy, as a nod to the apples they started with. It isn't their vodka. It's the early attempt kept next to the thing they got good at, and rye and apple brandy are famously good together. It comes out deep and warm, a little sweet, and brighter for the apple.

5. So, when "I'll do it" next comes out of your mouth, finish the sentence: "and I'll need a hand." Say it at the start, while you still don't know how, and say who you'd like. You worry it'll show how out of your depth you are. It will, a little. It's also the reason they'll come. The job gets done sooner, nobody waits for the experts, and the thing you started ends up belonging to everyone who helped. I hope plenty of them say yes.

## Resonance

- **As the guest:** the "how did they know" line is the tagline, and in the reading it's "only on the way home do you realise you've no idea how". The private part is the evenings spent learning and "the doubters turning out to be right". The gift is said plainly: the thing gets fixed, and the complaint becomes something people did together. The position gives back speed, company, and a thing that belongs to everyone who helped. The last line is a warm wish.
- **As the sceptic:** "you say yes before you know how" isn't true of everyone. Plenty of people never volunteer, and that's on purpose. The examples (bench, treasurer, school play) are small and civic, so a guest who never founded anything still sees themselves. There's no praise of bravery, no "hero" and no violence (the press sense of the phrase never appears).
- **Siblings and neighbours:**

  - Not *Anyone Would Have*: this person never practised. No "anyone can".
  - Not *Fine by Me*: the zoning officer's remark is one clause, and the mirror is the amateurs, not the opening.
  - Not *For Good*: a shared problem, no cleverness, and no "it's always been like that".
