## whoYouAre

When someone you love is having a hard time, you rarely know what to say. You know what to make: a meal from scratch, a card you started three times, a present you were still finishing at two in the morning because one small thing wasn't right yet. People call it perfectionism. It isn't. To you, a shortcut says "you weren't worth the time". So you never take one.

What they're really holding is proof that someone looked closely at them. What you rarely let anyone see is what it costs you: the evenings you vanish into it, sometimes away from the very person it's for. And the quiet hope, each time you hand it over, that they'll understand what it was saying.

## yours

1. In the 1920s, the most famous woman bartender in Britain was Ada Coleman, known as Coley, at the Savoy in London (Oxford pdf 554; F3). She didn't start behind a bar. She began at another London hotel, in its flower shop, making buttonholes for gentlemen to wear (F1). Small things, made by hand, for someone else.

2. One of her regulars was the actor Charles Hawtrey. He came in once worn out from overwork and asked her to make him "quite alive" again (F4). By her own account, she spent hours experimenting until she had something new for him (F5): gin, sweet vermouth and a little Fernet-Branca, a dark, bitter Italian digestif, the kind once sold in pharmacies (F6, F12). It became the Hanky Panky.

3. Coley made drinks at the Savoy for more than twenty years (F3, F11). Of everything she made, the only drink of hers known to survive is the one she made for one tired man (F8). That's a coincidence, and I won't pretend it's more. But I like to think she understood what you understand: you can't really tell someone "I can see how tired you are". You can make them something, take the time it takes, and let it say that for you.

4. So yours is her drink, made to measure: gin, sweet vermouth and a spoonful of Fernet, stirred with ice until it's very cold. In a small dose, the Fernet gives it depth without making it plainly bitter (F9). Then you taste a drop off the spoon, and look up at the person it's for before you decide whether they need a little more.

5. One thing, though. You do your best work alone, out of sight, and hand it over finished. Next time you make something for someone, this or anything else, don't vanish to do it. Make it where they can see you. Let them watch you taste, look up, and add a little more because of the day they've had. They've always had what you made. Let them have you, making it.
