# Mirror hunt: The Sculptor (creator-caregiver)

Hester, round 1. The person: painstaking, methodical, in love with transformation; the work is care ("make the world more beautiful, one creation at a time"). Fears mediocrity and indifference. The turn I hunted for: someone who made a beautiful thing slowly, *for someone else*, and the thing came alive.

## Candidate A (my pick): a person, Ada "Coley" Coleman, and the drink she made for one man
**Story.** Coleman began at Claridge's making buttonholes in the flower shop before she was moved behind the bar; from 1903 she worked the Savoy's American Bar, where customers took her for the head bartender. One of her regulars, the actor Charles Hawtrey, came in worn out and asked her, more or less, to make him alive again. She said she spent hours experimenting until she had something new for him: gin, sweet vermouth and a whisper of Fernet-Branca, the Hanky Panky, named (in 1920s slang) for a conjuring trick.

**Why it mirrors them.** Creator and caregiver in one act: the invention exists because one tired person needed it, and the patience is on her own record ("I spent hours experimenting"). The sculptor's wish, to make material come alive, is almost Hawtrey's own request. Fear of indifference: she didn't pour him the usual.

**Sources.** Fact card `_studio/fact-cards/hanky-panky.md` (F1–F11): Oxford COLEMAN, ADA (pdf 554), HANKY PANKY (pdf 966, quoting the *People* 1925 and the *Sphere* 1926), SAVOY HOTEL'S AMERICAN BAR (pdf 1745–1746); *Joy*, pdf 299; *A Proper Drink*, p. 252.

**Risks.** The famous "By Jove!" line is one of two versions (C3): leave it out or label it. The year is a likelihood (C4). Proportions vary (C2). Her motive beyond the hours is not on record: "because she cared for him" is our reading, signposted. Martini family, used once before (sage-lover); menu view only.

## Candidate B (runner-up): an ingredient-technique, hand-carved ice
**Story.** Before ice machines, every American bartender cut the drink's ice by hand from blocks; some carved perfect cubes so every guest got the same. After Prohibition the craft died in America but lived on in Tokyo, where bartenders kept carving ice to fit the glass, and it came back to New York through Angel's Share and Milk & Honey.

**Why it mirrors them.** Sculpture as service: a form carved for one glass that gives itself away as it melts. The keeping of a painstaking art nobody else bothered with.

**Sources.** Oxford ICE CARVING IN A BAR CONTEXT (pdf 1058–1060); *Imbibe!*, pdf 188.

**Risks.** No single person or turn; it's a craft, not a story. Better as Tomás's gesture in the glass than as the reading's history. No fact card yet (write one if the room uses it).

## My call
A. The person is the mirror, and the hours are on the record. B can live in the glass if Tomás wants it.

## Round 2: Tomás's question, Rodin's marble (checked, kept out)
- **Fact (Musée Rodin, the museum's own site, tier: institutional/secondary):** Rodin did not carve his marbles; *The Kiss* was carved by specialised collaborators (praticiens) under his direction. Commissioned by the French state in 1888, carved 1888–1898. Sources: musee-rodin.fr, "Dans l'atelier : les marbres d'Auguste Rodin" (agenda page); collection page "The Kiss" (musee-rodin.fr/en/musee/collections/oeuvres/kiss).
- **Unconfirmed:** the museum's collection database lists Jean Turcan as praticien on *Le Baiser* (search listing only; the record returned 404, so I haven't read it). Don't name him in anything the guest sees.
- **Why it stays out:** (1) Rodin isn't part of any drink's history, and the mirror has to be a drink, an ingredient or a person in a drink's life. (2) Marble and chisels are exactly the literal register Wren ruled out. (3) Its meaning (a famous name, with the hours done by uncredited hands) is the handover and the hired shakermen from *Down the Line*: the Visionary's ground. (4) The persona card names *Le baiser* as the Sculptor's example, and "the master didn't carve it" argues against the person, who does the hours themselves.
