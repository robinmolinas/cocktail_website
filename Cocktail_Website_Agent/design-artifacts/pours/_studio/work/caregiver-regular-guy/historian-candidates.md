# Mirror-Hunt Candidates: The Nurse (caregiver-regular-guy)

## Chosen: Joe Sheridan and the Irish Coffee (Foynes/Shannon, 1944–45)

**The story:** Joe Sheridan (1909–1962) was a chef — not a bartender, not a celebrity — at the flying-boat station at Foynes, Ireland. In late 1944 or early 1945 he was asked to make something special to greet a group of dignitaries touring the facilities. Oxford notes the best-known mixed drink from Ireland or Scotland "was invented not at a smart hotel bar but by a hungover chef at a rural airport" (Oxford IRISH COFFEE, pdf 1769). He made hot coffee, Irish whisky, sugar, and a head of whipped cream. By 1947 it was called "Gaelic Coffee" and offered to *all travellers* deplaning at Shannon — not just dignitaries, everyone. In 1953, travel writer Stanton Delaplane tasted it and brought it to Jack Koeppler at the Buena Vista Café in San Francisco; they reverse-engineered it. By 1955 the Buena Vista was going through 36 bottles of Irish whisky a day. The drink spread, was imitated, degraded (syrup for sugar, bigger glasses, canned cream), and later revived.

**Why this person:** Sheridan is the Regular Guy who caregivers most: a working chef at an airport, not glamorous, possibly hungover. He made something warm and welcoming for tired strangers arriving in the cold. He did it without fanfare. His name isn't in the drink's name — it's "Irish Coffee," not "Sheridan's." The drink did what The Nurse does: made the hard thing bearable, then became the first thing people felt when they arrived.

**Mirror for The Nurse:** The Nurse's goal is Service; fear is Selfishness. The Regular Guy secondary is about belonging, community, doing your bit without needing recognition. Sheridan gave warmth to everyone — and then watched the world take his drink and, for a while, make it worse. That's the Nurse: you make it good, someone else makes it bigger, and you don't go to pieces over what they did with it.

**The drink:** Irish Coffee. Hot. Dairy (whipped cream). A flip-adjacent form — spirit, coffee, sugar, cream. Veto contains: dairy.

**Sources (all primary):**
- Oxford IRISH COFFEE pdf 1075–1077 (Sheridan, creation date, Foynes, Gaelic Coffee, Shannon, Buena Vista, Koeppler, Delaplane, degradation and revival)
- Oxford IRISH COFFEE pdf 1769 (the "hungover chef at a rural airport" line — same Oxford entry, referenced in the WHISKY section)

**Fact card:** `_studio/fact-cards/joe-sheridan-irish-coffee.md` (F1–F5, L1) — already verified.

**Caution:** In caregiver-creator, this was the runner-up and was withdrawn because Wren felt "a warm drink belongs to the whole caregiver family." That objection was specific to the mix of drinks being considered for caregiver-creator. Here, the Regular Guy secondary gives a sharper angle — the *ordinariness* of the person — that distinguishes it. I'd hold this under Wren's review. ## Runner-up: Lucinda Sterling / Penicillin (Milk & Honey, New York, 2005)

**The story:** Sam Ross, an Australian bartender at Milk & Honey, made the Penicillin in 2005 while playing with a Compass Box Scotch shipment. He riffed on the Gold Rush (honey-lemon-bourbon) with blended Scotch, ginger-honey syrup, lemon, and a float of peaty Islay Scotch. He went to bed not thinking much of it. Nine months later, waitress Lucinda Sterling came back from a table and said he should put a Penicillin on — "every table needs to experience one." That was the moment he realised what he had made. By 2007 Ross was consulting in Los Angeles and planting the drink in bars there. By 2014, bartenders assumed it was an old classic (Proper pp. 170–171, 278). Joy classifies it as a Simple Sour (daiquiri family).

**Why Lucinda:** The most Nurse-like person in this story isn't Sam Ross — it's Lucinda Sterling. She was a waitress, doing rounds, noticing what people felt, and she went back to tell the person who made it. She saw value before its creator did. She did the care work — the connecting — that made something great known.

**Alternative mirror:** The drink's name (Penicillin) invokes the antibiotic, which invokes Fleming, which invokes medicine and healing. But that angle is thin for a sourced anchor — the name is Sam Ross's choice, not a documented reference to care or service.

**The drink:** Simple Sour (daiquiri). Blended Scotch, lemon, honey-ginger syrup, Islay float. Rocks glass. Veto contains: veto-free.

**Sources (all primary/reliable secondary):**
- Proper pp. 170–171 (Ross's account of creating it; Lucinda Sterling; the nine months; LA consultancy)
- Joy pdf 363 (Simple Sour classification; Ross's account in his own words)
- Oxford CLASSIC COCKTAIL pdf 473 (named among candidates for eventual classic status)

**Why runner-up:** The Nurse's primary is Caregiver, and the warmth of the Irish Coffee *is the drink talking back* to the person. The Lucinda story is emotionally true but the mirror is slightly harder to make — the Nurse would have to see themselves in a waitress who noticed a great drink. That works, but it's more abstract.

## Discarded: Gin and Tonic (quinine/cinchona, colonial India, 1868+)

Strong medicinal origin — quinine for malaria, made palatable with gin and lime — but the story's caregiver actor is anonymous. The Oxford entry (pdf 898–899) traces the drink's origin to British soldiers in colonial India mixing gin with quinine water; no individual credited. The commercial tonic water patent (Erasmus Bond, 1858; Schweppe's, 1858) is about commerce, not care. Lauchlan Rose's cordial (already used in the Gimlet for caregiver-creator) was a separate development. The "medicine made drinkable" arc is emotionally right but has no person at the centre.
