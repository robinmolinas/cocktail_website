📜 **Hester:** Wren, I have two candidates — one strong, one worth knowing.

The stronger one: Joe Sheridan. He was a chef — not a bartender — at the flying-boat station at Foynes, Ireland. In late 1944 or early 1945 he was asked to make something special for visiting dignitaries, and he made what became Irish Coffee. By 1947 it was called "Gaelic Coffee" and offered to every traveller deplaning at Shannon, not just the VIPs. Oxford puts it plainly: the most famous mixed drink from Ireland or Scotland "was invented not at a smart hotel bar but at a rural airport" (Oxford IRISH COFFEE). His name isn't in the drink. He made something warm for tired strangers arriving in the cold, and then the world took it and, for a while, made it worse (syrup for sugar, canned cream, bigger glasses). It came back the right way.

The mirror for The Nurse: service without recognition, warmth for everyone, ordinariness turned into something that greeted every arrival. The Regular Guy secondary sharpens it — Sheridan is as Regular Guy as it gets. A hungover chef at an airport.

The thing I'd flag: in caregiver-creator, you withdrew this because a warm drink felt like the whole Caregiver family. That was your call and I didn't fight it. Here the Regular Guy shifts the frame — it's less about warmth as metaphor and more about the *person* who made it and the total lack of glamour in how it happened. I'd like your read on whether that distinction holds before Tomás picks up the glass.

Runner-up: Lucinda Sterling, a waitress at Milk & Honey in 2005. Sam Ross invented the Penicillin and went to bed not thinking much of it. Nine months later, Lucinda came back from a table and told him every table needed to experience one. She was the one who noticed, who went back to say so. The most Nurse-like person in that story is her, not Ross. If the Irish Coffee is too warm for you, this is where I'd go next.
