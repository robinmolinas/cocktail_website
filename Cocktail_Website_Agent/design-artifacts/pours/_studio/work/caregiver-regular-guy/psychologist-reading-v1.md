## whoYouAre

Something small goes wrong in a room full of people. Someone arrives soaked, a glass goes over, somebody's aunt doesn't know a soul. By the time anyone else has noticed, you're already there with a towel, a dustpan, a chair and a name. Nobody asked you to. Sitting there watching would have felt stranger.

And everyone gets the same you. The friend of twenty years and the new neighbour, the boss and the person clearing the plates: the same question about their week, the same cup, made the same way. Having favourites feels a bit selfish to you, a bit unfair, so you don't. What you'd rarely admit is that the people closest to you sometimes wait the longest, because you know they'll understand.

## yours

1. Late in 1944, or early in 1945, Joe Sheridan was the chef at Foynes, in Ireland, a station for flying boats, the planes that landed on water. He was asked to make something special to greet some important visitors. He made hot coffee with Irish whiskey and sugar, under a head of cream, in a small glass on a foot.

2. It started as a welcome for a few. It didn't stay that way. By 1947 it was being offered to every traveller getting off a plane at Shannon: not only the important ones. Everyone. Later it reached San Francisco, got famous, and fame did what fame does: syrup instead of sugar, bigger glasses, cream from a can. The bars that brought it back went back to making it the way Sheridan did.

3. I like to think that was the part that was really his. Not the recipe: that it went to everyone the same, the visitor with a title and whoever was next off the plane. I think you'd have done it exactly that way. You don't keep a better version for the people who matter. Whoever comes in gets the real one.

4. Yours is close to his: a small glass on a foot, sugar cubes and fresh cream, with a little less whiskey than today's usual recipe so the coffee under the cream stays gentle. Warm the glass with hot water first. Two cubes of brown sugar, the darker kind, because their toasted taste suits the coffee. Hot coffee, Irish whiskey, then cream whipped by hand until it only just holds, spooned on so it floats. Two cubes for everyone: one is too few, three too many. Nothing extra for the important guest. The only care in it is the kind nobody sees, the warm glass and the cream whipped fresh each time.

5. Don't change a thing about how you treat everyone. But being fair to everyone was never meant to mean that nobody gets to be special. Somewhere there's someone close to you who has been getting the same you as the new neighbour, and has never once complained. Give them something nobody else gets. Not more work: an evening, a call that isn't checking in, the good cup. The first of these drinks was made for a few particular guests. Let someone be yours.
