## whoYouAre

Somebody's keys are in the fruit bowl, under the bananas, and you're helping them look, very seriously. This morning the sugar was salt. Everyone calls it trouble, and they keep letting you in anyway.

Nobody spots the rules. Every trick fits one person, and everything can be put right in seconds. The salt goes back. The keys turn up before anyone's late. For every trick people see, there are a few you decided against, because of who'd be on the end of it. You're not afraid of being told off. You're afraid of the trick that lands wrong and becomes somebody's bad day. So you keep them small, and gone by teatime.

Here's your gift, and few people have it: a house where being surprised is safe. Around you, grown-ups still get to play.

## yours

1. In the 2000s, Brian Shebairo owned a rough-and-tumble hot-dog shop on St. Mark's Place in New York, and was turning the bubble-tea lounge next door into a bar. His first plan was a rock-and-roll bar. It became a cocktail bar instead, and the door to it was his idea: a phone booth, at the back of the hot-dog shop. "A little Get Smart," he called it, after the 1960s TV spy spoof whose hero steps into a phone booth and dials a number.

2. The bar opened in 2007. More than a decade later, that phone booth was still its door, and one writer calls it, with a careful "arguably", the most famous bar entrance in the world. It's the same bar where someone else's bacon-washed bourbon was born, but that's another story.

3. I like to think that's exactly the joke you'd make, and exactly how you'd make it: played completely straight. No sign, no wink, just a phone booth in a hot-dog shop, where a phone booth has every right to be. Nobody gets hurt. The worst that can happen is you open a door and find a bar. But there's one difference from yours. His wasn't put back by teatime. It stayed. And I like to think it went on catching somebody new on a quiet weeknight, years after he first thought of it, with nobody there to watch it land.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is built on the same joke. It's a highball: apple brandy, dry vermouth and a spoon of rich syrup, mixed in a bottle that waits, labelled, in the fridge, for about a month. Whenever someone wants one, they pour a little over ice in a plain tumbler, top it up with cold soda water, then squeeze a lemon peel over it. It looks like any pale, fizzy thing you'd find in a fridge. It tastes like a proper cocktail: dry and bright, apple first, like the best sparkling cider. Nothing in it is hidden, and the label says what it is. The only trick is that it's there, ready, long after whoever made it has gone out, as a nod to a phone booth that kept being a door.

5. Keep your rules. They're why people let you in. But make one that stays. Not a trick that's put right by teatime: a surprise built into something that lasts. A note in the pocket of a coat they won't wear until winter. A line written inside a book you lend. You won't be there when it lands, and that's the point. Months from now someone will find it, grin at nobody, and know exactly who it was. I hope you leave a few of those behind you.

## Resonance

**"This is me" line:** *"For every trick people see, there are a few you decided against, because of who'd be on the end of it."* It names the rule nobody sees, and it's what lets the guest feel trusted instead of told off. The tagline promises it cold ("Only you know the rules"), and whoYouAre pays it off.

**On the drink as built (v1).** It does what the reading claims every time it's made. The bottle waits, labelled, in the fridge. Each glass looks like any pale fizzy thing and is a proper cocktail. Nothing is hidden and nothing is performed. So the drink acts out the position (one that stays, working without its maker there), not the wound. It's veto-free, and the first apple-brandy base in the registry. It sounds worth having ("dry and bright, apple first, like the best sparkling cider"). Robin's tie rule is met in one clause ("as a nod to a phone booth that kept being a door"). No spark from the Matrix, and Tomás says why (basil, sage and olive are other pours'; peas would cost the veto-free place). Robin allows that if the drink is interesting, and the serve is the interest. My one ask for Tomás is below the verdict.

**As the guest (the Elf/Imp).** Recognised, not flattered: an everyday scene (keys in the fruit bowl, salt for sugar, helping look "very seriously") with no victim. The hidden part is the rules, and the fear is the persona's own "never too far", turned inward: the one that becomes someone's bad day. The gift is said out loud once ("a house where being surprised is safe"). The position answers the unmet "leave a mark" and ends on a gain and a wish. Nothing childish ("grown-ups still get to play"), no gender, no archetype name.

**As the sceptic.** Barnum: "you love a prank" fits anyone. Rules kept in private, and the tricks decided against, don't. Siblings and cousins kept out: no faces or straight face (*Unrehearsed*, *I'll Tell You Later*), no "underneath the mischief", "care" or "invitation" (*No Accident*), no "way in" or "with a grin" (*Fine by Me*), no "at the door" (*Before the Room*), no "a minute later" (*The Other Berry*), no "ordinary evening" as the gift. No "secret", "password", "getting in", "speakeasy" or the bar's name. Credit is never the point (the Straight Man's). 4-grammed against every pour with the lint's own `ngrams()`: results in my turn.

**History share:** y1–y2 are about 125 of 430 words in yours (under a third). y3 is interpretation, signposted.

**Watch:** "teatime" appears twice (whoYouAre, y3) and once more in y5. That's deliberate: it's the motif the position turns on. Hester, flag it if it reads as a refrain. "Let you in" appears twice (whoYouAre, y5), which also echoes the persona.

**Names (Wren):** my pick is **Nobody Saw a Thing**: the innocent look, said by the guest with a grin, and it holds the reading's turn (nobody sees the rules either). It shares no word with the tagline or the closing line. I dropped my first thought, *House Rules*, because *ruler-outlaw*'s story uses "house rules of manners" for a bar's posted rules, which is the gatekeeping frame we've fenced out. I could take Tomás's *The Kind You'd Want* (my own whoYouAre phrase, now cut from v1, so it wouldn't spend a line). Hester's *Made to Stay* names the position, so the closing line would say it twice. Others: *Gone by Teatime* (the wound, not the turn), *Back Before Anyone's Late*.

**Verdict:** not yet. The story, the person and the drink agree. What's still missing is Hester's audit of v1, and Tomás's answer on one point: the closing line's "next month" makes a promise about time ("still there next month"), while the bottle "keeps for about a month", so on day thirty the line is only just true.
