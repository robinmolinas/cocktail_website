## whoYouAre

Somebody's keys are in the fruit bowl, under the bananas, and you're helping them look, very seriously. This morning the sugar was salt. They call it trouble, and they keep letting you in anyway.

Nobody spots the rules. Every trick fits one person, and everything can be put right in seconds. The salt goes back. The keys turn up before anyone's late. For every trick people see, there are a few you decided against, because of who'd be on the end of it. You're not afraid of being told off. You're afraid of the trick that lands wrong and becomes somebody's bad day. So you keep them small, and gone by teatime.

Here's your gift, and few people have it: a house where being surprised is safe. Around you, grown-ups still get to play.

## yours

1. In the 2000s, Brian Shebairo owned a rough-and-tumble hot-dog shop on St. Mark's Place in New York, and was turning the bubble-tea lounge next door into a bar. His first plan was a rock-and-roll bar. It became a cocktail bar instead, and the door to it was his idea: a phone booth, at the back of the hot-dog shop. "A little Get Smart," he called it, after the 1960s TV spy spoof whose hero steps into a phone booth and dials a number.

2. The bar opened in 2007. More than a decade later, that phone booth was still its door, and one writer calls it, with a careful "arguably", the most famous bar entrance in the world. It's the same bar as another of my stories, but that one belongs to someone else.

3. I like to think that's exactly the joke you'd make, and exactly how you'd make it: played completely straight. No wink, just a phone booth in a hot-dog shop, where a phone booth has every right to be. Nobody gets hurt. The worst that can happen is you open a door and find a bar. But there's one difference from yours. His wasn't put back by teatime. It stayed. And I like to think it went on catching somebody new on a quiet weeknight, years after he first thought of it, without him there to watch it land.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is built on the same joke. It's a highball: apple brandy, dry vermouth and a spoon of rich syrup, mixed in a bottle that waits, labelled, in the fridge. Whenever someone wants one, they pour a little over ice in a plain tumbler, top it up with cold soda water, then squeeze a lemon peel over it. It looks like any fizzy thing you'd find in a fridge. It tastes like a proper cocktail: dry and bright, apple first, with lemon over the top. Nothing in it is hidden, and the label says what it is. The only trick is that it's there, ready, long after whoever made it has gone out, as a nod to a phone booth that kept being a door.

5. Keep your rules. They're why people let you in. But make one that stays. Not a trick for this afternoon: a surprise built into something that lasts. A note in the pocket of a coat they won't wear until winter. A line written inside a book you lend. You won't be there when it lands, and that's the point. Months from now someone will find it, grin at nobody, and know exactly who it was. I hope you leave a few of those behind you.
