## whoYouAre
You know the difference between how things are meant to work and how they actually do: who to ask, what to say, which door opens if you knock the right way. When the official route stops answering, people come to you, and you sort it without a fuss, and without making them feel small for asking.

Behind all that, there's a line. You've got one, and you've never needed to write it down. There are things you won't do, whatever they'd win you, and you keep that to yourself, because out loud it would sound soft. But it's why the people near you feel safe. They may not know exactly where the line is. They know you won't cross it. That matters more than you'd guess: with you, nobody has to wonder where they stand.

It's never the rules that get to you. It's the people who write them, break them when it suits, and call it procedure. Being pushed around by someone like that, with no say at all, is the nearest you come to being afraid.

## yours
1. Roy Olmstead had been the youngest lieutenant in the Seattle police. In 1920 he was caught bootlegging Canadian whisky and dismissed. He understood the city from the inside: as an officer, he'd taken bribes. Soon, as the biggest bootlegger on Puget Sound, he was the one paying them.

2. He wasn't a good man by any law's measure. But in a trade that was dangerous and often violent, he had a rule of his own: his men weren't allowed to carry guns. Better to lose the liquor than a life, he told them. Then federal agents tapped his phones, though wiretapping was a crime in Washington State, and in 1928 the Supreme Court ruled, five votes to four, that the evidence could stand.

3. Justice Brandeis wrote, in dissent, that officials should be held to the same rules the law commands of everyone else, and that a government that breaks the law breeds contempt for it. Nearly forty years on, the Court came round to the dissent on wiretaps. I like to think you'd have nodded at that. Not because Olmstead was innocent: he'd enforced the rules while letting himself off, and you'd be the first to say so. Because it's what you've always known: whoever writes the rules keeps them too. And even in that trade, his men knew exactly where his line was.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a classic from David Embury's book *The Fine Art of Mixing Drinks*: his Canadian. It's straight Canadian whisky, the kind that filled Olmstead's cases, with orange curaçao, Angostura and a teaspoon of syrup. One thing is mine. Elsewhere in the book, Embury swapped maple for sugar with Canadian whisky, and said one of his maple drinks was better with less of it. So the syrup here is maple, for the country the whisky came from, and it's one teaspoon, scraped flat. A quarter-teaspoon over, and the drink tips too sweet. I stir it until very cold and serve it in a chilled coupe, finished with orange peel. A line, held, in the glass.

5. You make more of the rules around you than you'd ever admit: who gets helped, and how, and what's never done. So let one person you trust in on where your line is, and let them hold you to it, as you'd hold anyone else. It costs you nothing you'd ever spend. And once someone else can see it, your line isn't a habit any more. It's your word.
