📜 **Hester:** Wren, I've read all of HistoryLink, the full LII opinion, *Katz*, and the Federal Judicial Center's 2010 history of the case (Hamm). Card: `_studio/fact-cards/roy-olmstead.md` (F1–F20). Your test has a hole, and I'll start with it.

**He paid off the police, and before that he took bribes himself.** FJC p. 7: as an officer he took bribes, and before Prohibition he sold his probation recommendations (p. 29). Later he "bribed the Seattle police and the sheriffs of King County". The Supreme Court's own statement of facts (Taft) says the wiretaps showed his dealings with Seattle police, the messages that got his arrested men released, and "direct promises to officers of payments". So your "this is me" line, *people who wrote them and never had to keep them*, points straight back at him. As a lieutenant he was one of those people. Humanities Washington (2023, an interview) says he told his men to use "bribes rather than violence". The bribery is part of the same code. If we keep him, the reading owns this in one plain clause. No line may imply he was clean.

Your other questions:
- **Violence:** none by his own men in any of the three sources. HistoryLink: rivals he put out of business "turned to … hijacking". A 1920 headline in its bibliography reads "Bullets Fly at Autos", with no shooter named. That stays out.
- **The rule is solid, his words aren't.** On HistoryLink it's reported speech, with no quotation marks: he "did not allow his employees to carry firearms" and told his men he'd rather lose a shipment than a life. Humanities Washington puts different words in quotation marks ("No amount of money is worth a human life"). Paraphrase it, never quote it as his. Two of your test points aren't on any page. "It bound him first": the rule covers his *employees*. "It cost him cargo": it's a stated preference, and no shipment is recorded as lost because of it.
- **Washington law:** yes. Taft's majority itself quotes the 1909 statute: intercepting a telephone message is a misdemeanor. It let the evidence in anyway. Holmes's "dirty business" does turn on that: evidence "only obtainable by a criminal act", and it "makes no difference" that the crime is under state law rather than federal law. A bonus from Brandeis's footnote, quoting the government's own brief: the Prohibition Unit "disclaims" wiretapping and Justice "has frowned on it".
- **Not two justices: four.** It was 5–4: Holmes, Brandeis, Butler and Stone dissented. Holmes and Brandeis made the point that the government was breaking the law, and Stone joined both.
- **Brandeis, exact:** officials "shall be subjected to the same rules of conduct that are commands to the citizen", and "If the government becomes a lawbreaker, it breeds contempt for law". The sentence that starts with "Our" is the "omnipresent teacher" one, so we paraphrase. FJC credits the lawbreaker line to Holmes, which is a slip; LII is the record. The first line is your "the same rule for me too", nearly word for word, said by a justice about the state.
- **Katz:** decided 18 December 1967. The Court held that Olmstead's trespass reasoning "can no longer be regarded as controlling". Olmstead died on 30 April 1966, nineteen months earlier. Scope: Katz adopted Brandeis's view on wiretaps, not the state-crime point.
- **"The code is the part people remember" doesn't stand.** HistoryLink: Seattle would "always" know him as "King of the Puget Sound Bootleggers". What lasts is his name on the case, and the dissent's argument that the rule-makers must keep the rules too. That argument outlived him. If you want a "leave a mark" line, it rests there.
- Small one: HistoryLink names her Elise; FJC says "known as Elsie". It's out either way.

My verdict: Olmstead passes only on the narrower centre. A lawbreaker who paid his way and kept one rule with his men. The state caught him by breaking its own law. Four justices said officials owe the citizen the same rules. That's a true mirror of your person's grievance.
