## whoYouAre

The top of the high board, the whole pool gone quiet, everyone looking up. Years later it's a microphone, or a first day in a place where you don't know a soul. You've gone through it so many times in your head that you know every step of it. Nobody watching has any idea how frightened you are.

You're scared every time. Nobody's ever been able to tell. What gets you up there isn't a lack of fear. It's a bigger one: the day you could have gone and didn't, and knowing it for the rest of your life. So you go. And afterwards, when people ask, you make it sound like very little. That isn't modesty. You've seen what happens when something looks impossible: nobody tries. So you make it look possible, and somebody behind you decides they could do it too. Hardly anyone knows you do that on purpose. It's one of the most generous things a person can do for whoever comes after them, and you do it every time.

## yours

1. Most whiskey is blended before it's bottled: many barrels, sometimes a great many, poured together so every bottle tastes the same. A single barrel doesn't get that. Whatever that one barrel became is what goes in the bottle. In Kentucky, Albert Blanton, whose name the distillery in Frankfort once carried, used to bottle one particularly good barrel on its own, for himself or as a gift to people in the trade. Plenty of distillery bosses of his time did the same. It stayed among friends.
2. Elmer T. Lee had worked under Blanton, at the same distillery, since 1949. When new owners asked him for a premium bourbon, the idea, he said, was what he'd learned from the Colonel. In 1984, Lee and his staff did it in front of everyone: barrels bottled one at a time, and sold. It was the first single-barrel bourbon brand. Other fine bourbons had been launched before and taken off the shelves for lack of interest. This one's audience gathered slowly, and then, as Lee told it, the other distillers followed, three or four years later. "I had no idea," he said, "it would become as worldwide as it is." When the company asked if a bourbon could carry his own name, he said yes, provided he could pick the bourbon that went in it. At ninety, he was still picking.
3. I don't know if he was nervous. I like to think he was, a little, and that nobody could tell. This is the part of the story that's about you. Years of learning something where it was safe, among people who'd forgive a bad one. Then one day doing it on a shelf, where anyone could judge it. And afterwards, no speech: "I had no idea." You know that shrug, because it's the one you use. And it did what it always does for you: it made the thing look possible, and the others followed.

Your fear will show them they don't have to be fearless to do it. And you get back something you've been giving away every time you shrugged: how much it took.
