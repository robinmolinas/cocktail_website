**Lemon or orange (you asked).** Oxford's entry contradicts itself. Its description (pdf 381) ladles the flame down "a spiral-cut lemon peel". Its own recipe steeps a lemon peel and pours over a second, *orange* peel, cut in a spiral and studded with cloves. Commander's Palace's recipe (Regan, *Joy* pdf 262) and frenchquarter.com both side with the recipe. So write "a long spiral of orange peel studded with cloves" and keep the lemon to the steep. Never write "a lemon spiral" (C1).

**The truer mirror.** Oxford pdf 381 traces the drink to a nineteenth-century Paris café drink, *brûlot de café*: coffee and eau-de-vie, simply mixed and set alight. New Orleans took that plain café habit and made it a coffee service at the table, in "several intricate steps". That's your test (2) on the page: the most ordinary thing at the end of a meal turned into an event. Paraphrase it. My quotes stay in the card.

**The fence: the order.** "Coffee last" holds in the classic recipe. It's in Oxford's (pdf 382), in Commander's (*Joy* 262), in Brennan's 1961 cookbook, and in Antoine's own 1978 cookbook (OCR highlight, cut off: the hot coffee goes "into the flaming brandy"). But frenchquarter.com, the page Oxford itself cites, has the coffee going in *before* the brandy is lit. So write "in the classic recipe, the coffee comes in at the end and puts the flame out". Never "always", never "every restaurant" (C2). The hours of steeping come from Oxford's recipe only (Commander's has none), so say "the recipe I follow", never "at Antoine's" (C4).
