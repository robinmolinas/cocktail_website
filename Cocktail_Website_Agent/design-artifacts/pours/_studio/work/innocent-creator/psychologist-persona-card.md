Source: `persona.py innocent-creator` (essence, story, goal Safety / fear Punishment, drivers "seek paradise / provide structure", Gauguin and Dubuffet as examples), my family sketch (`_studio/plans/work/innocent/psychologist-sketches.md`), the plan row, and the sibling pours read this round: *The Other Berry* (Surrealist), *Off-Label* (Experimentalist), *In Your Own Hand* (Muse), *Still Yours* (Trend Setter), *Anyway* (Morale Booster), plus the ten Innocent profiles.

So the drawer, the sting and the apology are out. What's left is the part of this persona nobody else has.

## Core truth
They don't know it's "wrong". The Surrealist knows it's odd and says so first; the Trend Setter knows it's early; this one sincerely can't see what the fuss is. When someone explains how it's usually done, they nod, and they still don't see what was wrong with it. The rule never made sense to them. It was just added from outside.

## Shows / hides
- **Shows:** the thing, straight away, handed over with plain delight: a cake iced the colour of the day, a card drawn instead of bought, a room painted a colour people raised an eyebrow at, a song made up for a birthday. No disclaimer: they think it's obviously lovely.
- **Hides:** the nod. They've been corrected kindly enough times to know the "right" way now, and they go along with it where it matters. Privately, they still think the sky was that colour.

## Hidden fear → hidden gift
- **Fear:** not the laugh itself. It's the day the explanation finally makes sense to them, and they start seeing their own things the way the people who know the rules see them. The persona sheet's "learning to be careful", told in this row's own terms (and without the word "careful").
- **Gift, said out loud (Robin's rule):** what they make rarely looks like the thing. It looks like what the thing felt like, and the people it's for can always tell. That's worth more than getting it right, and they should hear it.

## The "real me" line to aim for
- whoYouAre: *"Someone once explained, kindly, how it's usually done. You nodded. You still don't see what was wrong with it."*
- Fear beat: *"The laugh you can live with. The day you'd dread is the one when the explanation starts to make sense."*
- Tagline direction (positive, invites, about the person): *"You never saw what was wrong with it. Neither did anyone you made it for."*

## Truth vs legend
The kind story, told honestly. They want the legend granted with a smile, not busted (being corrected is the wound). But nothing false: "the story goes", and the history on the page.

## Tone
Honest, optimistic, straightforward (Innocent), with a lift of imagination (Creator). Warm and a little funny, on the maker's side. Never amused at them. The bartender sounds like someone who'd have eaten the yellow cake and asked for seconds.

## Distinct from siblings
- **The Surrealist** sees that things are strange and plays with it, disclaimer first. **This one doesn't see anything strange at all.** No "weird", no "strange", no apology, no sting.
- **The Experimentalist** varies things on purpose and keeps a hidden pile. **This one isn't experimenting,** and hides nothing they've made. What they hide is a nod.
- **The Muse** has their work go unasked-about; **the Trend Setter** is early and gets eye-rolls. **This one isn't early or overlooked:** they're outside the rules without knowing there were any.
- **The Outsider** chose to step out; **this one was never in.** **The Free Spirit** asks about the world; **this one answers it by making something.** **The Wise Kid** says the plain truth; **this one makes the plain beauty.** **The Giggler** laughs; **this one is laughed at without having meant to be funny.** **The Heart-throb** loses the loud love to "silly"; **this one would lose the eye to "that's not how it's done".**
- Inside the Creator family: the Minimalist cuts until it's true; the Old Master and the Auteur know every rule. This one knows none, and it shows.

## Traps
1. **"Artist" as a label.** The guest may never paint. Scenes: a cake, a card, a room, a song, a table set oddly, a meal cooked without a recipe. No brushes, canvas or galleries in the reading (the image may have raw brush marks; the words don't).
2. **Condescension.** Never "naive", "raw", "childlike", "untrained", "innocent", "pure", no childhood setting, no art brut pun. And never imply the things they make are bad: they make it the way it felt, not badly.
3. **Laughing at the drink.** Oxford's own words for it ("cheap buzz", "plonk-and-coke", the shopping bags, "not very authoritative-sounding", Romania's "diesel fuel") are never used. If the reading laughs at the Kalimotxo, it laughs at the guest.
4. **Defiance.** They didn't break the rule on purpose (Outlaw, Trend Setter, Surrealist ground). No "rebel", no "heresy" worn as a badge.
5. **Happy accident.** No "by accident" (the Trickster's tagline, and the trope is crowded).
6. **Taken ground:** the apology/disclaimer, the sting of being taken as a joke and keeping things to yourself (*The Other Berry*); the hidden pile, "let me try something" (*Off-Label*); "show the unfinished thing", "put your name on it" (*In Your Own Hand*); "keep it", "whoever rolls their eyes" (*Still Yours*); "throw it anyway" (*Anyway*); "give it away" (*Down the Line*); "don't look it up" (Free Spirit plan); "careful" (Heart-throb plan); "never… by accident" (*No Accident*); "What scares you isn't… It's…" (the Trickster's frame).
7. **The origin as a con.** Oxford (after Miles) says young "entrepreneurs" added cola "to mask the flavor" of wine they'd planned to sell. Told carelessly, that's disguising bad wine to sell it: a trick. The rescue is optional, and if told, it's making it good to drink, never masking, never selling.
8. **Naming.** The Heart-throb's Brooklyns and the Giggler's Paper Plane both lean on names. The Kalimotxo's nickname name gets one plain clause, never the centre.

## Story test (sharpened)
(1) Made the "wrong" way by people who didn't see why it was wrong, out of plain sense or delight, not defiance; (2) the people who know the rules frowned, explained or debated it, on the page; (3) the people it was made for kept making it anyway and never came round to the rule; (4) made to share. **Out:** quality vindication (the experts came round, so it counts), tricks, happy accidents, anyone made to look foolish in the telling.

## Position direction (for yours 5, not the closing line)
Protect the not-seeing. Something like: let them explain; you don't have to start seeing it. Make the next thing for the person it's for, the way it felt, before you ask anyone whether it's right. The gain: the pleasure of their own eye, and the people who can tell. (To swap-test once drafted: *Still Yours*, *In Your Own Hand*, *Anyway*, *The Other Berry*.)

## Verdict on the plan's lead
**Keep the Kalimotxo, reframed.** Read on the page (Oxford CALIMOTXO pdf 386–387; *Codex* p. 18):
- The mirror is **not the rescue** (legend-grade, and commercially framed). It's the drink that never learned the rule. Wine people's view is that you don't pour cola into wine; the people it's poured for, in the Basque Country where it's "immensely popular" (Oxford), have been mixing it in equal parts for more than fifty years and still don't see the problem.
- **The find:** Oxford's only detailed history of the drink comes from *La verdadera historia del kalimotxo*, edited by a Bilbao **artists' collective** (Funky Project). The Companion admits the source doesn't sound authoritative, and takes them at their word anyway, *because the people of Bilbao are serious about the drink*. The people it's made for are the authority. That's this guest's position, already written into the book that tells the story. (Oxford's wording there is gently mocking: we say it kindly, in our own words, and never quote it.)
- **A correction to my own plan review:** I said "the scorn is on the page (Celhay's title)". It's thinner than that. The title is a question in a French marketing journal: *"cocktail hérétique ou nouvelle opportunité pour la filière vin?"* (heretical cocktail, or a new opportunity for the wine trade?). Told plainly, it's funny and it fits: the people who know wine debating whether it's a heresy or a business chance, while the Basques just keep pouring. But "wine people call it heresy" as a flat fact isn't on the page. Hester: can you fetch Miles, "If Bacchus Drank Cola" (NYT, 2 December 2007), or Celhay itself, for a real frown? If not, the reading uses the title as it is.
- **Clause 1 is honest only as "named", not "invented":** Oxford says Spaniards had been mixing wine and cola since the 1920s at least. That actually helps: it was always obvious to the people drinking it. Nobody needed permission.
- **The *Codex* line (p. 18):** craft respect, no grand wine in it. That's the rule-knowers leaving it as it is, and it's what Tomás's spark must honour.

**The Alexander stays backup**, with its guards. It still fails the person: a professional making a polished drink in a client's advertising colour, white reads as purity, and Oxford has it fading once the knowing took over (the fear winning).
