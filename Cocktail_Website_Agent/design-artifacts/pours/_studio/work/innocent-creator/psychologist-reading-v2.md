## whoYouAre

What you've made, you hand over straight away: a cake iced the colour the day felt, a card you drew instead of buying one, a birthday song with one verse too many. It's obviously lovely. It hasn't crossed your mind that there's a proper way to do it.

Then someone explains, kindly, how it's usually done. You nod. You still don't see what was wrong with it. The laugh, when there is one, you can live with. The day you'd dread is the one when the explanation starts to make sense. Because what you make rarely looks like the thing. It looks like how the thing felt, and the people it's for can always tell. That's rarer than getting it right, and it's worth more.

## yours

1. In the Basque Country, people pour red wine and cola into a tall glass of ice, half and half, and call it a Kalimotxo. Spaniards had been mixing the two since at least the 1920s. The name, the story goes, came at a festival in Algorta in 1972, after a friend's nickname, and within about ten years it was popular right across the Basque Country and Navarre. Chileans call it jota, Hungarians vadász.

2. People who know wine have their doubts. In 2012, in a tapas bar in Granada, a food writer's partner started pouring cola into a glass of the good local red. The barman saw it and came down the bar, pointing: the house wine from the barrel is the one you mix, and this bottle, you don't.

3. The rule never took in Bilbao. The fullest history of the drink was put together by an artists' collective there called Funky Project. The Oxford Companion admits that doesn't sound much like an authority, then takes them at their word anyway, because in Bilbao they're serious about this drink.

4. I like to think you'd have done what the Basques did: looked at the wine and the cola, and not seen the problem. So the cocktail I've made for you is a Kalimotxo, the way they pour it: a young Spanish red, the kind poured by the glass, and Coca-Cola, half and half, stirred. It's a Basque classic, dark, cold and fizzy, a little like sangria. I haven't corrected a thing. The one change is the ice. It's the same drink, frozen the night before, so as it melts it turns into more of itself, never into water. The fizz comes from the fresh pour, and the glass is still half and half at the last sip. That's for you: something you make should stay just as you made it, all the way down.

5. So when someone next explains how it's usually done, listen. Some of it will be useful. Then make the next thing the way it looked to you, and hand it straight to whoever you made it for, before anyone else sees it. They'll see what you meant. And you'll get to enjoy making it again, the way you did before anyone explained.
