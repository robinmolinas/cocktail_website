# The Romantic (lover-sage): Wren's read, round 1

Source: `persona.py lover-sage` (essence: an intellectual approach to love, a love of true beauty; goal intimacy/connection; fear isolation; "reads, reflects and writes the long letter instead of the quick message"; "love is a way of knowing the world more deeply"; dreads "shallow company, to be surrounded by people and still unseen"). Byron is out of the reading.

## Who this is

Something beautiful stops them: a line in a book, a piece of music, a face in an old painting, the way a friend talks about the thing they love. Other people say "lovely" and move on. This person can't. They have to know why it moved them, and the knowing is part of the loving. It's the same with people: they fall for someone by wanting to understand them, all the way down. A conversation that goes deep at one in the morning is, for them, a kind of intimacy most people only get from touch.

**The one true thing no sibling shares:** their loneliness happens in company. Not an empty room (that's the Geisha's fear), not the end of the night (the Pleasure Seeker's): a full table where the talk never leaves the surface. They can do small talk perfectly well. Then they go home and write three pages to someone who wasn't there.

**What they hide:** that they've started to save themselves. They hold the long version back for the one person who'd follow it, and give the room the short one. Part of them has quietly decided most rooms aren't deep enough. Sometimes that's true. Mostly they never find out, because they never offered. (Said gently: this is a small, private judgement, never snobbery. It's protection.)

**What they fear:** sending the long version and getting a short one back. Being answered at the surface. Under it, the Lover's isolation: being surrounded and still unseen.

**What they give:** they take what you love seriously. Tell them about the thing you care about, and they ask the second question, and the third. People feel, for once, that the deep end of them was worth visiting. That's the kindness to say out loud (Robin's rule).

**Sage in the Lover:** not truth-telling (the Connoisseur's), not judgement of quality. The Sage half is the *wanting to understand* as a way of loving. Their fear of deception shows up only as impatience with charm that has nothing under it.

## Story test (for any lead)
1. A real exchange at depth: someone made something long and beautiful, and someone else met it at the same depth (across distance or years is fine).
2. The risk of offering depth to people who may not want it (strangers, the wrong room).
3. It holds: the thing was received, and it survives.
4. Out: unrequited obsession (the Obsessed's), quality judgement and "put it into words" (the Connoisseur's), death as the romance, Byron, brothers.

## Siblings and owners to keep clear of
- **Lover family positions are crowded around "say it":** Geisha "Ask", Flirt "say it plainly once", Rough Diamond "one line", Pure Heart "reply now", Charmer "tell one person what you've been saving". The Romantic already says plenty. **Their position is not "say it" but *where*: give the long version in the room you'd written off, not only to the chosen one.** It must also not read as the Charmer's (who withholds to be chased; the Romantic withholds because they've judged the room).
- **The Obsessed (*Rosetta*):** owns "I love it" sounds like too much, the word "nice", watching their face to see if they felt it. Keep the Romantic about *people*, not a thing they keep going back to. Avoid "too much" and "nice" as the hinge.
- **The Connoisseur (*A Brother's Care*, approved):** owns "put it into words", "say why", the notebook, the true story vs the famous one. No "why" as the gift's hinge.
- **The Idealist (*The Long Answer*):** owns a reader's letter, mint going in last, and "see who writes back". So: no "writes back", no "long answer", keep "letter" sparing and never in title or closing line.
- **The Genius (*Show Your Working*):** "the real answer" in its tagline. "Answer" stays a plain verb inside the reading, never in name or tagline.
- **Off Duty (*caregiver-hero*):** owns "by heart". Say "every word", "word for word".
- **Pure Heart:** owns messages answered fast and "three hours to reply". If a message scene appears, it's about depth, never speed.
- **The Entertainer (*Cold Water First*):** performing for strangers' faces. McMillian's recital is never framed as a show.

## Traps
- Making the guest a reader/writer by profession ("the bookish one"). A scene, never a label.
- Flattery ("you feel more deeply than others"). Say what they do, not how deep they are.
- Romanticising loneliness. The fear is named once, then turned.
- Quoting the Ode at the guest, or dating it, before Hester has a text.
- Reading the drink as a performance the guest must give (the room's "no drink you perform" lesson).

## Verdict on the plan's lead: confirm, accept with a reframe (sharpened)

**Chris McMillian and Soule Smith's "Ode to the Mint Julep" (Oxford McMILLIAN pdf 1273, ICE MALLET pdf 1066).** Now that the family's readings exist, it fits better than I thought at plan stage, because it carries the one position no sibling has:

- **The exchange (limb 1):** someone once wrote a long, lavish poem about a drink. More than a century later a bartender learned every word, and still gives it to whoever's at his bar, pounding the ice in rhythm as he goes. That is a reply at the same depth, a century late. It's this person's hope: that somewhere, someone will read them that closely.
- **The risk (limb 2):** a hotel bar is the home of small talk. He brings the long version anyway, to strangers, without first checking whether they deserve it. That's the guest's move, the one they hold back.
- **It holds (limb 3):** he keeps giving it; the julep and the poem survive together because he does. "Making drinks is 10 percent of what he does" (Oxford) is the guest's other ninety.

**Conditions (carried from the plan, plus new):**
1. Nothing of the Ode is quoted, dated or summarised until Hester finds a period printing (one of her two web visits). Soule Smith's dates out until then.
2. McMillian is never a performer and the recital is never a show: he's giving the long version to people who didn't ask for it.
3. Present tense dated (he's alive; Oxford's "at the Ritz-Carlton's Library Bar" needs its year or a past tense).
4. "By heart", "writes back", "long answer", "put it into words", "too much", "nice" stay out.
5. The position: offer the long version in the room you'd written off, without choosing the reader first. Ends on what they gain (someone leans in), warm wish.
6. If the Ode can't be verified at all, the room's web hunt is for a true two-way exchange with a drink in it.
