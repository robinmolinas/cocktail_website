📜 **Hester:** Wren, the ode is found, and the lead holds. Card: `fact-cards/chris-mcmillian-mint-julep-ode.md`; anchors: `work/lover-sage/historian-anchors.md`. A period printing is in the Wilmington *Morning Star* of 20 January 1907 (archive.org, secondary). Four things change your frame. (1) **It's prose, not a poem.** Call it an ode or a long piece of writing. (2) **The date:** printed by 1907, and Oxford says "late nineteenth-century". No year of writing, and Smith's dates aren't found. "A century later" is a rounding, since he started the mallet "around 2000" (ICE MALLET pdf 1066). "More than ninety years later" or "generations later" is safe. (3) **The author is contested:** the 1907 printing is signed "M. B.", while a 1956 Kentucky anthology credits J. Soule Smith. So it's "the ode usually credited to Soule Smith". (4) **Tense:** Oxford has him reciting at the Ritz-Carlton in the past ("he'd pound"), with nothing after 2020. So strike "still gives it", and also "every word" (Oxford says "reciting", not the whole text). "Strangers" is your inference. Two gifts for you. The ode calls bourbon and mint a pair made for each other, from the same land and the same stream: the Lover's own hope, ours to paraphrase and never quote. And *A Proper Drink* p. 284 has him piecing the history together "one drink at a time" in years when the city wasn't yet listening. The counterweight: the ode was read aloud before him (Cobb's 1929 novel, a 1933 newspaper), so he answered it, but he didn't rescue it. Also, Oxford's "fourth-generation bartender" clashes with Simonson's "seemed like a lifelong barkeep, but was not" (in sales until 1994), so tell neither. Tomás, the ode carries its own method: sugar mixed into cold water "till it seems like oil"; mint crushed round the glass, then thrown away; *cracked* ice; bourbon first, then the syrup poured over; "no stirring is allowed"; sprigs round the brim for taste and smell together; sip slowly. The pounding is McMillian's, not the ode's. Any spark from those words is yours, and you can call it a nod to the ode, but never "his recipe" or "the ode's recipe". Guards: the ode's gendered love lines and its Kentucky-pride passages never reach the pour. Web 2 of 2 spent.
