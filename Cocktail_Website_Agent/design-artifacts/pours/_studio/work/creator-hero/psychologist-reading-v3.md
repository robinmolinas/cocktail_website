## whoYouAre

You see the finished thing first. Long before anyone else can, you can describe it: its size, its shape, what it will change. And you describe it so clearly that people want to help build it.

What they don't see is the arithmetic you do alone: how far away the end is, what it will cost, whether you're the one slowing it down. Your belief wavers more than anyone knows. You carry on anyway. You can live with slow, and you can live with hard. What frightens you isn't that it won't get finished. It's that you'll make it smaller so it can be.

## yours

1. In New Orleans, more than a hundred years ago, a saloonkeeper named Henry Ramos served a gin fizz unlike anyone else's: gin, lemon and lime, sugar, cream and egg white, shaken until the ice was gone and the drink had turned into a soft white cloud. It was slow to make. Then it became famous, which meant more of them, every one just as slow.

2. The obvious thing would have been to shorten the shake. He didn't. He hired more hands instead. Beside each bartender stood one or two young Black men hired only to shake. By Mardi Gras in 1915 there were thirty-five men doing nothing but the shaking, each one working until his arms gave out, then passing the shaker down the line. The drink was never one man's work, and it couldn't have existed without theirs. And Ramos had always given the recipe away to anyone who asked. When Prohibition closed his bar, he went on giving it, and in 1925 he dictated it to a newspaper, word for word. He died three years later.

3. I don't know why, but I like to think he knew what you know: the recipe was never the hard part. The hard part was refusing to make it smaller, and keeping it whole even when it needed more hands than he had. And when he couldn't carry it any further, he left it clear enough for other people to carry on. They still are. A century later, bartenders are still inventing ways to reach his standard, and his formula still works.

4. So this is his drink, made to his standard: gin, lemon and lime, a little sugar, cream, egg white, three drops of orange flower water as written in the recipe he gave away, and soda to finish. Bartenders today shake it once without ice, to build the foam, and then hard with ice for five minutes, far longer than anyone expects. The soda goes in slowly, until a white cap rises above the glass. If someone is with you, hand them the shaker halfway. It can't be made halfway. It can be shared.

5. So, a proposal. Think of the thing you've been quietly trimming so that it fits: your time, your budget, other people's patience. Put it back to its real size. Then tell someone the whole of it, not the version that sounds reasonable, and ask for their hands. The right people will take the shaker. Things this size are never finished by one person. But someone has to refuse to make them smaller.

## Closing line ruling (Tomás's)
Proposed: *Pass the shaker if you must. Just don't stop shaking.* Ruling: keep the shape, lose "if you must". It turns passing into a concession, and this guest already hears asking for help as weakness; the whole reading says passing it on is part of the vision. Offer: *Pass the shaker when your arms give out. Just don't let it stop.*
