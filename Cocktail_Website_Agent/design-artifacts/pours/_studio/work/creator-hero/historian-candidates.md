## Candidate A (my lead): the Ramos Gin Fizz

**Three sentences.** Henry Charles Ramos, a New Orleans saloonkeeper, put two old drinks together that nobody had combined (a Silver Fizz and a Cream Lemonade) and insisted it be shaken until the ice was gone, so hard that at Mardi Gras 1915 he had thirty-five men passing each shaker down a line, one pair of arms after another. When Prohibition shut his bar, he didn't take the formula to his grave, as San Francisco's Duncan Nicol did with his Pisco Punch; he gave it away to anyone who asked, and went into the paint business. He is the only bartender in history whose name stayed on a great classic; not even Jerry Thomas managed that.

**Why it mirrors.** Leave a mark, literally: his name is the drink. A vision so demanding it took a chain of hands to finish each glass (Sagrada Familia in miniature). And the choice at the end: give the plan away so it outlives you, versus keep it and let it die. A century of bartenders has kept building methods toward his standard (dry shake, paint-can shakers, nitrogen), and his formula still holds. Hero's side: it is still the classic test of a bartender's skill and stamina.

**Risks and conflicts.**
- **Year of invention: contested, keep out.** *Imbibe!* (pdf 110) puts Ramos at the Imperial Cabinet in 1888; Oxford says May 1887 and dates the first fizz most likely 1892–94, first in print 1895. The reading says no year.
- **Shake length: contested.** Contemporary accounts say fifteen minutes; Wondrich doubts it (*Imbibe!* pdf 111); Oxford says "five minutes and more" (pdf 1606). Never state a number; "until the ice was gone" is safe.
- **The shaker boys were young Black men** (both sources). True, and it can't be erased or romanticised: if the chain image goes in, Wren decides how it's told with care, and it must not become a heroic-founder myth that forgets whose arms did the shaking. This is a real edge; I'd flag it to Robin either way.
- Motive: why he gave the recipe away isn't on the record. "He gave it away" is fact; "so it would outlive him" is **our reading** and must be signposted.
- Menu: a fizz (cream, egg white) is a new family and style for the menu. Allergens (egg, dairy) are Tomás's to check against the veto list.

## Candidate B (runner-up): the Last Word

**Three sentences.** An equal-parts drink (gin, maraschino, Chartreuse, lime) appears on a Detroit Athletic Club menu in 1916 and then all but vanishes for most of a century. In 2003 one bartender, Murray Stenson, put it on the menu at the Zig Zag Café in Seattle, and within a few years it was everywhere. Its four-part pattern became a template: scores of new drinks (the Final Ward, the Naked and Famous) are built on its frame.

**Why it mirrors.** A design good enough to wait: forgotten, then found, then became the foundation others build on. The Creator's wish that the work outlast its moment.

**Sources.** Oxford Companion, LAST WORD (pdf 1166): the 1916 menu, 2003 revival, the riffs. Oxford Companion, CHARTREUSE (pdf 439–440): the revival's effect on Chartreuse.

**Risks.** The creator is unknown: the vaudevillian Frank Fogarty rests on one unnamed source in a 1951 book, so he's a **legend** at best. Weaker "person" than Ramos; the hero here is the reviver, not the visionary. Fits "rediscovery" better than "a vision followed through".

## Round 2: tested against Wren's card (the smaller version; the handover)
- **Ramos against "making it smaller so it can be finished":** the long shake made each fizz slow to serve. As demand grew, he added hands (eight bartenders in 1899, fifteen by 1909, 35 shakermen in a chain at Mardi Gras 1915) and kept the drink the same (Oxford, RAMOS, HENRY CHARLES, pdf 1606; *Imbibe!*, pdf 111). **Inference**, labelled: nothing on the record says he ever considered a shorter shake. The added hands are fact.
- **The handover:** recipe given away when Prohibition came; dictated to the *Item-Tribune* in 1925 (*Imbibe!*, pdf 112); rights sold by his heirs to the Roosevelt Hotel; brought back after Repeal (Oxford, RAMOS GIN FIZZ, pdf 1609). Bartenders are still inventing ways to meet his standard (dry shake, paint-can shakers, nitrogen) while "the basic formula still works" (same, pdf 1609). That is a work outliving its maker and being carried on: **fact**.
- **Honest limit:** his drink was finished; what goes on is the carrying. Nothing records his doubts, so Wren's "the belief wavers" is hers to give the guest, not something to pin on Ramos.
- **For Tomás, time in the glass:** a solera is a row of barrels that is never fully emptied, topped up from the next youngest as the oldest is drawn (Oxford, SOLERA, pdf 1833). A structure built never to finish. Craft fact only; any "oldest solera" date needs its own source.
- **Gaudí's "my client is not in a hurry":** not in the library. **Legend** until I find a primary source. Keep it out.
