## whoYouAre

Two friends fall out, and sooner or later each of them tells you their side. You hear both out to the end, and you ask for the bits that don't flatter them. Then you tell them plainly what you think happened, and it's rarely what either of them hoped. And here's the odd part: when trouble comes round again, it's the one you disagreed with who calls first.

People assume plain speaking is easy for you. It isn't, not once. You want to be liked as much as anyone, and later, alone, you replay the conversation, half hoping someone will show you what you missed. What you won't put up with is the kind-sounding fudge, the "let's not make a fuss", because you know who pays for it, and it's never the person saying it. It's the quietest one there. So here's what your plainness gives people, and it matters more than they ever tell you: the quietest voice gets the same answer as the loudest.

## yours

1. For decades, distillers argued about a word. In the 1830s, whisky makers in the Lowlands took up a new kind of still, one that could run without stopping, and made a light, pure grain whisky cheap enough for city factory workers. The makers of malt whisky, from the old pot stills, said the new spirit wasn't whisky at all. They had age and tradition on their side. The grain side had the size: in 1877, six of the biggest grain distillers joined forces.

2. In 1908 a British commission sat and took evidence in person from across the spirits trade. Where it landed was plain. Whisky was a spirit distilled from cereal grain, its starch turned to sugar by malt, and nothing in that said which still. The new spirit was whisky. Blending stayed legal, and the commission set out what a label could claim.

3. That settled the law, not the argument. Over decades of marketing, the idea that the older whisky is the real one has largely won people over. The ruling stood anyway. That will be familiar to you: a call that went against the side with age and tradition, and then held, liked or not. I like to think the people it looked after most weren't in the room at all: whoever was buying the cheaper bottle.

4. The cocktail I've made for you starts from one of the most renowned classics of all, the Old-Fashioned. I make it with blended Scotch: malt and grain whisky together, the two whiskies that argument was about. Only the sugar is different. I sweeten it with a syrup made partly from barley malt, which the commission's definition asked of every whisky, whatever still it came from. That's as a nod to how you treat people: one rule, whichever side they're on. The malt makes it rounder and warmer than plain sugar would. Angostura goes in as well, the glass is a heavy one packed with ice, and last, orange peel is squeezed above it for its scent.

5. One thing I'd say to you, gently. Once everyone's been heard and you've made the call, you've done the fair part. You don't also owe the room its agreement. Some of them will never come round, and that doesn't make the call any less fair. If the evidence ever changes, it'll find you, because you're the one who always asks for it. Until then, take the evening off from deciding. You've earned the nights when nobody needs an answer from you.
