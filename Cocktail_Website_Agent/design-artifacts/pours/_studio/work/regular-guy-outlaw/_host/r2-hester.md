**Lead: Simon Ford, the salesman who never sold a bottle.** *A Proper Drink* pp. 108–112 (Simonson, interviews on the record) and Oxford FORD, SIMON (pdf 828), primary. Card: `fact-cards/simon-ford-plymouth.md`.
- **Who he is.** In 2000 Seagram's laid him off. In 2002 his old colleague Nick Blacknell sent him to New York to hand-sell Plymouth gin, then down to five thousand cases a year: "a suicide mission" (p. 109). Simonson's portrait: he "laughed after every other sentence and was hard to dislike" (p. 110). His own line: "I never once sold a bottle." He asked bartenders "can I teach you about my gin?" instead (p. 110).
- **Test 1, a rule bent:** years later, at Pernod Ricard, he'd already spent $60,000 on press dinners when "his bosses cut the entire budget. Rather than drop it, Ford forged ahead": five dinners, at a cost of $330,000 (p. 111). One limit: the page doesn't say whether he told them first. That press dinners are public is our inference.
- **Test 2, the cheek on the record:** "I thought I might get fired," Ford said, "but the events were so well-received I got funding to do more" (p. 112).
- **Test 3, let off, and the rule stayed bent for everyone:** when Jim Beam took over distribution, they "wanted nothing to do with whatever a brand ambassador might be". Blacknell bargained: "Give him three months." "Ford stayed" (p. 111). On the invoices for the bartender trips, Blacknell said: "I didn't really mind; he was doing a good job" (p. 111). And the table: he got Plymouth to fly American bartenders to the distillery. That began a practice in which "Humble drink-slingers" who'd never been offered "so much as car fare" were flown to Cognac, Kentucky, Jerez and beyond (p. 111). Oxford: he "convinced his employers to pay", and the model "was copied and employed across the liquor and bar industries" (pdf 828).
- **Test 4, nobody ordinary paid:** the cost fell on Plymouth and Pernod Ricard. Here is the counterweight, in the open: on the page before (p. 108), freelance consultants lost work as in-house ambassadors took over ("a kick in the teeth for us", Ben Reed). That was a shift across the industry, not Ford's cheek, and they weren't newer or ordinary people.
- **Test 5, the fear:** he names it himself, "I thought I might get fired". Beam is your "rules are rules" moment: a new owner who didn't know him and wanted nothing to do with his job.
- **Risks, so you can weigh them now:** he's a salesman. Oxford gives both motives, wider reach for the brands *and* standing for the bartenders. Simonson calls the role "a confidence man" (p. 109, about ambassadors generally), and calls Ford "glad-handing" with "profligate spending habits" (p. 111). Those words stay out of the reading, and so do the parties. Conflicts to keep out: the year he started (Oxford "late 1990s", *Proper* 2002) and the dinners' year (undated). "Let off" is our word. The page says "Ford stayed" and "got funding to do more".
- **Siblings:** *Who's In?* quotes him only as a witness to LAB. The chef at the dinners belongs to *Unrehearsed*, so he goes unnamed. The 86 Company he cofounded is *Loose on Top*'s shape: one clause at most.
