## whoYouAre

It's ten past ten, and the kitchen closed at ten. You go up to the counter, make the cook laugh, and five minutes later there are chips for six. The doorman, the receptionist, the teacher at the gate: you've had a yes out of every one of them, and you asked to their face.

People say you get away with everything. You don't. You get let off, which is different: someone decided to, because it's you. Every "go on, then" is a small proof you're still wanted. What you dread was never the telling-off. It's the one who doesn't look up, says "rules are rules", and handles you like a stranger.

There's a code in it, though you'd laugh at the word: you only try it on someone who can afford to say yes. And here's what you never get credit for. When a rule bends for you, it bends for everyone you came with.

## yours

1. When Nick Blacknell was brought in to save Plymouth gin, it was down to five thousand cases in a year. He sent an old colleague, Simon Ford, to New York to hand-sell it. Ford said he never once sold a bottle. He asked bartenders, "Can I teach you about my gin?" He was, by one account, hard to dislike.

2. An American whiskey company took over selling Plymouth and wanted nothing to do with whatever his job was, so Blacknell bargained for three months. Ford stayed. He talked Plymouth into flying a handful of American bartenders to its distillery in England. On the bills, Blacknell said: "Knowing Simon, he probably didn't tell me how much it cost… I didn't really mind; he was doing a good job."

3. That's the sentence I'd frame. Somebody guessed, and didn't mind. And those trips spread: bartenders who'd never been offered so much as a bus fare were flown to Cognac, Kentucky and Jerez, and the idea was copied across the trade. I like to think he never forgot who'd let him off. That's you. A yes you win is never only for you.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a French 75, a renowned Champagne classic, made with Plymouth, the gin he said he never sold. It breaks a rule people love to quote: the Champagne goes over ice, a whole wine glass of it. Somebody will wince. They needn't. The original recipe went over cracked ice, in a tall tumbler. I've made it a little sharper and sweeter than the flute version, so it stays balanced as the ice melts into it. The rest of the bottle goes round the table, as a nod to those trips: when Ford got a yes, other people came along.

5. Keep asking. It works, and whoever says yes enjoys it more than they let on. But now and then, ask for something you're owed, plainly, with no grin and no "go on". Watch the yes come anyway. The people who let you off were never doing it for the charm. They like you. I hope they keep saying yes.

## Resonance (self-test, v2)

- **The "this is me" line:** *"You get let off, which is different: someone decided to, because it's you."* The tender beat is the fear ("handles you like a stranger"). The private thing nobody has named for them is the code ("only … someone who can afford to say yes"). The gift is said out loud: "it bends for everyone you came with".
- **As the guest:** the kitchen at ten past ten is a scene they'll have lived. y2's "wanted nothing to do with whatever his job was" is the stranger who doesn't know them, the fear, met and won ("Ford stayed"). y4 lets them break a rule, then tells them the drink's own history is on their side: let off again, by the recipe.
- **As a sceptic (siblings):** the cheek goes up, at a rule, never at a newcomer (*Got You*). The yes comes from fondness, not from a hard "only if" (*Fine by Me*). The bending is partly for fun, and the reading admits it (*Can't Watch*). There's a grin and a code, not a system or a line (*Where You Stand*). "Line", "plain sight", "only if", "invitation", "ignored" and "chosen" are all absent.
- **Guards kept:** no field gun, war, Prohibition, "intoxicating" or Jazz Age line. No "confidence man", "profligate", parties, the chef, LAB or the 86 Company. Never "Ford saved Plymouth" (N2: Blacknell was "brought in to save" it, the page's verb). Ford is alive, so every line about him is in the past tense. "He"/"him"/"his" in yours refer only to Ford or Blacknell, never the guest (lint warnings accepted).
