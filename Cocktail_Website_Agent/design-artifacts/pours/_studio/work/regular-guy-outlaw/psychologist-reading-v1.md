## whoYouAre

It's ten past ten, and the kitchen closed at ten. Everyone else has accepted it. You haven't. You go up to the counter, say something that makes the cook laugh, and five minutes later there are chips for six. The doorman who said no trainers, the receptionist who said the form was due yesterday, the teacher who said nobody leaves early: you've had a yes out of every one of them. You don't sneak. You ask to their face.

People say you get away with everything. You don't, and you know it. You get let off, which is different: someone decided to, because it's you. You never say that part. Every "go on, then" is a small proof that you're still wanted. And what you dread was never the telling-off. It's the one who doesn't look up, says "rules are rules", and handles you by the book, like a stranger.

There's a code in it too, though you'd laugh at the word: you only ever try it on someone who can afford to say yes. And here's what you never get credit for. When a rule bends for you, it bends for everyone you came with. And for a minute, whoever's behind the counter stops being the rule and gets to be someone who laughed.

## yours

1. When Nick Blacknell was brought in to save Plymouth gin, it was down to five thousand cases in a year. He sent an old colleague, Simon Ford, to New York to hand-sell it. Ford said he never once sold a bottle. He asked bartenders, "Can I teach you about my gin?" He was, by one account, hard to dislike.

2. When a big American firm took over selling Plymouth and didn't want him, Blacknell bargained for three months. Ford stayed. Then he talked Plymouth into flying a handful of American bartenders to its distillery in England. On the bills, Blacknell said: "Knowing Simon, he probably didn't tell me how much it cost… I didn't really mind; he was doing a good job."

3. That's the sentence I'd frame. Somebody knew, and chose not to mind. And those trips spread: bartenders who'd never been offered so much as a bus fare were flown to Cognac, Kentucky and Jerez, and the whole trade copied the idea. I like to think he never forgot who'd let him off. That's you. A yes you win is never only for you.

4. The cocktail I've made for you starts with Plymouth, the gin he never sold, and breaks a rule everyone knows: the sparkling wine goes over ice. Somebody will wince. I've chosen a wine with a little more sweetness, so the melting ice rounds it off instead of watering it down. It's poured from one bottle for the whole table, as a nod to those trips: when Ford got a yes, other people came along.

5. Keep asking. It works, and whoever says yes enjoys it more than they let on. But now and then, ask for something you're owed, plainly, with no grin and no "go on". Watch the yes come anyway. The people who let you off were never doing it for the charm. They like you. I hope they keep saying yes.

## Resonance (self-test, v1)

- **As the guest:** the kitchen at ten past ten and "go on, then" are the scenes. The line that should stop them: *"You get let off, which is different: someone decided to, because it's you."* The fear ("handles you by the book, like a stranger") is the tender beat, and the code is the private thing nobody has named for them.
- **As a sceptic:** could a sibling claim this? *Got You* aims its jokes at a newcomer; here the cheek goes up, at a rule. *Can't Watch* bends rules for strangers out of distress; here the bending is partly for fun, and the reading admits it. *Fine by Me* takes on the hard "only if"; here the yes comes free, because someone likes you. *Where You Stand* has a system and a line; this person has a grin and a code. The words "line", "invitation", "ignored", "plain sight", "only if" and "chosen" are all absent on purpose.
- **Risks I can see:** (a) the salesman frame. Simonson's "confidence man" and "profligate" never reach the reading, and Ford's motive stays unspoken, except for one signposted "I like to think". (b) Ford is alive, so everything about him is in the past tense, with no present-tense claims. (c) "Chips", "trainers" and "doorman" are British words, all everyday. (d) History share: 50% of yours (158 of 318 words, the lint's `words()`), counting y3's "Somebody knew" and trips sentences as history and the signposted line as mine. y4 will change once the spec is in.
