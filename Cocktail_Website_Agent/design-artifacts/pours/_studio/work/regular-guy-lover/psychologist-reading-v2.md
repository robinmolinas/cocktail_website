## whoYouAre

Half past eight on a Friday, your phone lights up: "free tonight?" You know what it means. Something else fell through. And you nearly always are free, and you nearly always go, and ten minutes after you arrive they've kicked their shoes off and they're telling you about their week. People are at ease with you in a way they aren't anywhere else. Families take to you on the first visit. New people get brought to meet you first, because with you nobody has to try.

What you don't tell anyone is that you'd like, now and then, to be the plan and not the fallback. Not the easy option: the one somebody picked on purpose. You don't say it, because wanting something out loud feels like a fuss, and you've never wanted to be the one causing it. So you stay gracious, and part of you wonders whether you'll always be liked by everyone and picked first by no one. Here's what deserves saying: with you, nobody has to earn their place. That's rarer than anyone admits, and it was never a consolation prize.

## yours

1. In the 1890s, a Chicago saloon keeper named Williams was a guest at a summer house in Cohasset, Massachusetts, when the party agreed that New England made the best punch. Williams wasn't satisfied with the verdict. As it's usually told, he wired his partner, Tom Newman, to make a new punch and ship it by the keg, express. It came, and the room changed its mind.

2. And here's the twist. Chicago's answer to New England was made with New England rum, from Medford, a few miles north of Boston. Whether Newman meant anything by it, nobody can say: plenty of American bars poured that rum then. But I like to think that crowd was won over by something they'd known all along, in a glass they didn't expect.

3. Then it won Chicago. It was bottled, and its recipe printed, but the regulars agreed neither did it justice. They wanted it as the bar served it: stirred at length, over a piece of peach soaked in brandy until soft. Later owners put up a neon lighthouse that read "Home of Cohasset Punch", and the drink stayed for about ninety years.

4. A whole city felt at home with this drink, and it only began because one guest wouldn't let the matter rest. The cocktail I've made for you is the Cohasset Punch, a Chicago classic: rum aged until it turns gold and sweet vermouth (red wine with herbs, lightly sweetened) in equal parts, lemon pressed for its juice, a little syrup, orange bitters. It's stirred for a full minute, as that bar always did. The peach starts as an everyday tin. You cut it small and leave it overnight in brandy, so it becomes the soft, soaked slice they got at the bar, not the half peach the printed recipe gave. I did it that way for the people who could tell the difference, and because something familiar, given a night's care, is worth coming back for.

5. Williams was a guest in someone else's house. He could simply have agreed. Instead he sent for something better, and let the room decide. When you next notice you're someone's easy option, don't be gracious about it. Let them see you'd like to be first. Whoever picks you then won't be settling. They'll know exactly what they've got, and I hope you let yourself enjoy being chosen.
