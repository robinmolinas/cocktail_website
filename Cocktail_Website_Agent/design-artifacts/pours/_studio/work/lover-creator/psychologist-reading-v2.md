## whoYouAre

Friends show you things first: the song before it has words, the first chapter, the dish they've never cooked for anyone else. Something in the way you pay attention makes them want to finish it, and often they do. People are braver with their own work when you're around, and that's a rare thing to give. Sometimes you even turn up in it: a line in the song, a character who talks the way you talk.

You make things too, and there's usually something of yours, half done, that nobody has seen. It's lovely to be someone's reason, and it feels safer than being judged on your own work. But the you in their songs is their idea of you, and an idea is easy to love from a distance. People have made beautiful things out of you. Very few have asked what you're making.

## yours

1. In the 1920s, an American couple, Gerald and Sara Murphy, had a favourite little beach near Antibes, and soon Picasso, Hemingway and the Fitzgeralds were on it with them. When F. Scott Fitzgerald wrote *Tender Is the Night*, he drew its main couple partly from the Murphys. And Gerald had a line about his cocktails: they were "just the juice of a few flowers". It sounds like a charming man's joke. Maybe that's all it was.

2. But among the Murphys' papers at Yale there's a card in Gerald's handwriting, headed "Juice of A Few Flowers". An ounce of orange, an ounce of grapefruit, half an ounce each of lime and lemon, an ounce of gin, shaken, in a glass with its rim wet with lemon and dipped in coarse sugar. He was a painter in his own right, too. Seven of his fourteen paintings survive, and in one of them, *Cocktail*, the lid of a cigar box alone took him four months.

3. Everyone kept the charming version of him: the man in the novel, the lovely line. The exact part, the half-ounces and the four months, was his, and you have to go looking for it. I like to think that card is what he'd most have liked someone to ask him about. I suspect you have a card like that too.

4. The cocktail I've made for you keeps every one of his measures, and his sugar on the rim. But sugar on the rim sweetens your lips, not the drink, so it needed a little more, inside. I made that sweetness with mint, from a cocktail he called the Bailey. In a letter, he wrote that he'd invented it, "as were a great many other good things". So this drink comes from two of his recipes, and joining them is my idea: I put the mint in as a nod to that sentence, where he simply says he made it. It's light, bright and properly sharp, four fresh juices and a little gin, and it fills two small glasses.

5. So the next time a friend shows you what they're working on, show them something of yours. Not the polished version, and not so they'll praise it: the unfinished one, in your own hand. Let one person see the maker, not the reason. That's how you get known up close. And since hardly anyone asks, I will: what are you making?

**closingLine** (Tomás's to own; my proposal)
*Pour both glasses. Hand one over with something you've made, and say you made it.*
Why: his draft's "and let them ask about it" is the third ask in a row (whoYouAre's last line, y5's last line, then this one). It also leaves the guest waiting to be asked, which is the fear. "Say you made it" is the guest's own act, and it's what Murphy did in the letter. Against *Four Shares* ("Ladle it out, and keep one cup back for yourself"): that's a bowl for many with one cup kept back. This is one glass handed to one person with something of your own. Different gesture, pass. It needs no partner: "hand one over" is to anyone.

## Resonance

**As the guest.** The first scene is mine: someone shows me the first chapter before anyone else. "People have made beautiful things out of you" is the "how did they know" line. The new sentence, "something of yours, half done, that nobody has seen", is the private one, and it stings the right amount. y4 now does something v1 couldn't: the one thing the bartender added is a man's plain sentence that he made something. That's exactly what I don't say. y5 and the closing line ask me to say it.

**As the sceptic.**
- *Barnum:* v1's weakest pair is fixed. The half-done thing nobody has seen is concrete, and it hangs on "you make things too", which comes from the persona.
- *Swap test:* the same passes as v1 (Sex Symbol, Romantic, Geisha, Visionary, Sculptor). The closing line against *Four Shares* passes (see above).
- *History weight:* y1–y2 and half of y4 are history, against whoYouAre, y3, half of y4 and y5 for the person. About half, and no more. y2 is shorter than v1's to pay for y4's Bailey clause.
- *Twist tied in words:* "I put the mint in as a nod to that sentence" (Robin's caregiver-batch rule).
- *Drink worth having:* "light, bright and properly sharp", with no modest words. The OUT is owned in Checks, not in the reading.
- *Risk:* y4 is the longest paragraph and carries three moves (rim, mint, two glasses). If Hester's audit adds words, cut "four fresh juices and a little gin" first.

**On the OUT:** I accept that it closes flagged for Robin. My case for him: the numbers are out because his hand is three parts juice to one of gin, and the reading promises every one of his measures. Pulling the drink into the sour's ranges would make it our idea of him, which is the one thing this person fears. Light and sharp is his.

**Verdict:** *ready*, pending Hester's audit of the three flagged rows ("in Gerald's handwriting", the rim wording, the Bailey sentence).
