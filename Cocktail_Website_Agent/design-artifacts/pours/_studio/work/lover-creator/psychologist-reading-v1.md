## whoYouAre

Friends show you things first: the song before it has words, the first chapter, the dish they've never cooked for anyone else. Something in the way you pay attention makes them want to finish it, and often they do. People are braver with their own work when you're around, and that's a rare thing to give. Sometimes you even turn up in it: a line in the song, a character who talks the way you talk.

You make things too. You just don't often say so. It's lovely to be someone's reason, and it feels safer than being judged on your own work. But the you in their songs is their idea of you, and an idea is easy to love from a distance. People have made beautiful things out of you. Very few have asked what you're making.

## yours

1. In the 1920s, an American couple, Gerald and Sara Murphy, found a little beach near Antibes, and soon Picasso, Hemingway and the Fitzgeralds were on it with them. When F. Scott Fitzgerald wrote *Tender Is the Night*, he drew its main couple partly from the Murphys. And Gerald had a line about his cocktails: they were "just the juice of a few flowers". It sounds like a charming man's joke. Maybe that's all it was.

2. But among the Murphys' papers at Yale there's a card in Gerald's handwriting, headed "Juice of A Few Flowers". An ounce of orange, an ounce of grapefruit, half an ounce each of lime and lemon, an ounce of gin. Shake it, and pour it into a glass whose rim you've wet with lemon and dipped in coarse sugar. He was a painter in his own right, too: he showed in Paris from 1923, and seven of his fourteen paintings survive. One, *Cocktail*, is a still life of a shaker, a glass, a cut lemon and a cigar box. The lid of the box alone took him four months.

3. Everyone kept the charming version of him: the man in the novel, the lovely line. The exact part, the half-ounces and the four months, was his, and you have to go looking for it. I like to think that card is what he'd most have liked someone to ask him about. I suspect you have a card like that too.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is that card, exactly as he wrote it. I didn't add a thing. It's his work, and I wanted you to have it the way he made it, not the way someone else would remember it, because that's what I'd want for you too. Four fresh juices and a little gin, light and bright, with the sugar waiting on the rim, so every sip starts sweet and ends sharp.

5. So the next time a friend shows you what they're working on, show them something of yours. Not the finished, polished version, and not so they'll praise it: the thing that's still half done, in your own hand. Let one person see the maker, not the reason. That's how you get known up close. And since hardly anyone asks, I will: what are you making?

## Resonance

**As the guest.** The whoYouAre opens on a scene I've lived: someone shows me the first chapter before anyone else. "People have made beautiful things out of you" is the "how did they know?" line. It names something flattering and lonely at once, which I'd never say about myself. Then "Very few have asked what you're making" catches the part I hide. The ending turns the bartender into the one person who does ask. That's the payoff, and it's the gift.

**As the sceptic.**
- *Barnum:* "You make things too. You just don't often say so" is true of many people. The context carries it (the half-made thing that's never shown, the safety of being the reason), but it's the weakest pair of sentences. Watch it.
- *Swap test:*
  - Sex Symbol: "made beautiful things out of you" could fit Monroe, but "what you're making" can't. The two sentences only work together.
  - Romantic: writes the long letter, but isn't made into other people's work. Pass.
  - Geisha: owns the evenings, "What you'd never tell them", "Here's what people take home" and "That's where I see you in him". None are used, and y3 has a different shape.
  - Visionary: "people want to help build it". Here friends make their own things. Pass.
  - Sculptor: making for one person. Here it's their own work. Pass.
- *Phrases grepped against every pour:* "from a distance" and "braver" appear only in caregiver-outlaw's notes, and "the maker" only in checks tables. None are in readings.
- *History weight:* y1–y2 are history, y3–y5 are the person. Under half, if y2 stays this length.
- *Risk:* y4's "because that's what I'd want for you too" leans on the ending. If it reads as a lesson, cut the clause.
