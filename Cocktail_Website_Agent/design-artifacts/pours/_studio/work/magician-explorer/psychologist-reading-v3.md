## whoYouAre

Right now, something you've made is nearly there. A recipe, a piece of writing, a shelf, a plan. Everyone who's seen it says it's done. Only you can tell what's still off, so you change it, bit by bit, and you note each change so you can always find your way back.

You'll do a thing forty ways without complaint, because every one of them can still be undone. It's the last step you put off: the moment it's served, sent, signed or said, and can't be taken back. So it waits, sometimes long after it's ready. What you hide even better is the hope. People see someone careful, a little sceptical, who wants to see proof. Underneath, you believe almost anything can become something better, and you want that to be true more than you'd ever say before it works. That's a gift, rarer than you'd guess: give you something everyone else has written off, and in time you'll hand back a better one. The people close to you start to believe things can get better, because they've seen you make it so.

## yours

1. Aeneas Coffey spent twenty-four years in Ireland's excise, the service that taxed spirits and chased the illegal stills. He started at the lowest grade and rose to inspector-general of excise in Ireland. Then, in 1824, at forty-four, he left and bought a distillery in Dublin.

2. His first still was made of wood and iron, a single column. It was soon replaced by two columns of copper. The new still was simpler and sturdier than the design it improved on, cheaper to heat, and it made a stronger, purer spirit from grain, in one continuous run. Ireland's biggest distillers scorned it; as late as 1878 they were printing that these things made whiskey no more than they made wine or beer. Scotland took it up, from 1834 on, and it went on to change the whole British spirits industry.

3. I couldn't say why he began in wood and iron. I like to think it was where he could still change his mind, and the copper was where he stopped. And notice the order: he'd left the excise first, years before any patent. That's the step you find hardest. Not the forty tries. The copper.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Martini built on single grain Scotch whisky, the spirit stills like his still make in Scotland. It's lighter than malt whisky, and in a mixed drink it can easily vanish, so I built this one for it to lead. It's most of what you pour, stirred very cold with a blanc vermouth, pale and gently sweet, and a spoonful each of syrup and calvados, the apple brandy of Normandy, with lemon oil on top. Apple isn't what anyone expects beside grain, and I put it there as a nod to what you do with ordinary things. Clean, bright and quietly surprising.

5. When something you've made is nearly there again and your hand goes back for one more change, ask who the change is for. If it's for the thing, make it. If it's for you, put it in copper: serve it, send it, say it. A result you can't take back is also one nobody can take away from you. And then, at last, you get to enjoy it finished.
