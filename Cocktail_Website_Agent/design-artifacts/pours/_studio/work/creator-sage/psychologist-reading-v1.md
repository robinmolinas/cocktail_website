**Tagline candidates (the person)**

**whoYouAre**
People bring you what they've made so you can tell them it's right. Often it is, and you say so. But alone with something you made years ago, the one people still quote back to you, you keep asking whether it could be better. Not because it failed. Because it didn't, and everyone stopped looking.

What you dread isn't being wrong. It's settling into being right, becoming the way it's always been done, with everyone too respectful to mention it. So you question it yourself, first. And because you'll do that to your own best work, the people around you feel free to do it to theirs.

**yours**

1. In 1862 a bartender called Jerry Thomas published *How to Mix Drinks*, the first book to give the cocktail a section of its own: thirteen recipes. In 1876 the book came out again, and Thomas had gone back to his own cocktails. He added three new ones and called them "Improved". He swapped the orange liqueur for a cherry one, and he added a dash of absinthe, the fashionable new bottle of the day.

2. He didn't tear out the old recipes. The plain ones stayed in the book, and the improved ones were printed beside them. The best historian of his drinks calls the change "a subtle one". It outlasted him, though: that same historian reads the Sazerac, one of the most famous drinks New Orleans ever made, as Thomas's improved whiskey cocktail under another name.

3. I like to think nobody would have blamed him for leaving it alone. It was his book, and it worked. That's what I see in you. You'd rather ask the hard question about your own work than wait for someone else to find the courage. And you never throw the old one away. You put the new one next to it, so anyone can see what changed, and why.

4. So yours is built on his improved whiskey cocktail: rye, a little sugar, orange liqueur where he first had it, bitters, stirred cold. And where he put his dash of the new bottle, there's our own change, a spoonful of green Chartreuse, the herbal liqueur monks still make. The shape is his. The correction is ours, in the same place he made his.

5. Here's where I'd take a stand. Take the thing you're best known for, the one everyone says not to touch, and change one thing in it. Don't replace it. Show the two side by side, and let people choose. You'll learn more from the ones who choose the old one.
