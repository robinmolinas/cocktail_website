## whoYouAre

People bring you what they've made so you can tell them it's right. Often it is, and you say so. But alone with something you made years ago, the one people still quote back to you, you keep asking whether it could be better. Not because it failed. Because it didn't, and everyone stopped looking.

What you dread isn't being wrong. It's settling into being right, becoming the way it's always been done, with everyone too respectful to mention it. So you question it yourself, first. And because you'll do that to your own best work, the ones who work with you feel free to do it to theirs.

## yours

1. In 1862 a bartender called Jerry Thomas published *How to Mix Drinks*, the first book to give the cocktail a section of its own: thirteen recipes. In 1876 the book came out again, and Thomas had gone back to his own cocktails. He added three new ones and called them "Improved". He swapped the orange liqueur for a cherry one, and he added a dash of absinthe, the fashionable bottle of the day.

2. He didn't tear out the old recipes. The plain ones stayed in the book, and the improved ones were printed beside them. The historian who knows his drinks best calls the change "a subtle one". It outlasted him, though. That historian reads the Sazerac, now New Orleans's official cocktail, as Thomas's improved whiskey cocktail under another name.

3. I like to think nobody would have blamed him for leaving it alone, since it was his book and it worked. That's what I see in you. You'd rather ask the hard question about your own work than wait for someone else to find the courage. And you never throw the old one away. You put the new one next to it, so anyone can see what changed, and why.

4. So this drink starts from his improved whiskey cocktail: rye, plain syrup, bitters, and half a teaspoon of the orange liqueur his first cocktails used, kept beside the new. It's shaken hard with ice and strained into a small chilled glass whose rim has been wiped with lemon. There's one real change, and it sits where he made his. Instead of his dash of absinthe, a full teaspoon of green Chartreuse, a herbal liqueur still made under Carthusian monks, to a recipe only a few of them know, and one that American cocktail books only took up in the 1880s. His change was a whisper. This one you can taste.

5. Here's where I'd take a stand. Take the thing you're best known for, the one everyone says not to touch, and change one thing in it. Don't replace it. Show the two side by side, and let people choose. You'll learn the most from the ones who choose the old one.
