## whoYouAre

The oven gives up an hour before twelve people arrive. Someone says the evening's ruined, someone else starts looking up takeaways, and you're already at the open fridge, asking what you've got. You'd never wish it on anyone. But when the plan breaks and every face turns to you, a small part of you lights up. You keep that quiet. It hardly seems fair to enjoy someone else's emergency.

The bit you never mention is the blank before the answer comes. They're all looking at you, and there's nothing there yet. That blank is your nightmare, and somehow the whole fun of it too, because a puzzle that couldn't beat you wouldn't be worth solving. So you keep a level face through it, and no one learns how close it felt. And here's what it does for everyone else: the moment you ask what there is to work with, the room stops panicking and starts looking. Everyone gets something to do with their hands, and a little of your nerve. That's a real gift, and most people never notice who started it.

## yours

1. In the 1890s, Jennings Cox ran an iron-mining company near Daiquirí, a port town in the east of Cuba. The family story goes that he had some American visitors to serve, and found he'd run out of gin. He did have rum, from a distillery in Santiago, under thirty miles away. But he worried, the family says, that it wouldn't suit the Americans' taste.

2. So, in the family's telling, he picked limes from a tree in his own garden, added their juice and some sugar to soften the rum, and stirred it all in a pitcher with shaved ice. It went down well at once, the accounts say. One of his engineers later remembered it as more of a group effort among Cox and his crew, and maybe it was. People in the Caribbean had been mixing rum, limes and sugar for two centuries by then, but the Daiquiri, a renowned classic today, goes back to that pitcher: the *Oxford Companion* says he made the first one, or at least oversaw its making. And for years afterwards, the old newspaper accounts say, he simply called it his rum sour, and kept taking it to the bars of Santiago.

3. I like to think nobody at his table saw the worry the family remembered. They saw a man with a pitcher and a handful of limes. I recognise you in that: the answer arrives with a level face, and the doubt stays behind it. And then there's the name. He'd solved a problem with people watching, and for years he went on handing the answer out as plain "rum sour", no fuss at all. Whether he enjoyed that day isn't known, but I like to think he did. You'd do the same: you hand the answer over plainly, and nobody hears how much you loved finding it.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is that pitcher, for six: a Daiquiri in the shape the family story gives it. White rum and fresh lime, with sugar stirred into the lime until it's gone, swizzled on crushed ice for thirty seconds and poured straight away into six small chilled tumblers, so nobody waits longer than anyone else. The cocktail books say a Daiquiri always asks for some quick thinking, because no two limes are equally sour. I've measured everything a measure can fix, and the limes are yours to judge. My only addition is four makrut lime leaves, pressed into the lime and lifted out before you pour. Makrut is a different lime, grown for its fragrant leaves rather than its juice, so the answer comes from the part of the tree you'd never think to squeeze. The leaves bring lime's scent without making the drink any sharper. They're there as one more answer to his worry, and as a nod to the question you always ask first: what else is there? It's icy, bright and fresh, and the lime carries right through it.

5. So this is what I'd wish for you. When every face next turns to you and nothing has come yet, tell them the truth, smiling: this is the bit you love most. Watch what that does. They stop waiting for you to produce an answer and start hunting with you, and the blank isn't yours alone any more. It becomes a game the whole room is playing, and you can love it in the open.
