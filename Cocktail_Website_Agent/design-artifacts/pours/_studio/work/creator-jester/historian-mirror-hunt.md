# Mirror hunt: The Surrealist (creator-jester), round 1 — Hester

Person (persona.py): sees from a different angle; turns the ordinary into the extraordinary; provocative, dreamlike, playful. Fear: status quo, mediocrity (Creator) and boredom, monotony (Jester). Goal: innovation, pleasure, originality.

The shape I hunted for: **an ordinary thing made strange on purpose, and then made ordinary again by people who copied the recipe and missed the point.** That second half is the fear.

Traps from the family (Off-Label, round 2): literal artists. Dalí is the persona's example; nothing in the library ties him to a drink worth telling (Joy's "Passion of Dalí", pdf 360, is a branded Rob Roy variation named after him, and the Rob Roy is already the Connoisseur's). Kept out.

## A (my pick): the Knickebein and "Professor" Leo Engel (drink + person)
**Story.** In 1878 Leo Engel, who ran the American bar in a new theatre-restaurant in Piccadilly and was billed as "Professor", wrote down a drink that came with stage directions: a whole egg yolk floating on three liqueurs, under a cap of whipped white, to be smelled, then the froth drawn off in one breath, then a third drunk without touching the yolk, and finally the head thrown back and the rest swallowed, yolk breaking in the mouth (Oxford, KNICKEBEIN pdf 1137; ENGEL, LEO pdf 722). He didn't invent it (a San Francisco saloon was advertising it in 1869), but he wrote the performance down. Later books shook the same ingredients into an ordinary flip, which Oxford calls a mistake that keeps the flavours and loses the idea (pdf 1137).

**Why it mirrors.** The drinker is the performer: the ordinary (an egg, a glass of sweet liqueurs) becomes an event with acts and pauses. And the thing that nearly killed it is exactly the Surrealist's fear: the copyists kept the recipe and flattened the strangeness into something normal. Jester side: the menu it sat on had Galvanic Lip Pouters and Magnetic Crushers (Joy pdf 41).

**Spark for Tomás.** Oxford gives the physics: any liqueurs work if they're denser than the yolk, about 1.025 g/ml (pdf 1137); a 1913 German book already listed fourteen versions. So the riff is licensed by the record. What must survive the riff is the unbroken thing and the order of the acts, not the three liqueurs.

**Sources.** Oxford KNICKEBEIN pdf 1137–1138, ENGEL, LEO pdf 722–724, POUSSE CAFÉ pdf 1557; *Joy* pdf 41–42. Card: `_studio/fact-cards/knickebein-leo-engel.md` (F1–F15, C1–C4).

**Risks.** Not his invention (say "wrote down"). "Didn't catch on" (Joy) is contested by the 1913 fourteen; hold to "little known in America" (Boothby 1908). "Four different sensations" is Boothby's phrase, not Engel's. The name's meaning ("knee bend", Oxford) has no story behind it on record. Raw yolk and white: egg, not veto-free, and a food-safety line for the Checks. A floating yolk is older (Jerry Thomas's Pousse l'Amour, 1862), so never "the first".

## B (runner-up): Eben Freeman's Royal Blush (drink + person)
**Story.** Freeman, a would-be actor and a sculptor's son, worked at WD-50 translating the kitchen's techniques into drinks (Proper p. 229–230). His Royal Blush was a Gimlet with cherry purée floated on top; when he added Champagne it turned from green to red. In his words it became a molecular cocktail "unintentionally", and it made the restaurant a lot of money (Proper pp. 230–231).

**Why it mirrors.** Play and accident as invention; a drink that changes in front of you. **Why second:** the turn is a happy accident (closer to the Explorer's finding-out) and it rests on one source, one page; Freeman already sits in the Off-Label dossier (the fat-washing lead). No card yet: I'll write one if the room wants it.

## Considered, not offered
- **Tony Conigliaro, 69 Colebrooke Row** (Proper pp. 226–229): art-history and painting student who built a bar like a gallery and a stage set where "the customer was the main feature". Lovely line, but it's a person without a drink turn in our library, and it's close to the Visionary's shape.
- **The Pousse Café** (Oxford pdf 1557–1558): layers kept apart by density, built up to eleven or fourteen as showpieces. Floating and gravity-defying, but it's the bartender's trick, not the drinker's; the Knickebein is its stranger child.

## Round 2 addendum
- **Knickebein withdrawn.** Wren's "no theatre" is right: its stage directions are exactly theatre, and the drinker performs rather than sees. Kept as a card.
- **New pick: the Black Velvet** (`fact-cards/black-velvet.md`). Champagne and stout, first recorded in the 1830s among German students as the "Philanthropist". Outside Germany it was called strange and almost unbelievable, stayed a curiosity until the 1920s, then became fashionable, and it's still poured at brunches (Oxford, BLACK VELVET pdf 281). Oxford's beer entry: it sounds worrying but can be very pleasant (pdf 249). Two things from opposite ends of the bar that met and stuck, and the "weird" wore off (after about ninety years). Legend caught: the Prince Albert mourning story isn't in our books, and the 1830s record rules it out as the origin. Bismarck and Rhodes: dossier only.
- **Tomás's Casanova** (`fact-cards/dali-casanova.md`): sourced, secondary. Popova (*The Marginalian*, 2014) quotes the book (orange, Campari, ginger, brandy, Vieille Cure, cayenne, 30 min in the freezer, OJ last, for "an excess of sobriety"). Taschen confirms the book (1973, 136 recipes, many from Paris restaurant chefs). Guards: "printed in Dalí's cookbook", never his invention; Vieille Cure is a herbal liqueur, not old brandy. The book is a gap for Robin if we use it.

## Round 3: one story — the tomato (Velvet to runner-up)
- **Sourced** (`fact-cards/tomato.md`): *Nix v. Hedden*, 149 U.S. 304 (10 May 1893, Justice Gray; opinion via Cornell LII). The Nix family imported West Indies tomatoes in 1886, paid the 10% vegetable duty under protest; evidence = dictionaries read aloud and two produce sellers; holding: botanically a fruit of the vine like cucumbers, beans and peas, but in the everyday speech of sellers and buyers a vegetable, because it's served with the soup, fish or meat and not as dessert. *Flavor Matrix* pdf 52: botanically the tomato is a berry, the strawberry is not. Matrix pdf 244: strawberries are the tomato's substitute. Oxford TOMATO JUICE pdf 2040: 1917, French Lick, a chef out of orange juice served tomato juice (dossier).
- **Weighing.** Velvet: two things that met and stuck, but they're outside the glass, and its people (Bismarck, Rhodes, the sporting crowd) are loud. Tomato: the odd true thing *is* the ingredient in Tomás's glass; the Court conceded the botany and filed it by where it sits at dinner; and the fact that sounds made up (tomato a berry, strawberry not) is the guest's "this is going to sound weird, but…", true a minute later. Pick: tomato. Velvet = runner-up card.
- **Guards.** "For the tariff" always; the Court said both. "Botany notwithstanding" is ours (not in the opinion). No poison legends. Not "the first tomato cocktail" (Oxford pdf 2039).
