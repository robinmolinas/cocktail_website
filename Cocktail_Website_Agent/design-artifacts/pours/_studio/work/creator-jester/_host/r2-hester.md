Here's the true story that sounds made up: **the Black Velvet**, champagne and stout, two things from opposite ends of the bar. It first turns up in the 1830s as a drink of German students, and they called it the "Philanthropist" (Oxford, BLACK VELVET pdf 281). Outside Germany people found it strange and almost unbelievable. It stayed a curiosity until the 1920s, then became fashionable in London, and it's still poured wherever there's a fancy brunch (same entry). Oxford's beer entry says it sounds worrying at first but can be very pleasant (BEER pdf 249). That's your minute in which it stops sounding weird, though here the minute lasted about ninety years. I've also caught a legend. The famous London story, that it was made to mourn Prince Albert in 1861, isn't in any of our books, and the 1830s record rules it out as the origin. Bismarck and Rhodes both drank it, and both stay in the dossier. Nobody recorded why it was called the Philanthropist, so any joke we make about the name has to be signposted.
