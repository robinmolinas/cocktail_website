## whoYouAre

You've started a hundred sentences with "this is going to sound weird, but…". Then you say what you saw: the thing that looks like something else, two ideas nobody thought belonged together. There's a pause, then a laugh. About a minute later, it doesn't sound weird at all.

None of it is a performance. You're just saying what's there. You laugh along, but you meant it, and it stings a little when it's taken as only a joke. So the strangest things you see, you mostly don't say. What you can't stand is the sensible version: an idea sanded down until it fits in. What you give people is a second look at things they thought they knew.

## yours

In 1886, four men named Nix brought tomatoes into New York from the West Indies. They paid the tax on vegetables, under protest, because fruit came in free, and sued to get the money back. At the trial, both sides read dictionaries aloud. The importers looked up "fruit", "vegetables" and "tomato"; the other side looked up "pea", "cucumber" and "squash". Two men who'd sold produce for thirty years gave evidence.

The case reached the US Supreme Court, and in 1893 it answered. Botanically, the Court said, the tomato is the fruit of a vine. But the people who buy and sell food call it a vegetable, because it's served with the soup, the fish or the meat, not as dessert. So for the tariff, it was a vegetable. And botany goes further than the Court did: the tomato is, botanically, a berry. The strawberry isn't.

My guess is you'd have skipped the dictionaries. You'd have looked at it, red, its seeds kept on the inside the way a berry keeps them, and served it for dessert. It's what you do with everything: you go by what a thing is, not by where it usually sits.

So this is a daiquiri (rum, lime and sugar, shaken), made the way you'd make a strawberry one, with the tomato in the strawberry's seat. The syrup is made the way a strawberry syrup is: ripe cherry tomatoes blended cold with sugar, plus a vanilla pod, because vanilla and tomato, surprisingly, go well together. It tastes of ripe red fruit and vanilla first, and only then of an idea. There's no trick in it. It's the tomato, served where it looked like it belonged.

You apologise in advance for things you'll turn out to be right about. Drop the "this is going to sound weird". It gives people a way to laugh it off before they've looked. Say what you saw, plainly, the first time. Then let them catch up. They usually do, about a minute later.

**Closing line (pick):** *Tell them it's tomato before they taste it.* (Tomás's alternative.) It carries the reading's position in the drink's own words: no warning, no reveal, say the strange thing first. Tomás's lean, *Serve it for dessert*, is already in yours 3 as my guess about the guest; as the closing line it would repeat the reading rather than hand over the position (Minimalist lesson: the closing line agrees with the position).
