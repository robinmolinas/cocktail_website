# Persona card: The Game-Changer (sage-magician)

Wren, round 1, 2026-10-02. Sources: `persona.py sage-magician`, the Sage plan row, Oxford JACKSON pdf 1091–1092 read whole, the registry, sibling and near-sibling pours (grepped).

## Who this is
The source line is "they study a problem until they see what everyone else missed, then make it so simple it seems obvious", and they want that knowledge "to change how people live". What frightens them is "a revolution built on a flawed idea", getting it wrong at scale. Drivers: leave a mark, seek paradise.

Every Sage fears deception. This one has it the other way round. The Connoisseur, the Philosopher and the Genius are afraid of being fooled. The Game-Changer is afraid of fooling other people, by accident and at scale. A small mistake stays in your own life. A mistake that changes how other people do things spreads through everyone who believed you.

## The one true thing (no sibling has it)
**Most people worry that nobody will listen. You worry that everyone will.**

Their ideas are meant to spread, so the weight they carry is the weight of being believed. That's why they keep working until the idea is simple. It isn't polish and it isn't showing off. A simple idea is one that other people can pick up, use and see through for themselves, so they aren't just following. What they give people isn't an answer to accept. It's a new way of looking that people can then use on their own.

## What they hide
- The doubt that comes before every big change: "what if I've got this wrong, and they all do it anyway?" Outside, it looks like confidence and clarity. Inside, it's a lot of checking.
- How much they want it to last (leave a mark). Wanting that feels a bit vain, so they talk about the idea and never about themselves.
- (I'm deliberately not using the line "it looks effortless and it isn't". *Not Too Polite*, *Nothing to It* and *Half a Rim* already own it.)

## Fear, and the gift it becomes
- **Fear:** being followed into a mistake. Being the one who misled people, not the one who was misled.
- **Gift (said out loud, per Robin's batch-caregiver rule):** they give people a way of seeing, not something to obey. Once someone shows you the pattern, you can find your own way. That's a kindness: it makes other people capable, and it doesn't make them dependent.
- **Possible "this is me" line:** "You never asked anyone to believe you. You showed them how to look."

## The position (Ryu's order: earned, at the end)
Because they're afraid of being followed, they hold things back until they're sure, and they never feel sure enough. The position is in their interest: **trust the people who'll follow.** If you've shown them how to look, they're following with their eyes open, not following you blind. So put it out. Wording is for the reading. Avoid these taken verbs and frames: "tell/say" (closing lines), "let them check" (*Are You Quite Sure?*), "show the unfinished thing" (*In Your Own Hand*), "throw it anyway" (*Anyway*), "trust the others' hours" (*Anyone Would Have*).

## Traps
- **Steve Jobs and keynotes.** No stage, no phone, no "think different", no "disrupt". No job labels (founder, inventor, visionary).
- **Grandiosity.** Most guests haven't changed the world. Their scale is their own: how a team, a family or a group of friends does something. The fear works at any size. Write the scene small.
- **Flattery.** "You see what others miss" is a compliment. Recognise the weight instead.
- **The lone inventor.** Jackson didn't invent beer styles from nothing. Oxford says he introduced "the idea of beer styles". Never "he invented tasting" or "he invented styles out of nowhere".
- **The singer.** One plain clause owns the name ("not the singer"). No joke.
- **Health.** Jackson's Parkinson's, the "I Am Not Drunk" quote and "tasting more and drinking less" are all on the illness page (pdf 1092). They stay in the dossier only. It's tempting, because "people think I am drunk. I am not" is literally a deception fear, but that's exactly why it's off limits: it would turn his illness into a mirror for the guest.
- **Barnard** is the Scientist's: never named. **"Rated every single malt"** is out (the Scientist's every-distillery shape).

## Story test for the lead (Jackson)
1. One clear insight: beer "styles", the groupings and relationships (1977); then the relationships among the world's whisky types (1987). **Passes on the page** (pdf 1091).
2. It changed how many people drink: his influence on "writers, readers, and drinkers", and on distillers and brewers. **Passes.**
3. It's taken for granted now: "seem common and obvious today is perhaps the greatest tribute". **Passes.** We spend the word once (see the Innovator trap).
4. The risk or the check, and the "before". **The "before" is on the page:** the 1977 book was "notable for its thorough and serious approach to beer", so beer hadn't been treated that way. **The risk isn't on the page.** It's a motive, so a signposted "I like to think he worried…" is allowed. Hester, please look once for his own words on doubt or on styles being only a starting point. If you find them, (4) becomes fact.
5. **Mirror of the one true thing:** a style is a way of seeing that readers then use on their own. Nobody has to memorise his verdicts. That fits "showed them how to look". The fit is interpretation, and it gets signposted.

## Ruling on the plan row
**Accept, with conditions.** Tomás builds drink v1 in round 2.
- Tell Jackson through the styles (1977) and the map of how whiskies relate (1987), never through the 1990 ratings.
- The one true thing is fear of being believed and followed. "Obvious" is not the hinge (the Innovator owns that).
- The illness material stays in the dossier.
- (4) is either found or signposted.
- **For Tomás:** the plan's direction is fine (an Old-Fashioned with two whiskies from two countries; no single malt, no blended Scotch, never Scotch with Irish; bourbon or rye only as one half). A person condition: the pair should be a *relationship* the guest gets from one plain clause, something that sounds surprising and then makes sense ("cousins from two countries"), so the drink acts out "a new way of looking". Don't build a gesture where the guest tastes each half first: that's *In a Minute*'s closing and the Prodigy's "let them check". The drink needs a spark beyond sugar, bitters and water, tied to the story in words.
- The backup (Gay-Lussac) is fine as a backup. It's weaker on the person, because a measurement standard is a rule, not a way of seeing.
