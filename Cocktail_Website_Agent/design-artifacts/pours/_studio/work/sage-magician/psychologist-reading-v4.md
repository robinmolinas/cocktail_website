## whoYouAre

You've seen how something could work better: the way the team runs its week, how the family shares out the holidays, the fix a friend has needed for years. You can see it whole. And before you say a word, you walk back through it, more than once, until you can explain it in a sentence. From outside it looks like confidence. It's closer to care. You know that if they like it, they'll actually do it.

A no is easy. What keeps you up is the yes: everyone's, and then finding out you were wrong. A mistake you make alone stays with you. A mistake people take up because of you travels with every one of them.

So you never hand anyone an answer to obey. You give them a way of seeing it, plain enough to use without you, and to notice if you've slipped. You never asked anyone to believe you. You showed them how to look. That's generosity, and not the usual kind: people leave you more capable, not more in need of you.

## yours

1. In 1977 an English journalist called Michael Jackson (no relation to the singer) published a guide to the world's beer. He'd started at sixteen, writing about pubs for weekly papers in West Yorkshire. His book took beer thoroughly and seriously, and it brought in an idea: beer "styles". Pale ale, porter, pilsner, bock, India pale ale: groups of beers, and how each one relates to the others.

2. Ten years later he did the same for whisky, with a book that showed how the world's whisky types are related. Before then, apart from a handful of earlier books, publishers hadn't imagined anyone would want to read more about whisky than a label. His influence reached writers, readers and drinkers, and brewers and distillers too, many of whom counted him an inspiration, or simply a friend. Perhaps the best tribute to him is that his kind of book, new in its day, now seems obvious.

3. Did it ever keep him up at night? I can't say. But I like to think it did, and that it's why his big idea was a way of grouping, not a verdict: with a style in your head, you can place a beer you've never tasted, by yourself. I see you in that. You don't want people simply following you. You want them to see what you saw, and carry on from there without you.

4. So the cocktail I've made for you is a renowned whiskey classic, an Old-Fashioned, with its whisky drawn from two countries. Canadians often call their whisky "rye", a name from the days when it was wheat whisky flavoured with rye; today most of it is made mainly from corn, usually with a little rye for flavour. American rye whiskey must be at least half rye. Same name, the grains usually swapped, side by side in one glass. To honour his guide to how whiskies relate, I used a guide of my own, a chart of which flavours go together, and looked for a flavour that goes with both grains. I found tamarind, dark and sour, and it gives the drink the sourness it needs to balance. Stirred with ice in a jar, it's strained into a frozen glass with none, and finished with oil pressed from orange peel.

5. Let me be honest with you about one habit. You keep the next idea back until you're sure, and you'll never feel quite sure enough. But look at what you've already done to it: you've made it simple enough to see through. People who take up an idea like that aren't following blindly. So share it before it's big. A flaw found among a few friends costs a laugh. And if there's no flaw, you get what you were after all along: people doing something better, their own way, because of what you showed them.
