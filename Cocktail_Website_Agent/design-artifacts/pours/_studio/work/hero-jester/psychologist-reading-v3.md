## whoYouAre

A flat tyre on the motorway, in the rain, with no signal. By Friday it's the best thing anybody's heard all month: there's a tow-truck driver who sang the whole way, a hailstone the size of a plum, and a lorry that people ask you to do again. All of it happened, more or less. You simply know which version deserves to be the one people keep.

What nobody at the table knows is that the bigger the story, the worse it really was. The forty minutes on the hard shoulder, cold, and sure nobody was coming, never make it in. Told plainly, a bad day might sound like weakness. Worse, it might sound like nothing much, and be forgotten by the weekend. So you tell it larger, until the bad day is the small thing in it. And here's what the people listening take away: they go home believing their own bad week will pass, and might even be funny one day. That's a real kindness, and it's why they keep asking for another.

## yours

1. Victor Bergeron lost a leg as a boy. Years later, when people knew him as Trader Vic, the man behind a chain of South Seas restaurants and the Mai Tai most people know, he liked to tell them that a shark had eaten it. As he got famous, plenty believed it. Maybe a shark really did. The odds are against it.

2. The plain version goes like this. He left school, then tried one job after another, selling paint and tyres among other things, and none of them lasted. (Yes, tyres. I didn't make that bit up.) In 1934, in his early thirties, he opened a barbecue shack in Oakland with $800 borrowed from an aunt, and ran it as owner, bartender and cook. One writer says that's where the nickname began, with his habit of trading meals for goods and services. Four years later the shack became Trader Vic's, and by the time he died there were more than twenty, across North America, Europe and Japan.

3. I like to think he knew the shark was the better line at a table. But look at the plain version again: the jobs that didn't last, the borrowed money, a cook paying his way in meals, and a name that grew out of it. None of it needs a shark. It's a terrific story as it stands, and it was his all along. So is your own. The version you tell is a gift, and it does people good. The one you leave out would hold the table just as well.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is his Mai Tai, the renowned classic. I built it on his own account of making it, which he dated to 1944: aged Jamaican rum, fresh lime, almond syrup, orange curaçao, a dash of sugar syrup, all shaken hard with shaved ice, then half the squeezed lime shell and a branch of fresh mint standing up out of the glass. This one keeps close to his formula, and the main change is mine. He used a French almond syrup. I make mine at home and toast the almonds to pale gold first, the way a cook would, as a nod to the cook he was before anyone called him Trader. I like to think the cook in him would have approved. It's golden and cold, sharp with lime first, then round and nutty underneath.

5. So keep telling them. The shark stays: people need it, and you're good at it. But once, tell one exactly as big as it was. I'd bet the table goes just as quiet. And what they'll remember afterwards won't be the shark. It'll be you.

**closing line:** Tomás's (final: *Take the almonds to gold before anything else: that's where the taste is. Next time someone asks how it went, give them what happened, nothing added.*). It agrees with y5's position, not the creed. y5 avoids "nothing added" so the two don't repeat each other.

## Rulings for the room

- **Pronouns (lint warnings, yours 1–4):** accepted. Every "he/him/his" is Trader Vic, a historical man; none is about the guest, and each sits next to his name or in his own paragraph.

- **Hester's F9 (the leg pulled out from under the table): dossier only.** It's lovely and it's his own joke. But it's a showman's move, pulling the leg out to surprise a guest, so it isn't really the plain telling my position needs. It's another performance. And it would put a prosthetic on the table as the gag, which a reader with one may not enjoy finding in their own reading. My rule stands: the mirror is the telling, never the leg.
- **F6, the nickname from trading meals: in**, as "one writer says". It's the position's proof in one word: the big name grew out of a plain habit.
- **The tyres coincidence:** owned in one clause, inside the parenthesis in y2.
- Facts I dropped on Hester's word: "sickly", the age, "about thirty-two" (now "in his early thirties"), any "good" vs "the best".

## Resonance test

The second lands the hidden gauge ("the bigger the story, the worse it really was") and then says the kindness out loud.
