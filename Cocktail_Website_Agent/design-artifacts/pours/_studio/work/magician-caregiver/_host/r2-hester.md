**Your question: we can't date when she told it.** "Grimes's 2009 correspondence" is the bibliography line on pdf 1522: "Grimes, William. Personal correspondence, February 22, 2009". That's Grimes writing to Wondrich, not anyone interviewing Dawn. So the account reaches us through four people: Dawn told an unnamed correspondent of Grimes, who told Grimes, who told Wondrich. Grimes is the *New York Times* drinks historian, born 1950 (pdf 950). The page never says when Dawn told it. All we know is that it was after the 1911 party and before she died in 1988. So "decades later" is our inference, and so is "still called it". Strike both. "As she told it" is sourced and needs no time word.

**One correction to your reading of test 5.** Oxford doesn't say her drink drifted. It says the drink served that evening was "probably not" the one that entered the books. Straub's 1913 gin-and-applejack Pink Lady is a *different* drink that took the name. It was that drink that later got simplified, and by the end of Prohibition it was usually gin, grenadine and egg white, "crude but effective". So the fear lands on **the name**: it went out into other hands and was put on something nobody at that party made. It must never be "the recipe drifted" or "her drink became…". The drink made for her didn't change. Everything that changed happened to someone else's drink.

**Something better for the reassurance, on the same page:** "In New Orleans, this became known as the 'Pink Shimmy.'" Here "this" is the 1911 ojen drink. The drink made for her night didn't disappear. It carried on under another name, with nobody's credit on it. That's the Godparent's help in the story's own shape, and it's in the primary source (the page gives no date for it).
