## whoYouAre

You notice what someone needs before they've said it, and you act before anyone asks. By the time their big day comes, it's done: the call made, the table booked, the right people quietly in the room. And you're at the back, or not there at all, because it was never meant to be about you.

What you'd never admit is the question that comes after: did I have the right? Help nobody asked for can land wrong, and you know it, so part of you is always listening for the moment a kindness turns out to be meddling. Most of the time it doesn't, because you only do it for people you've watched closely enough to know. So let me say plainly what you give. Help nobody has to thank you for is help nobody has to repay. You hand someone a moment that is completely their own, and not many people give like that.

## yours

1. In 1911 a musical called *The Pink Lady* opened at New York's New Amsterdam theatre, starring a young actress named Hazel Dawn. A drink was named after the show, and as she told it, it was first made for a surprise party in her honour at Murray's Roman Gardens on 42nd Street. The 1911 Pink Lady was a measure of ojen, a sweet Spanish anise liqueur, shaken with Peychaud's and Angostura bitters.
2. Who arranged that party, or who made the drink, isn't on record. And the name probably didn't stay with their drink: in 1913 a drinks book printed a Pink Lady made with gin and applejack, and that's the one that went into the recipe books. In other hands it got simpler and cruder, until by the end of Prohibition most bars made it with gin, grenadine and egg white.
3. That's the worry you carry: something kind you set up, taken out of your hands and put on something you'd never have made. But look at what lasted. The drink from that night didn't disappear. In New Orleans it carried on under another name, the Pink Shimmy. And I like to think it matters that, when Hazel Dawn told the story, she told it as a party in her honour: the name wandered off, and the night stayed hers. That's exactly what your kind of help is for.
4. The cocktail I've made for you starts from the Pink Lady of that night, not the one that went into the books: Spanish anise shaken with Peychaud's and Angostura bitters. I've changed one thing, as a nod to whoever made it first. Three quarters of the sweet anise is swapped for a dry one, with far less sugar. You won't see it. It just takes the edge off the sweetness, so what was a sweet liqueur with bitters becomes a proper cocktail, and nobody will thank it for any of that. It reaches the glass hazy, because anise clouds when it meets cold water, and pink from the bitters.
5. Keep doing it without asking. The people you do it for are the ones you know well enough. Only, when you've arranged someone's moment, don't leave before it arrives. A surprise is the one kind of help you can see land: the door opens, the room shouts, and for a second they can't speak. Be there for that second. That's how you'll know you had the right.
