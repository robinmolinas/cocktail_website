## whoYouAre

You notice what someone needs before they've said it, and you act before anyone asks. By the time their big day comes, it's done: the call made, the table booked, the right people quietly in the room. And you're at the back, or not there at all, because it was never meant to be about you.

What you'd never admit is the question that comes after: did I have the right? Help nobody asked for can land wrong, and you know it, so part of you is always listening for the moment a kindness turns out to be meddling. Most of the time it doesn't, because you only do it for people you've watched closely enough to know. So let me say plainly what you give. Help nobody has to thank you for is help nobody has to repay. You hand someone a moment that is completely their own, and not many people give like that.

## yours

1. In 1911 a musical called *The Pink Lady* opened at New York's New Amsterdam theatre, starring a young actress named Hazel Dawn. As she told it, there was a surprise party in her honour at Murray's Roman Gardens on 42nd Street, and a drink was made for it and called the Pink Lady, after the show: a measure of ojen, a sweet Spanish anise liqueur, shaken with Peychaud's and Angostura bitters.
2. Nobody wrote down who arranged that party, or who made the drink. And the name didn't stay with their drink. In 1913 a bartending book printed a Pink Lady made with gin and applejack, probably a different drink altogether, and that's the one that went into the recipe books. In other hands it got simpler and cruder, until by the end of Prohibition most bars made it with gin, grenadine and egg white.
3. I like to think that's the worry you carry: something kind you set up, taken out of your hands and put on something you'd never have made. But look at what lasted. The drink from that night didn't disappear. In New Orleans it carried on under another name, the Pink Shimmy. And the story Hazel Dawn told wasn't about who made her drink. It was about a surprise party in her honour. The name wandered off. The night stayed hers, and that's exactly what your kind of help is for.
4. The cocktail I've made for you starts from the drink of that night, the Pink Lady the books lost: Spanish anise shaken with Peychaud's and Angostura bitters. I've changed two things, both underneath, as a nod to whoever made it first. Part of the sweet anise is swapped for a dry one, the same spirit without the sugar, and there's a measure of fino, a pale, bone-dry sherry. You won't see either of them. Together they take the edge off the sweetness and turn a sweet little drink into a proper cocktail, and nobody will thank them for it. It reaches the glass cloudy, because anise clouds when it meets cold water, and pink from the bitters.
5. Keep doing it without asking. The people you do it for are the ones you know well enough. Only, when you've arranged someone's moment, don't leave before it arrives. A surprise is the one kind of help you can see land: the door opens, the room shouts, and for a second they can't speak. Be there for that second. That's how you'll know you had the right.
