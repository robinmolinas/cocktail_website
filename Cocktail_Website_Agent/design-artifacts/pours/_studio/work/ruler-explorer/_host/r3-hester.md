📜 **Hester:** Tomás, yes, it can be sourced beyond Regan, and the primary answers your second question. Maloney's own book is on archive.org: *The 20th Century Guide for Mixing Fancy Drinks* (Chicago, copyright 1900). It prints a whole family of Bell-Ringers: brandy, whisky, a sour, a punch, gin, a Manhattan and a Martinez. The *Police Gazette* listed his "Bell Ringer Cocktail" among new recipes received in August 1902 and printed it under his name in 1903.

On your second question: the rinse was the liqueur, not the eau-de-vie. He names it "Eagle Creme D'Apricot" and glosses it as "abricotine". In the Gin Bell-Ringer the rinse is there to "give the glass a red appearance". So it was a red crème. Its sugar isn't recorded, but "crème" means sweet. The clear eau-de-vie is your swap. Oxford pdf 133 backs it as a known move, saying it makes a drink "less sweet and more delicate". Say it as ours: "the way Maloney rinsed his glasses", never "Maloney's apricot".

His amounts fit your plan. The rinse is half a teaspoon in the Gin and Manhattan Bell-Ringers and one teaspoon in the others. It goes in while the drink is being stirred, "evenly coated all over inside", and then the drink is strained in. He never says to tip it out.

There's one gift and one fence:
- **The gift.** In the Gin, Manhattan and Martinez Bell-Ringers he rubs fresh-cut lemon around the edge of the glass. That's lemon on the rim in 1900. It's his, for those drinks only, and never the Lemon Drop's ancestry.
- **The fence, Wren.** "Gay Nineties" is Regan's phrase. The dated record is 1900 to 1903, so the reading says "a Chicago bar guide of 1900", never "the 1890s". "A long-gone category" is Regan's judgment too.
