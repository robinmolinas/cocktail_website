## whoYouAre

Every summer there's the same street party, and every summer a few more people find a reason to miss it. You're the one who suggests the Saturday instead of the Sunday, and the neighbours nobody ever thought to knock for. You keep the food everyone brings, the old songs, the raffle with the terrible prizes. Someone who's been coming for thirty years tells you it won't be the same. It isn't. The next summer, the street is full.

Here's the part nobody guesses: you love the old version more than the people defending it. You argue with dates and numbers, because "I'd hate to lose this" sounds soft, so the ones who love it too can hear someone wrecking it. The row doesn't scare you. What does is the change you pushed for turning out to be what finishes it off, and everyone remembering it was you. And look what you leave instead: the things people love are still there, with more people in them, because someone cared enough to change them. Nobody says so at the time. Someone should.

## yours

1. Before he opened Henry Africa's in San Francisco in 1969, Norman Hobday found the place: a bar with blackened windows. There wasn't enough money to remodel, he said later, so he brought in plants. As he put it, they did "a hell of a job for what they cost". The room turned bright and cheery, and people, he said, started swarming in.

2. Hobday loved the place as much as his customers did. A few years before it closed, he legally changed his own name to the bar's, Henry Africa, and kept it until he died. Many bars like his were done up with brass, Tiffany lamps and ferns, like the lounges of the 1890s, which had often turned away women who came on their own. Now women and men came in on equal terms. Fern bars are credited with bringing America's saloon culture back from the dead.

3. He gave the cheap reason, and I believe him. But I like to think it was never the whole reason: nobody takes a bar's name for the rest of their life over a cheap fix. You'd know that move. You give the sensible reason out loud, and the love shows in how long you stay. And what you change tends to outlive the place. Henry Africa's closed in 1986. The change didn't. When a new generation of bartenders went back to the old craft, around 2000, one thing they chose to keep was the bars where women drank on equal terms, and the women working behind them.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is Hobday's most enduring drink, the Lemon Drop, made the elegant way it began, before it shrank into a shot: served up, in a cocktail glass with sugar all the way round the rim. The lemon is squeezed fresh, the way his bar made its juices, to order. It tastes of the lemon candy it's named after, sweet and bright. Before it's poured, I coat the glass with a little clear, dry apricot brandy, just enough to wet it, and tip none of it out. In 1890s Chicago, a bartender called James Maloney served drinks whose glasses were coated first with apricot brandy, in the decade the fern bars dressed up as. It's there as a nod to what you do: the old thing kept on purpose, holding the new one. The apricot reaches you first, then the lemon.

5. One thing I'd like you to try. Next time you want to change something you love, say the love first. Before the dates and the numbers, tell the ones defending it what you'd never touch, and why. They'll hear someone on their side, because you are. And once the place is full again, don't stand at the edge checking that it held. Go in and enjoy it. You were never the one wrecking it. You were the one who wanted to stay.
