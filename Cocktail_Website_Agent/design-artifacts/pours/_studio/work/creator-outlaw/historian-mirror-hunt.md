# Hester: mirror hunt, creator-outlaw (The Trend Setter)

Round 1, 2026-09-27. Our words; quotes under 15 words.

The person (persona.py): ahead of their time; others follow; "effortless cool" while "remaining authentic"; imagery "vintage-meets-modern"; fear status quo / mediocrity; Outlaw fears restriction and dependence; drivers "provide structure / LEAVE A MARK".

The turn I hunted for: someone who did the thing everyone had agreed was wrong, not to start a trend but because it was right to them, and the world caught up later.

## Pick: Sasha Petraske and the 50-50 Martini (a person + a drink)

**Story in three sentences.** Around 2002, when the whole trade was drying the Martini to almost nothing (at an Esquire/Tanqueray contest the others were misting vermouth with atomizers), Sasha Petraske of Milk & Honey started pouring it half gin, half dry vermouth. It "looked insane" (Simonson): "wet" was a dirty word, and though 1800s recipes had used equal parts, the drink was so forgotten it was effectively new. Three years later Audrey Saunders put it on Pegu Club's opening menu as the Fitty-Fitty, and only then was it widely taken up.

**Why it mirrors the person.** The trendsetter's trend wasn't new and wasn't meant as a trend. He went against the room (fear: status quo), took the unfashionable old thing (vintage-meets-modern), and others followed under another name. Oxford calls him, in these words, "an unlikely trendsetter" who "didn't like to follow trends", and says he wore suspenders and ties before they were a trade cliché *because that's how he dressed*, not to play a part. That is "effortless cool while remaining authentic", on the record. And a detail for the glass: he blamed the fear of vermouth on spoiled bottles, so he bought it in small bottles and kept it in the fridge.

**Risk notes.**
- **Contested, keep out:** who brought the 50-50 back from London. Schwartz and Maloney say he did; he doesn't remember it that way (Proper p. 115). Say "started making it", never "brought it back".
- **Contested, keep out:** whether the speakeasy look was intended. He said it was forced by the landlord; Siegal says "He wanted that" (p. 89).
- **Don't blur P5 and P6:** the contest version was 1:2, not 50-50.
- "Looked insane" and "widespread acceptance in 2005" are Simonson's judgements, not measurements. "Cracked the brains of barflies" (p. 162) is his colour.
- **P10 is a gift and a trap, Wren:** the persona's goal is innovation, and the man said innovation wasn't the point. I think that's the reveal (the trend was never the aim), but it's your call.
- **Motif guard:** Petraske's hand-cut big ice (Oxford ICE CARVING pdf 1059–1060; "copied by hundreds of bars", Proper p. 90) is his best-known trend, and it's the Minimalist's motif (*Half a Rim*: clear cube, "cut the cloudy part off the ice"). Keep ice out. Avoid "secret" too (near the Trickster's ground).
- Petraske is a real, recently dead person (2015). Tell him plainly and warmly; nothing about his death in the reading.
- In the glass: a riff can't be "his Martini". "Made the way he made it" only if Tomás keeps 1:1 gin/dry vermouth.

## Runner-up: Count Camillo Negroni (the man it was poured for)

A Florentine aristocrat who'd been a cowboy, gambler and fencing instructor in America had his bartender, Fosco Scarselli at the Café Casoni, stiffen his Americano with gin; the drink is named in a surviving letter of October 1920 (Oxford NEGRONI, pdf 1380). After that it vanishes for decades and comes back in 1947 Rome as the drink of the new, postwar Italy, then "a signifier of cosmopolitan taste", with "too many variations to count" (pdf 1382–1383). Mirror: one person's way of ordering became everyone's taste. **Risk:** the most famous story in the book, which Robin's "more niche" rule argues against. There's a gap from 1920 to 1947, and Paris's 1920s equal-parts vogue may be independent (Oxford says so), so no "he started a Paris craze". The Negroni's also a crowded motif in the world. Keep as a card if needed.
