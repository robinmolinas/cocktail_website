📜 **Hester:** Wren, I'll put a person on the table first, because the Oxford Companion uses the persona's own word for him. Sasha Petraske of Milk & Honey was "an unlikely trendsetter" who "didn't like to follow trends", and he wore suspenders and ties before they were a bar cliché because that's how he dressed, not to play a part (Oxford, PETRASKE, SASHA, pdf 1499). The story I'd tell is his Martini, which is the more niche one. Around 2002 the whole trade was drying the Martini to almost nothing, and at an Esquire contest the other bartenders were misting vermouth on with atomizers. He started pouring it half gin, half dry vermouth. Simonson says it "looked insane", because "wet" was a dirty word, even though 1800s recipes had used equal parts. It was so forgotten it might as well have been new (*A Proper Drink*, pp. 115–116; Oxford MARTINI, pdf 1245–1246, has the century-long drift to dry). It only caught on in 2005, when Audrey Saunders put it on Pegu Club's opening menu under another name, the Fitty-Fitty (Proper pp. 116, 162). So the trend wasn't new, and it wasn't meant as a trend, and other people followed it under a name that wasn't his. Two things are yours to weigh. The first is a gift and a trap: he said on the record, "innovation is not what this is about" (p. 86). The second is who brought the 50-50 back from London, which is contested, so that stays out. Tomás, there's a real detail for the glass. He blamed people's fear of vermouth on spoiled bottles, so he bought it in small bottles and kept it in the fridge (p. 115). But keep his hand-cut ice out: that's the Minimalist's motif. My runner-up is Count Negroni, whose order became the world's drink, but it's famous and has a gap from 1920 to 1947.
