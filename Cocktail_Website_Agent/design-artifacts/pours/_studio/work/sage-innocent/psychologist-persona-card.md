# Persona card: The Prodigy (sage-innocent)

Wren, round 1, 2026-10-02. Read from `persona.py sage-innocent`, my Sage sketches (`plans/work/sage/psychologist-sketches.md`), the family plan's row, and the sibling drafts in this family. Nothing here is a fact for the guest until Hester sources it.

## The sheet, in one breath
Knew things early and still wants to know everything. Grown-up books at ten, questions that silenced the dinner table, and the delight never went out of it. A mind that runs ahead with wide-open eyes. Fears being patronised, or finding out the grown-ups were bluffing. Goal: wisdom. Fear: deception. Innocent half: wonder, trust, honesty, straightforwardness; afraid of punishment and of being in the wrong.

## The one true thing (no sibling shares it)
**They were right before anyone was ready to hear it from them.** What they learned early isn't only the thing they knew. They also learned that people hear *who* is talking before *what* is said. So the Prodigy has spent a life getting there first and then waiting to be believed. And the wonder survived it. That's the remarkable part: they're still delighted by the next thing, not bitter about the last.

The line between the Prodigy and the Lone Voice is the sharpest one in the family, so I'll draw it here once. **The Lone Voice is disbelieved for what they say. The Prodigy is doubted for who's saying it.** The Lone Voice pays for a claim in standing and company. The Prodigy pays for their age, or their newness, and the claim is often accepted the moment someone older repeats it.

## What they hide
- **The old sting.** It still flares, decades later, when someone explains a thing they already know, slowly, as if to a child. They smile and let it pass. Inside, they're ten again.
- **The dimming.** They've learned to make themselves smaller so the room stays comfortable: "I think…" in front of something they know, a pause left for the older person to fill, a fact held back so nobody feels shown up. They do it so well that people who've known them for years don't know how much they know.
- **The day an adult didn't know.** There was a moment, long ago, when someone they believed turned out to be bluffing, and the floor moved. They still check the floor.
- **The quiet worry about "for your age".** The praise always came with that qualifier. Once it falls away, some part of them wonders whether what's left is just ordinary. (Gently: this is one clause at most, and never as a diagnosis. Many readers will be long past being young.)

## What they fear
- Being patronised: a pat on the head instead of an answer.
- Bluff passed off as knowledge, especially by someone with standing.
- Under both: being dismissed before they've been checked. Real doubt doesn't frighten them. Someone who says "show me" is taking them seriously. The fear is of the person who never looks.

## What they give (say it out loud, Robin's rule)
- **They never talk down to anyone.** They answer the real question a child asks, they explain things to newcomers as if the newcomer can keep up, and they say "I don't know, let's find out" instead of bluffing. They give everyone the respect they once had to wait for. People leave a conversation with them feeling cleverer than when they came. That is worth a lot, and the reading should say so plainly.
- The question with no agenda that silences the table. Because it's sincere, it makes honesty safe for everyone there.

## Traps
- **Age.** The guest may be seventy. "Early" is how a life began, not how it is now. Never "young person", "kid" or "gifted child" about the guest. The scene can be a memory ("when you were ten…") or a scene from today (someone explaining your own field to you).
- Praising intelligence: "gifted", "brilliant", "genius", "precocious" as a label, or IQ. The reading recognises the delight and the sting, never the cleverness.
- Child-genius clichés: the chessboard, the violin, Mozart.
- "Proved them all wrong": revenge, gloating, the elder humiliated, fame as the payoff.
- The sheet's example (Tavi Gevinson) is gendered and fashion-world. It's fine on Robin's sheet, never in the reading, and nothing gendered about the guest.
- "Mansplaining", or any gendered word for being talked down to.
- Health (family-wide), which matters a lot for my lead below: no medicine, vaccines, germs, milk or "pasteurised".
- "Grown-ups" is the sheet's own word and can be used once, in a memory. Never call the guest's elders liars: "bluffing" is the right size.

## The story test (sharpened for Hester)
The mirror must show:
1. **Someone young or new to a field who knew or noticed something, and the knowledge is on the page**, dated and specific (what they saw, not "a gifted youngster").
2. **Doubted by someone with more standing, on the page.** Best if the record shows the doubt was about *them*: their age, their newness, their rank. Doubt of the result alone is acceptable if the record shows their age mattered to the elder (a word, a gesture).
3. **They answered by showing, not by arguing or resenting.** The curiosity outlived the doubt.
4. *Best:* an earlier, senior answer that was wrong on the record. An honest error is enough. **I waive "bluffing" as a story event:** we can't invent a bluff, and making one up would be exactly the deception this guest fears. The bluff lives in the guest's half (whoYouAre).

**What must hold:** the turn is *being taken seriously*, being checked, and not fame or the elder's defeat. Best if the thing they knew can be put in the glass.
**Out:** revenge, gloating, fame, youth only asserted, the elder glad to be surpassed (the Mentor's), a claim against authority paid for in standing (the Lone Voice's), "nobody's sure" (the Free Spirit's), health, and everything the plan lists as already tried.

## A lead I'm bringing (for Hester to source; nothing in it is fact yet)
**Louis Pasteur and Jean-Baptiste Biot, Paris, 1848: the crystals of the acid of grapes.** I know this one from outside the library, so every word below is a claim for Hester to check, not something I'm asserting.
- (4) The elder's answer: in 1844 Eilhard Mitscherlich, one of the great chemists of the day, reported (through Biot, to the Académie des sciences) that the salts of tartaric acid and of "racemic" acid, both from winemaking, were identical in crystal form, though only one turned polarised light.
- (1) The young one's knowledge: Pasteur, about 25 and barely known, looked again with a lens and saw tiny facets that Mitscherlich had missed. The racemic crystals came in two mirror-image shapes, like left and right hands. He sorted them by hand, and each pile turned the light the opposite way. It's often told as the discovery of molecular "handedness".
- (2) The doubt: Biot, about 74 and the authority on exactly this, wasn't convinced. He made Pasteur repeat it in his own laboratory, with Biot's own acid, and Biot made up and read the decisive solution himself.
- (3) The turn: when it came out as Pasteur had said, Biot took his arm and said, as Pasteur later told it: "My dear child, I have loved science so much throughout my life that this makes my heart beat." **"My dear child"**: the very words that usually sting the Prodigy, said by the elder who checked, as a blessing. That's the "how did they know?" for this guest. And the elder's doubt is the respect, not the insult.
- Why it's this person and nobody else's: the knowledge is a young person's, on the record. The doubt comes from standing. He answers by showing, with no grudge (they became close). And the earlier senior answer was wrong. None of it is about Pasteur's later fame.
- **In the glass:** tartaric acid, "the primary acid of grapes" (*Liquid Intelligence* pdf ~59–61, the acids table, library primary; the *Codex*'s TARTARIC ACID entry too). "Racemic" is said to come from the Latin for a bunch of grapes (Hester to check). For Tomás, from the books: a grape-based drink (brandy or cognac, or wine) with tartaric acid as the acid, which is the very substance he sorted. The Sidecar family and brandy are both free in this family. *Liquid Intelligence*'s **The Carvone** (pdf 180) is a chirality drink, the same idea of a mirror-image pair, but it's Arnold's aquavit, mint and caraway, and caraway is *Not Only the Way*'s closing word. So it's for interest, not to copy.
