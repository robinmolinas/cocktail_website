## whoYouAre

Someone is explaining your own subject to you, slowly, as if to a child. You've known it for years, better than they do. You smile, you let them finish, and you put "I think" in front of the part you're sure of. You learned that early, along with everything else: people hear who's talking before they hear what's said.

Being doubted doesn't hurt. A real "show me" is a kind of respect. The pat on the head does, from someone who never looked, and so does finding out that the confident voice was bluffing all along.

And the delight never went out of it. You give everyone the thing you once had to wait for: you never talk down to anybody. Ask you anything and you answer the real question. People leave a conversation with you feeling cleverer than before. Nobody forgets how that felt.

## yours

1. In Paris in 1848, Louis Pasteur was a chemist of twenty-five, just made a doctor. A senior Berlin chemist, Mitscherlich, had written about two salts. One came from tartaric acid, the main acid of grapes, the other from a strange twin found at a tartaric-acid works. Their crystals looked alike, yet in water only the first turned polarised light to one side. Pasteur noticed tiny facets on the crystals that Mitscherlich had missed. His first idea about them didn't work, so he looked closer: the twin's crystals came in two shapes, mirror images, like a left hand and a right. He sorted them into two piles, and each turned the light the opposite way.

2. Jean-Baptiste Biot, seventy-four, was an authority on that kind of light. "Are you quite sure?" he asked, and said he'd like to investigate "that young man's results". His biographer says Biot could hardly believe someone so new had managed it. Pasteur wrote and asked to show him. Biot offered to check it, brought his own acid, watched the salt being made, put the dish out of reach for two days, then made up the solutions and read the result himself. It was as Pasteur said. Biot took his arm and, as Pasteur told it, said: "My dear child, I have loved the sciences so much in my life that this makes my heart beat."

3. My dear child. Those are the words you've been talked down to with, and here they come from the one person who had checked, as a blessing. I like to think that's what you've been hoping for since you were small: not to be believed on sight, but to be looked at properly. The doubt was never the insult. The insult is someone who never looks, and you've learned to tell the two apart faster than most. You've had plenty of practice. Pasteur's biographer adds that when he explained his chemistry, no question surprised him, and he never laughed at anyone for not knowing. That part is you as well.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Sidecar, a renowned classic, built on pisco, South America's clear grape brandy, with a little blanc vermouth (the pale, lightly sweet kind) and an orange liqueur. The change that matters: the lemon is gone, and white verjus, juice pressed from wine grapes before they ripen, gives the sour instead. I swapped it as a nod to Pasteur: the sourness is the grapes' own acid, and its tartaric is the familiar one of the two hands he sorted apart. It's stirred very cold and served up, bright and winy, with orange peel to finish.

5. So when someone doubts what you know, don't shrink and don't argue. Show them, the way he did, and let them look for themselves. The ones worth keeping will look. You've waited long enough to be believed. You don't have to wait to be heard. And I hope that more than once in your life, someone who checked takes your arm.

## Resonance

**The "this is me" line:** *"You put 'I think' in front of the part you're sure of."* It's the habit nobody outside notices, and the guest will recognise it at once. Second strongest: *"My dear child. Those are the words you've been talked down to with, and here they come from the one person who had checked, as a blessing."*

**As the guest:** the opening scene of being explained to is universal for this person at any age: a seventy-year-old meets it as often as a teenager does. The sting and the bluff are named without blame. The gift is said out loud. The story gives back the moment they've wanted: the doubter who looks. *Ready, pending audit.*

**As the sceptic:** is the first scene a Barnum line (everyone's been condescended to)? Yes, partly. What makes it this person's is "your own subject", "better than they do" and the self-applied "I think". The Free Spirit hides the asking, and this guest hides the knowing. The Lone Voice is disbelieved for the claim, and this guest is doubted for the speaker. The Mentor's elder glad to be surpassed is kept out: no sponsorship, no friendship, no "explain it to you". Is there flattery risk in "cleverer"? It praises what they *give* others, not their brain. Robin likes the gift said plainly.
