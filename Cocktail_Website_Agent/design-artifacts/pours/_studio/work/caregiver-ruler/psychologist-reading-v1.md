## Why the core moved from round 1
My round-1 line ("Everyone thinks the no comes easily to you") is taken twice in this family:
- *Not Too Polite* (Campaigner): "People think it comes easily to you. It doesn't." (and *The Rest of You*: "People think calm just comes to you. It doesn't.")
- *The Other Kindness* (Doctor): "Yes would be so much easier… You say no anyway, and then you go over it all the way home."
So the Nanny isn't "the costly no". The Doctor owns the no. The Nanny owns the **measure**: not taking the good thing away, making it last, and doing it where everyone can see it's fair. Hester's record (he didn't take the rum away, split it in two, left lime and sugar to them, mixed it in the open) gave me that.

## whoYouAre
It's late, everyone's having a good time, and you're the one who says "one more, then we go." You're the one who checks what's left in the account before the trip gets booked, who asks for a text when they're home, who keeps the Sunday call going after everyone else has let it slide. Some people hear a rule. It isn't one, not really. It's how you make sure the good thing is still there tomorrow.

People call you the sensible one, and on worse nights, something less kind. It stings more than you show, because you were never against the fun. You're the reason there's any left in the morning. Everyone else gets to forget the time because you never do. And somewhere under the plans is a fear you wouldn't say out loud: that if you stopped holding it all, it would come apart, and it would be your fault for wanting one night off.

## yours
1. In 1740, Admiral Edward Vernon, in command of the British ships in the West Indies, put a stop to the way his sailors took their daily rum: in drams, often all at once, with work waiting for them high in the rigging. From then on it was mixed with water, four parts to one, and served in two portions a day instead of one.

2. What I like is what he didn't do. He didn't take the rum away. He let the men buy lime and sugar to make it taste better. And he had it mixed in the open, in a cask kept for the purpose, so nobody could think they'd been given short measure. The story goes that they named the drink after the coarse coat he wore: grog.

3. The ration outlived him by more than two centuries. When the navy poured its last one, in July 1970, sailors held mock burials and wore black armbands. I like to think some of what they were mourning was the order of it: the same measure, the same hour, every day, for as long as anyone could remember. That's how you hold a line. You don't take the good thing away. You make it last, you leave room for everyone's own taste, and you do it where everyone can see it's fair.

4. [the drink as proof: waits for Tomás. What it has to carry: a fixed measure (the part that's theirs, the Nanny's) and room for someone else's taste inside it; orderly, repeatable, nothing performed.]

5. So here's the one thing I'd change. Next time you set the limit, set it, and then hand over the lime and the sugar. Let them make it their own, and don't check how they did it. The measure can stay yours. The taste was always theirs. It'll still hold, and you might get the evening off.
