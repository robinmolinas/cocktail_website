## 1. Admiral Vernon's order, 1740 (grog): my pick
**What:** a person and a rule (the order that made grog).
**Story:** On 21 August 1740 Vernon, commanding the West Indies station, stopped his sailors drinking their half-pint of rum neat and all at once: it would be mixed with a quart of water (four to one) and served in two portions a day, not one. He didn't take the rum away, and he let the men buy lime and sugar out of their savings to make it taste better. And the mixing had to happen openly, on deck, in a cask kept for it, so nobody could think they'd been cheated of their share.
**Why it mirrors:** a rule made for their safety (men who climbed rigging drunk fell), not for his comfort; a limit that still leaves room for their own taste; authority that works in the open. Coda (fact): when the navy ended the daily ration in 1970, sailors held mock burials and wore black armbands. The rule they'd been given became the thing they mourned.
**Sources:** Oxford GROG (pdf 951: 1740, Vernon, "pernicious custom ... in drams, and often at once", four parts water, split twice daily, rigging as the reason, "Old Grogram" *reportedly*); Oxford SPIRITS IN THE MILITARY (pdf 1868–1869: half a pint of rum to a quart of water, lime and sugar "that it be made more palatable", scuttled butt, dispensed openly so the men were "not defrauded"; "good husbandmen" buy lime and sugar); Oxford RUM, NAVY (pdf 1701–1702: 1970, Black Tot Day, mock burials, black armbands; "Up spirits!"); Oxford timeline (pdf 2248). *Joy of Mixology* pdf 20 (Vernon, 1740). Web, secondary: wordorigins.org (Wilton), fetched 2026-09-27: order text "between the hours of 10 and 12 ... 4 and 6"; "fatal effects to their morals as well as their health"; first print use of "grog" Feb 1749.
**Risks / fences:**
- The name: "Old Grog/Grogram" from his cloak is *reported* (Oxford's word). Tell it as "the story goes" or leave it out.
- Scurvy is NOT Vernon's reason. Lime was for taste; vitamin C was a later benefit (Oxford pdf 1869). Keep scurvy out (and it's the sibling *Not Only the Way*'s lineage territory: Rose's lime cordial, 1867).
- The order's quoted words come through Oxford and a web transcription, not the manuscript. "husbandmen" (Oxford) vs "good husbands" (other transcriptions): don't quote the word.
- Ratio: four to one at first; it varied later (two to one from 1937). Say "four parts water" only for 1740.
- Sailors' feelings in 1740: no record in the library that they resented it. Don't claim it.
- Sibling watch: caregiver-creator's image brief bans ships/navy for that pour; here it would be ours, but the two Caregiver pours shouldn't both lean on lime-at-sea.

## 2. Sasha Petraske's posted rules (Milk & Honey, 1999): runner-up
**What:** a person and his house rules.
**Story:** Petraske opened his bar on the promise to a noise-averse landlord that nobody would know it was there; the reservation policy was to keep crowds off the pavement. He posted "Rules of Etiquette" (no name-dropping, no shouting, no unwanted advances), and before that, as a young bartender, he'd quietly talk a pest away from the people he was bothering.
**Why it mirrors:** rules as protection of other people (neighbours, guests), enforced by leading someone away rather than throwing them out.
**Sources:** Oxford MILK & HONEY (pdf 1303–1304); *Proper* pp. 87–88. Card exists: `fact-cards/sasha-petraske-fifty-fifty.md` (runner-up in *Still Yours*).
**Risks:** the rules as written are gendered ("women", "gentlemen"), and one is crude; Petraske already sits in the Trend Setter's dossier; the neighbour-keeping rules were dropped uptown (card F17).
