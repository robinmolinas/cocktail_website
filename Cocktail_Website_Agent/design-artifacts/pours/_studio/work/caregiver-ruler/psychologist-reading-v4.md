## whoYouAre
It's late, everyone's having a good time, and you're the one who says "one more, then home." You're the one who checks what's left in the account before the trip gets booked, who asks for a text when they're home, who keeps the Sunday call going long after the others have let it slide. Some people hear a rule. It isn't one, not really. It's what keeps the good thing there tomorrow.

People call you the sensible one, and on worse nights, something less kind. It stings more than you show, because you were never against the fun. You're the reason there's any left in the morning. Everyone else gets to forget the time because you never do. And somewhere under the plans is a fear you wouldn't say out loud: that if you stopped holding it all, it would come apart, and it would be your fault for wanting one night off.

## yours
1. In 1740, Admiral Edward Vernon, in command of the British ships in the West Indies, put a stop to the way his sailors took their daily rum: in drams, often all at once, with work waiting for them high in the rigging. From then on it was mixed with water, at first four parts to one, and served in two portions a day instead of one.

2. What I like is what he didn't do. The rum stayed. He let the men buy lime and sugar to make it taste better. And he had it mixed in the open, in a half-barrel, so nobody could think they'd been given short measure. Legend has it he was called Old Grog, after the stiff, gum-proofed coat he wore, and the drink took his name.

3. The ration outlived him by more than two centuries. When the navy poured its last one, in July 1970, sailors held mock burials and wore black armbands. I like to think some of what they were mourning was the order of it: the call of "Up spirits!", the measure, the hour, every day. That's how you hold a line. You don't take the good thing away. You make it last, you leave room for everyone's own taste, and you do it where everyone can see it's fair.

4. Yours is his order made into a long drink, with something he never had. One jigger of a strong rum, bottled well above the usual strength so the rum isn't lost at four to one, and four jiggers of cold water, measured with the same jigger. Then half a jigger each of fresh lime and a demerara syrup made ahead. There's no ice, because ice would melt and quietly change the measure. What's poured is what's drunk. The thing I added is in the syrup: a splash of sherry vinegar, a vinegar made from sherry, which you can smell as well as taste. So even the sweet part, the part that's handed over, has a little sharpness in it. I don't think you can help that, and I wouldn't want you to.

5. Next time you set the limit, set it, and then let the rest be theirs to make. Don't check how they did it. The measure can stay yours. The taste was always theirs. It'll still hold, and you might even get that night off.

**closingLine (Tomás):** *Four of water to one of rum. The lime and the sugar are theirs.*

## My rulings for the room
- **Sherry vinegar stays.** It does something for the person, not just the palate: care that doesn't only soothe. y4 owns it as a dry smile ("I don't think you can help that"), never as a scold. Tomás's sulphite flag goes to Robin as written.
- **Epigraph:** cold, 13 words, no bare "he", no sailors, and it doesn't echo the tagline or the closing line (which leads with "four of water to one of rum", so the epigraph stays off the ratio). True: 180 of 270 ml is water (67%). Runner-up: *There's no ice in this glass, on purpose. Nothing gets to change the measure.*
- **Names:** Fair Measure (pick). It's the person's move, not the ingredient. Runner-up: The Allowance. Against "Four to One" (Rosetta) and "Still There Tomorrow" (it leans on the morning line).
- **Image brief:** keep the morning clock. The line stays.

## Resonance (v2): ready
**As the guest (a Nanny).** Tagline: it stops me, because "against the fun" is exactly the accusation I've heard, and "why it lasts" is the answer I've never managed to give. whoYouAre ¶1 is three scenes I've lived, with no children and no job in them. ¶2 is the line: *"Everyone else gets to forget the time because you never do."* The fear, "your fault for wanting one night off", is the thing I'd never say. The story doesn't make me a tyrant. It's a man who didn't take the good thing away, and I'd enjoy that the famous name is only a legend. y4 made me smile: even my sugar has a bite. y5 asks the hardest thing for me, not checking, and pays it back with the night off I'd already confessed to wanting.
**As the sceptic.** Barnum watch: "the sensible one" could fit anyone who's organised. It's saved by the concrete scenes and by "forget the time", which is specific to someone who holds the clock for others. Sibling swap: no "People think… It doesn't." (Campaigner, Angel), no "say no anyway" (Doctor), no "just once" (Rescuer), no "you'd rather" frame, no "here's where I'd…" opener (Overnight, Old Master) and no "change one thing" (Old Master's position), no "looked after too", no sea-lime lineage. *Quite Alive*'s "The care is in the measure, not the size" is a different sense (made to measure). My "measure" is the ratio, and I never pair it with "care". History is y1–y3 plus a clause of y4, under half. Guest never holds the drink.
