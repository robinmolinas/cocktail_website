## whoYouAre

Two hours into the evening, the table is arguing about something that sounds important: who said what, whose fault it was, what to call it. You've let all of it go. It isn't that you can't follow it. You followed it once, a while back, and found out how little of it was worth the heat. But buried in all that noise there's one small thing nobody else noticed was the point, and that one you won't let go.

Your ease took effort, though nobody sees it. You've been pulled into someone else's story before, and found yourself carrying part of something that wasn't true. So now you step back early. And because you step back, people sometimes decide you don't care. You do. You care about very few things, and you care about those all the way.

That gives people something, and they rarely say it: with you, nobody has to win. The temperature drops. When someone wants an argument to go quiet, they bring it to you.

## yours

1. Sauza was founded in 1873, and the name grew into one of the biggest in tequila. Then the business was sold, and the name passed from one company to the next: a Spanish firm, then Allied Domecq, Fortune Brands, Beam and Suntory. Today its agave goes through a diffuser, a big industrial machine that breaks it down with steam and chemicals. It came in when agave ran short, and it gets more out of every plant.

2. One piece stayed in the family. As Guillermo Erickson Sauza, the founder's great-great-grandson, tells it, his grandfather kept the family estate in the town of Tequila, and on it an old distillery he'd turned into a museum. Guillermo felt the tequilas he could buy had lost something next to the ones he'd drunk with his grandfather, and he thought getting the old place running again would be a fun project. He wasn't allowed to put the family name on it. He called it Los Abuelos, for the grandfathers before him, and then a rum's trademark forced him to change that too. The name it ended up with was Fortaleza. The old distillery had been called La Fortaleza. Maybe one led to the other. I like to think so.

3. A name is what everyone fights over. Guillermo lost two, and kept the old way going. Over twenty years later, they still cook the agave slowly in a brick oven and crush it with a tahona, a big stone wheel pulled round and round a stone bed, which he'll tell you is far less efficient than a modern mill. As he puts it, they won't make the most tequila, or earn the most from it. I like to think the name was never the part he cared about most.

4. You'd have understood Guillermo straight away. Everyone else was busy with the name: who owned it, who could use it, what to put on the bottle instead. What mattered to him was smaller than all that: a piece of land, an old stone wheel, and a taste he remembered from drinking with his grandfather. You know that kind of small.

5. So the cocktail I've made for you is the simplest long drink there is, and one of the best: Fortaleza Blanco, or another blanco made with a stone wheel, with cold soda and nothing else poured in. No lime, no sugar, nothing covering it up. With soda, a blanco tequila turns bright and citrusy all by itself. The one extra is a sprig of oregano, pinched once and laid on the ice, as a nod to the small thing nobody thought to pick. It shares a note with the tequila, so it brings out something already there.

6. Your way has one cost, though. Because you let so much go, people take your ease for not caring. Next time the table's arguing and the thing you care about is caught in the middle of it, don't just keep hold of it quietly. Tell them, out loud, that it matters to you. The people who thought you were easy about everything will be surprised. Then they'll trust your ease even more, because they'll know it was always your choice.

## Resonance

- **The "this is me" line:** *"You care about very few things, and you care about those all the way."* It's the Dude's hidden truth: the ease isn't indifference, it's how they ration their caring. The line I'd hand Robin: *"with you, nobody has to win."* That's the gift said out loud (his rule), and it's warm.
- **As the guest:** the opening scene is checkable by anyone who's sat through a long argument at a table. Then the cost of the ease (pulled in once, carrying something untrue) without a diagnosis. Then the gift. The story lets them watch someone lose a name twice and keep the wheel turning, and y4 hands them the likeness in one short paragraph without listing parts. The position is gentle, earned last, and ends on what they gain: their ease gets trusted more.
- **As a sceptic:** "you're easy-going" alone would be Barnum, and so would "you care about what matters". What pins it down: the one small thing nobody else noticed was the point, the heat that isn't worth it, ease taken for not caring. Sibling swaps: the Wise Fool jokes their way into the argument, and this guest steps out of it. The Lone Voice fights the official story, and there's no fight here: the diffuser is fair, and nobody's a villain. The Philosopher goes deeper, the Connoisseur has ceremony (none here), and the Architect's structure words ("rests on", "holds") are absent. Across families: *Kept* (fake vs real: neither word used), *Good as It Is* (innocent delight: this ease is learned), *Go On* and *In One Piece* (calm power: here the calm is a choice about what's worth it, never strength in reserve), *First Choice* ("nobody has to earn their place": mine is "nobody has to win", which shares "nobody has to" only). No film, no catchphrase, no White Russian, no "guy", "man" or "dude".
- **The story mirrors the person:** the noise around the name, the small thing that stayed, the slow way chosen on the record, and the trade said in his own words.
- **The drink proves it:** nothing in the glass but the tequila and soda, nothing covering it, and one small herb nobody would have picked that only brings out what's there.
