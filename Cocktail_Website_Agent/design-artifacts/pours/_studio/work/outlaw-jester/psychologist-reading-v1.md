## Reading

**epigraph**
*A song made this drink famous. The song was never really about the drink.*

## whoYouAre
There's a meeting, or a family dinner, or a rule nobody's supposed to question, and everyone's nodding along. Then you say something, lightly, and the table laughs, the person at the head of it too, a beat late. Nobody can quite tell you off. It was only funny. That was the idea.

Nobody notices that you chose it. The joke isn't a reflex. It's how the true thing gets past the people it's about, because said straight, it would be stopped. So you keep quiet about how much of it you mean: the moment they know, they stop laughing and start stopping you. A telling-off you can live with; it means it landed. What you dread is hearing your line from somebody else, everyone laughing, with the point quietly taken out.

And what you give is more than a laugh. When a room laughs at whatever's in charge, it sees how ordinary the thing really is, and fears it less. That's how you hand people their nerve back, and it's a real gift, whatever you call it.

## yours

1. In wartime Trinidad, with an American base on the island, a calypso singer called Lord Invader set new words to an old tune by Lionel Belasco. Where he sang, songs had to get past a board of censors chaired by the police commissioner. His got through. It was called "Rum and Coca-Cola", it laughed at what the soldiers' money was doing to the island, and soldiers and Trinidadians sang it together.

2. In 1943 an American comedian, Morey Amsterdam, came to entertain the troops, heard it, and took it home. The Andrews Sisters made it a hit, and the first 200,000 records named Amsterdam as the writer. A New York court later found the words were Lord Invader's, and that the taking was deliberate. The money reached him seven years late, and even after that he was often short.

3. While the case went on, he recorded "Yankee Dollar", a sort of sequel that turned the joke on himself: the man who'd sung the hit, still without the dollars. Years later came "Crisis in Arkansas", which laughed at the black bow tie of the governor who had kept nine Black students out of a Little Rock school. I like to think he'd worked out what you know: someone can take a line, but never the place it came from.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is rum and Coca-Cola. It's older than the song, and people rarely give it much credit, but made properly it's a classic worth having, long and cold over plenty of ice. The rum is from Trinidad. Fresh lime is squeezed and dropped in: most colas carry lime peel in their taste, so the juice draws out what the cola was already hiding and cuts its sweetness. That's there as a nod to the song, sweet enough for everyone to sing, with its bite kept. Angostura bitters, made in Trinidad too, go on last, so the island reaches you before anything else.

5. One day you'll hear a line of yours from someone else, to a big laugh, with the point gone. Don't spend the evening proving it was yours. Write the one after it, and aim it a little higher. A joke can be borrowed; the person it came from can't, and every room that laughed with you is a little braver for it.
