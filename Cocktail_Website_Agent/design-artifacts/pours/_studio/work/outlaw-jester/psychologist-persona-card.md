# Persona card: The Subverter (outlaw-jester), Wren, round 1

## Who this is
Someone whose laugh is aimed upward. They joke about exactly the thing a room isn't supposed to joke about (the boss, the rule, the sacred cow, the people with the money), and the laugh is how the true thing gets said without anyone being able to punish it. Essence: humour and sarcasm to subvert and unsettle. Goal: liberation. Fear: powerlessness, and in this persona's own words, "being silenced, the joke taken away".

## The one true thing (no sibling has it)
**The joke is a vehicle, chosen on purpose: it's how the true thing gets past the people it's about.** They put the point inside the laugh because said straight, it would be stopped.
- Not the Surrealist (*The Other Berry* owns "you meant it, and it stings a little when it's taken as only a joke": misread, by accident). The Subverter dresses it as a joke deliberately. So no "only joking" / "meant it" in our text.
- Not the Reassurer (*Worth the Trip* owns "you joke because it is [serious]": comfort, one person, timing). The Subverter's joke isn't comfort, it's cover.
- Not the Trickster (*No Accident*: a trick, a hoax; fear of being ignored). The Subverter's joke is told in the open, with a target, and the fear is the joke being taken.

## Hide / fear / give
- **Hides:** how much they mean it. If people knew it was serious, it would stop working, and they'd be stopped.
- **Fears:** not the telling-off (that means it landed). The joke taken: stopped, or repeated by people who sing along and never hear what it was about. Powerlessness = the moment the laugh changes nothing.
- **Gives (to say out loud, per Robin's rule):** when they laugh at the thing in charge, everyone else sees it at its actual size. A room that has laughed at something together is less afraid of it. That's how they hand people their nerve back.

## Siblings and neighbours (keep apart)
- Outlaw: Gentle Giant (*In One Piece*, restraint), Rule Breaker (*Asked In*: made without permission, then adopted; **the "taken by the establishment, answered" arc is theirs**, so our ending can't be about ownership or credit), Gangster (*Where You Stand*, the line), Thrill Seeker (*Whoopee*, own idea, fear under the laugh), Maverick (*Can't Watch*: **a deliberate arrest, "break it where it can be seen"**), Adolescent (*Kept*, smells a fake), and the unauthored Devil's Advocate (argues the other side; also funny: an argument, never a laugh).
- Jester and others: Stand-up (*Is It Just Me*: "nobody's the only one"; keep the gift off belonging), Prankster (*Got You*: jokes on people you keep), Scandalmonger (plan: Croswell, the gasp, a press trial), Campaigner (*Not Too Polite*: "someone had to say it"; a cause, an argument).

## Traps
The Joker (cruelty, chaos); punching down; a hoax or invented story; a deliberate arrest; a press or libel trial; a cause argued rather than a joke told; "it's only a joke" used glibly; sneering at the people who laughed; taking a political side today; colonial history as costume; anything sexual in the story's joke; "fake"; "say it".

## Story test (five points)
1. A joke aimed at power or the sacred, told openly (not a hoax), punching up.
2. Power pushes back: banned, censored, fined, sacked, or **the joke taken** (stolen, made harmless: the persona's own fear words).
3. They keep joking afterwards (on a page).
4. The joke made people think again, or landed on the record as true.
5. **The ending holds after the page** (lesson from *Kept*): the person's later record doesn't make them a mirror a guest would be ashamed of, and the story's win isn't reversed later.

## Ruling on the plan row
**Mencken: struck.** Fails 5 and collides twice. (a) Going to Boston to be arrested on purpose is now *Can't Watch*'s shape, in this family's registry ("got himself arrested"). (b) A newspaperman in court for what he printed is the Scandalmonger's Croswell. (c) After the page: his diary (published 1989) and his 1918 Nietzsche introduction carry antisemitic and racist lines (Wikipedia, lead only, citing the 1989 AP story "Mencken Was Pro-Nazi, His Diary Shows"; scholars dispute how far). For a persona whose gift is punching up, a guest who looks him up finds him punching down. Plus the coin bite is a stunt (Trickster).

**Lord Invader: now on a primary page, so promoted to lead**, with corrections (pages saved here):
- *Khan v. Leo Feist, Inc.*, 70 F. Supp. 450 (S.D.N.Y., 24 Feb 1947), affirmed 165 F.2d 188 (2d Cir., 20 Dec 1947), Caselaw Access Project (static.case.law; `psychologist-khan-v-feist-1947*.json`): Finding 1, Rupert Grant, "whose platform title was 'Invader' and 'Lord Invader'", wrote the words in February 1943; he assigned the copyright to Mohamed H. Khan, who ran the calypso tent he sang in; Feist, Amsterdam, Paul Baron and Sullavan infringed. Khan testified that calypsos went to "a board of censors of whom the Commissioner of Police is chairman, before public performance was permitted", this one included. The defence called the song "inherently salacious, immoral and lewd"; Judge Byers: the words were "a recital or recognition of a development in the economy of Trinidad which was too important to escape the comment freely circulated in the Calypso shows". In 1942 "the 'Yankee dollar' began to play an important part in the every-day lives of the people".
- *Baron v. Leo Feist, Inc.*, 78 F. Supp. 686 (S.D.N.Y., 14 June 1948) (`psychologist-baron-v-feist-1948.json`): the song, "at least in part, a ribald comment upon the social interrelationship existing between the American soldiers stationed in Trinidad and the natives", "became an overnight success and was sung a great deal by both natives and soldiers"; Amsterdam, there with the U.S.O. in September 1943, claimed never to have heard it; "There is no doubt in my mind that Amsterdam brought both the words and the music with him from Trinidad"; the first 200,000 Andrews Sisters records named Amsterdam composer of words and music.
- **Correction:** the lyric case was **Khan's, 1947**, not Lord Invader's in 1948 (1948 was Belasco's melody). The plaintiff and the money were Khan's. Wikipedia's "$150,000 to Lord Invader" and "Amsterdam kept the copyright" are lead-only: out of guest text unless fetched. So the reading can't end on "won it back".
- **Still open (Hester, round 2):** point 3 on a page (he kept writing and singing: "Yankee Dollar", later recordings; Smithsonian Folkways liner notes or similar). Without it the story stops at the court record and fails point 3.
- **Guards:** the joke's target is said as what the soldiers' dollars were doing to the island, in the court's own spirit, never the women and never the lyric's subject; never "banned for its bite" (the radio refusal was for drink and the brand name); never "he won", the court record names him as the writer; the censors are Khan's testimony, said as such; colonial history plain.
- **Ending (so it holds after the page):** not credit or money (*Asked In*, *Still Yours*). The court put his name on it, and the answer to a stolen joke is the next one.
- **Backup:** none yet. A lead for Hester, same island, so a fallback only: calypsonians singing against Trinidad's 1930s censorship of their own songs (e.g. Atilla the Hun). Needs pages.
