# Anchors: outlaw-jester (The Subverter), r5 (Hester, after audit v2)

Card: `_studio/fact-cards/lord-invader-rum-and-coca-cola.md` (F1–F18, C1–C4). Story: Lord Invader and "Rum and Coca-Cola" (Wren r1: lead). Primary: *Khan v. Leo Feist* (1947, aff'd 2d Cir. 1947), *Baron v. Leo Feist* (1948), Oxford, *Joy*, *Codex*. Secondary: Cowley, Smithsonian Folkways SFW 40454 notes (2000), cited "SFW p. n". | story | During the war, Rupert Grant, a Trinidad calypso singer whose stage name was Lord Invader, wrote the words of "Rum and Coca-Cola" (*Khan*, Finding 1; month conflicts across sources, so no month). It was a hit on the island, and the 1948 court says both soldiers and islanders sang it a great deal (*Baron*; "together" and "as much as" are not on the page: audit A1, my own shorthand struck). | A joke aimed at the people with the money, sung openly, and the people it was about sang along. | test 1; the one true thing (the joke gets a true thing past the people it's about) |
| target | The 1947 judge read its chorus as a comment on a change in Trinidad's economy, too big for the calypso tents to leave alone (*Khan*). SFW p. 4: calypsonians called the American bases, slyly, "the American social invasion". | What it laughed at was the soldiers' dollars and what they were doing to the island. **Never the women, never the lyric's subject** (Wren). | test 1; the gift (the thing in charge seen at its real size) |
| setting | Mohamed Khan, who ran the tent Grant sang in, testified that calypsos had to go before a police-chaired board of censors before anyone could perform them, and that this one did (*Khan*). | Said under watch, and it still got through. **Khan's testimony about the tent's routine; nothing says the censors objected.** | test 1; what they hide (how much they mean it) |
| taken | Morey Amsterdam, an American comedian, came to Trinidad in September 1943 as a USO entertainer (*Baron*), heard it, took it home, and copyrighted it in 1944 as his own work (with Sullavan and Baron) (*Khan*; SFW p. 4). The Andrews Sisters' record was a hit, and its first 200,000 copies credited Amsterdam with the words and the music (*Baron*). | The joke taken by another joker, and sung back by millions who never heard what it was about. | test 2; the fear (the joke taken, sung back harmless) |
| radio | Many US radio networks at first refused to play it, as a song about an alcoholic drink (Oxford pdf 504). SFW p. 14 adds advertising and the lyrics. | The bite wasn't the problem: the drink was. **Never "banned for its bite".** | Wren's guard; test 2 only as the drink |
| record | The New York court found that Grant wrote the words (Finding 1) and that the taking was "deliberate and intentional" (*Khan*; dossier only since v3, cut for length, not corrected). It accepted his own protest that he was the composer as the basis of the decision. **The plaintiff was Khan, who held the rights** (Grant on the stand: "I don't own no rights"). The tune was Lionel Belasco's 1906 "L'Année Passée", which Grant had learned and changed a few notes of (*Baron*). | It landed on the record as true: a court wrote down whose words they were. **"His words", never "his song" or "his tune"; never "he sued", "he won", "he got it back".** | test 4 |
| kept joking | In January 1946, with the case still open, he recorded "Yankee Dollar" in New York. *Down Beat* called it "a sort of sequel" (SFW pp. 12–13). Its chorus makes his own hit the punchline: having sung "Rum and Coca-Cola" gets him nowhere without the dollars. | He didn't stop to grieve the stolen joke. He made a new one, partly at his own expense. **Chorus only; the verses are about women.** "At his own expense" is our reading. | test 3; the gift |
| kept aiming up | In March 1959 he recorded "Crisis in Arkansas" (Folkways FW 8733), aimed at Governor Faubus, who had kept nine Black students out of Little Rock's Central High in 1957. Its chorus teases the governor for wearing a black bow tie and tuxedo while he called Black people names (SFW pp. 23–24). | Years after the theft he was still laughing up, at a man in power, out loud. **Bow-tie joke only;** Wren rules on "political side today" (my view: segregation is history, not a party). | test 3; test 1; the one true thing |
| after | The defendants didn't pay him until seven years later. Folkways letters show he was always short of money, even after the settlement (SFW pp. 4–5). He kept recording for Moses Asch until a last session on 6 June 1961, months before he died (SFW pp. 5–6; **death year conflicts inside SFW: no year**). | The ending holds, at a price: the court gave him the words, not a fortune. What he kept was the next song. **No dollar figure** ($132,000 vs $150,000). | test 5; the fear (powerlessness answered by the next joke, not by money) |
| drink | Rum with Coca-Cola is older than the song: it was already popular in Venezuela by 1911 (Oxford pdf 107). The Cuba Libre is "also known as" Rum and Coca-Cola (Oxford pdf 613). *Joy* says the song made it popular in the 1940s (pdf 71). | The everyday drink the song made famous. **Never "his drink" or "he named it".** Tomás: call it "rum and Coca-Cola" in the text. | the drink |
| twist | In the classic Cuba Libre, lime juice lifts a plain rum and Coke, because lime peel is a key ingredient in most colas: the juice brings that out and cuts the sweetness (*Codex* p. 213). *Joy* (pdf 272–273, under CUBA LIBRE): it needs at least an ounce of lime to balance the cola; seldom thought much of, but heavenly when made properly. | The sweet, cheerful drink everyone sang along to, made with the bite left in. **"Most colas" is the *Codex*'s scope: never "Coca-Cola's recipe has lime" or "what the cola was hiding"** (it's secret, card C2; audit A6). The *Codex* classic is white rum over three cubes: ours is a riff (aged rum, full glass, bitters, and the lime doubled from the *Codex*'s ¼ oz to 15 ml; *Joy* asks for at least an ounce, so never "twice the usual": audit v2 B1). | the gift; the drink |
| ingredient | Angostura bitters are made in Trinidad (*Codex* p. 14); there a bottle sits on every table, for cooking and as a condiment (Oxford pdf 111). The recommended rum, Angostura 1919, comes from the same company's distillery (rum from the 1930s, Trinidad Distillers from 1949: Oxford pdf 114); the substitute rum isn't Trinidadian, so the reading says "the rum I'd reach for" (audit A5). | The island's own bitter on top of the famous sweet drink. A joke always tastes of where it was made. **Our interpretation.** | the drink; test 4 |
| caveat (bottle) | No page describes a Trinidad rum style. The only Trinidad rum page is one company's: Angostura's rums, column-distilled and aged in bourbon barrels (Oxford pdf 114), from a distillery founded in 1949, after the song. | If the base is Trinidadian it's a named, recommended bottle, never "a Trinidad-style rum". | Tomás |
| caveat (veto) | Two published historical Coca-Cola formulas (Beal's notebook, in the *Atlanta Journal-Constitution*, 1979; Pemberton's, via Pendergrast) list nutmeg oil (This American Life, "The Recipe", 2011). Coca-Cola says the real formula is still secret (Reuters via NBC, 2011). No library page either way. | The safe side is `nuts`. Tomás's call; any change to *The Way It Felt* goes to Robin as a note. | Tomás; the veto floor |
