## whoYouAre

Someone opens a jar, and before anyone has said a word you're standing in a kitchen you haven't been in for years. It happens to you all the time. A smell on a stairwell, the first bite of a plum, the weight of an old wool blanket, and you're somewhere else, completely. You can't always remember what year it was. You can remember exactly what the bread tasted like. And it works the other way too: whatever is in front of you right now, you're really tasting it.

What you play down is how hard it hits. You say "mm, lovely", because you've seen the eyebrow go up across the table, the one that says it's only bread. It's never only bread to you. The life that frightens you is one with the senses turned down: the same lunch at the same desk, a menu somebody else chose, a reason needed for every thing you want. Quieter than that is the meal with someone who isn't tasting, which can be lonelier than eating alone. But look at what you do with the good things. You tear the warm end off the loaf and hand it over. You crush a leaf under someone's nose and say "smell this". For a moment they're fully there. And years later, the taste that takes them somewhere has you in it. That's a lovely thing to leave in someone's life, and you've left it in more lives than you know.

## yours

1. A man who supplied cider to the London bar where Dick Bradsell worked once brought in three liqueurs: a blackcurrant, a raspberry and a blackberry. It was the blackberry that did it. "As soon as I tried it," he said, "I was back in that blackberry patch on the Isle of Wight," the island where he grew up. "It was spot-on, that flavour."
2. He made a drink around it, with gin, lemon and sugar: a British drink, at a time when, as he put it, the scene was totally vodka. The shape he already had, a short version of a Singapore Sling he'd made at another club. The blackberry gave it its fruit and its name: the Bramble, after the bush the berries grow on. At Fred's, the Soho members' club where he worked, it was popular straight off, and before long it had crossed the Atlantic.
3. I like to think that, for a second, people at Fred's stood in a blackberry patch on an island they'd never seen, and nobody had to tell them. That's how it is with you. A taste doesn't only please you. It takes you somewhere, and you can't help wanting to take someone along. He didn't stop to ask whether a blackberry liqueur was the fashionable thing. The taste was right, so he followed it.
4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Bramble, now counted among the great modern classics: gin, lemon juice and a little sugar, shaken and poured over crushed ice, then the blackberry liqueur poured over last, so it runs down through the ice in dark threads. My change is in that pour. Into the blackberry I stir a teaspoon of balsamic vinegar, the dark, sweet Italian kind, a cook's trick with berries. I like to think the berries he remembered were the ones eaten warm off the bush at the end of summer, soft and almost like wine, so the vinegar is there to make the blackberry taste riper and darker, the way those do. It's the sort of thing that raises an eyebrow. Then people taste it.
5. So here's something to do, and not with this glass. Find the taste that takes you somewhere, the one you could never quite explain, and give it to someone you love. Cook it, pick it, track it down. Don't tell them the story first. Just hand it over, and then let them tell you where it took them. You've been taking people into your places for years. This time you'll get to go into one of theirs, and you'll know that the person across the table is tasting too.
