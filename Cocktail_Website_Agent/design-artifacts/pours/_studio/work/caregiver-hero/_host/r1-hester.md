📜 **Hester:** Wren, before anyone reaches for the St Bernard: the brandy keg is a legend. The Natural History Museum in Bern, which has Barry, says the dogs' main job was finding the way back to the hospice in a blizzard. Barry "is said to have" helped save 40 people, and he spent his last two years retired in Bern (d. 1814). The library has nothing on the keg except Wondrich's joke (Imbibe pdf 85). So the keg goes in labelled, as an image, or not at all, and nothing gets anchored on it.

The mirror I'd bring you is a person: **Félix Kir**, the Kir's namesake. In WWI he was a medical orderly and won the Croix de guerre. In October 1940 the Germans arrested him for helping the people of Dijon and for resisting. Two military tribunals sentenced him to death, and he was released that December. In 1943 he was arrested again, accused of getting escapees to England. In January 1944 a man came into his home and shot him. In 1945 he was elected mayor at 69. Source for all of that is the Assemblée nationale's Sycomore biography (secondary, official).

The better turn is that the drink wasn't his. Dijon had been serving white wine with cassis at receptions for decades as "Blanc Cassis". The name moved to it only after his election, because of who he'd been (Oxford KIR, pdf 1133). He was honoured with a drink he never invented, made from what his own region grew. For someone whose fear is selfishness, I think that's the shape: nothing in it was taken for himself.

What stays out: the "5,000 Longvic escapes" (not in the official bio), the naming-rights story, and any motive we can't source. The Blanc Cassis's 1904 date is also only "often claimed" (Oxford, KIR), so it stays out too. Tomás, it's wine and cassis, so no vetoes, and it isn't a warm drink. I dropped Frank Meier of the Ritz: Oxford calls his Resistance record "murky" and then has him redirecting clients' tabs into his own account (pdf 1276).
