# Pour — More Than One Head · The Sorcerer's Apprentice (magician-innocent)

## Cocktail

- **name:** More Than One Head
- **tagline:** You never worry it won't work. You worry it will.
- **glassware:** tall tumbler (about 400 ml), filled with ice cubes
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 30 ml | strawberry and lemon cordial (made ahead, below) | the first measure; each one after it is double |
| 60 ml | Johnnie Walker Red Label blended Scotch whisky (40%), recommended | the name on the label is the 1880s–1890s nickname "Johnny Walker", spelled "Johnnie", which the company came round to in 1908, after Alexander Walker's death. Or any blended Scotch whisky (40–46%) |
| 120 ml | soda water, well chilled | double again |

**Strawberry and lemon cordial (about 300 ml, ten drinks):** 100 g ripe hulled strawberries and 100 g white sugar, blended cold until the sugar has gone, pressed through a fine sieve; made the *Codex*'s way (Blended Strawberry Syrup, p. 47: equal weights of fruit and sugar, blended cold, sieved), then lengthened with the same volume of fresh lemon juice (about 150 ml, four or five lemons; mine). Fridge, up to a week (my craft call).

**method**
1. Make the cordial ahead: blend the strawberries and sugar until smooth and no grains of sugar are left, press the purée through a fine sieve to take out the seeds, measure it, and stir in the same amount of fresh lemon juice. Keep it in the fridge.
2. Fill a tall tumbler with ice cubes.
3. Pour in 30 ml of the cordial, then 60 ml of the Scotch, and stir a few times to mix and chill them.
4. Top with 120 ml of cold soda water, poured gently, and lift the drink once from the bottom with the spoon.

**closingLine:** *Thirty, sixty, a hundred and twenty. When it starts growing like that, say the "almost" out loud.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*A whisky merchant wrote he could almost wish his business would halve. This glass doubles instead.*

**whoYouAre**
You start things. A dinner that turns into a monthly fixture. A small idea that ends up as a group of forty. A side project that people you've never met now ask you about. Usually you start before you're quite ready, because the excitement won't wait, and finding out as you go is half the fun.

What hardly anyone guesses is this. Failure isn't what keeps you up. Success is: the kind that keeps going faster than you can follow, in directions you didn't pick, with your name on it. So you keep one small wish to yourself: that it would slow down for a week. You keep it quiet, because who complains when something's working? That wish doesn't betray the joy. It's the price of it. And here's what the joy gives everyone else: a lot of the good things around you are only there because you couldn't wait to begin them.

**yours**

1. In 1820 a man called John Walker bought a grocery, wine and spirits shop in Kilmarnock, in Scotland, and began selling whisky. His son Alexander turned that whisky into a real brand. He had trademarked his blend, Old Highland Whisky, before 1870. The firm opened an office in London around 1871. Between 1865 and 1880 the business grew fourfold, and from 1880 it boomed.

2. In 1885 Alexander wrote this: "It seems that my business doubles itself every year, and I feel sometimes as if I could almost wish it would double itself back because it is really more than one head can carry." This wasn't a beginner talking. He'd been building that brand for twenty years or more. And something else was running ahead of him. He wrote that quality would stand on its merit, and the firm didn't advertise. Into that silence, in the 1880s and 1890s, came a nickname, "Johnny Walker". Popular culture celebrated it. The Walker family didn't: they felt it was disrespectful to the founder.

3. When I read that letter, I hear you. Not in the size of it, but in the "almost": the delight and the alarm in one breath, and a wish nobody would really want granted. And then the name: the thing picked up a name out in the world, and nobody asked the family. That's the mess you're afraid of. Not a disaster. A direction you didn't pick. In 1886, a year on from the letter, the business became a limited company and brought long-standing employees in alongside his sons. Whatever the reason, I like to think that was the second head he'd been wishing for.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Whisky and Soda, the classic highball. Britain was already drinking it in his day, and in 1906, as it took over from hot toddies, the company brought out a new range. Every measure is double the one before: thirty millilitres of a strawberry and lemon cordial, sixty of blended Scotch, a hundred and twenty of soda. The sixty to a hundred and twenty is the proportion one of the great modern cocktail books gives for its ideal whisky highball, so the doubling is a proper recipe, not a stunt. The Scotch is a blend, whiskies from several distilleries married in one bottle. That's my nod to his letter: a whisky that was never one maker's work. I'd pick Johnnie Walker Red Label. Today its label carries that nickname, spelled "Johnnie". I added strawberry as an ode to that second head: a strawberry is many tiny fruits carried as one. One of the compounds behind its scent smells of baked bread, and I think that's why it sits so easily with a whisky made from grain.

5. So keep starting things before you're ready. Most of your good ones began that way. But when one of them starts to run ahead of you, don't keep the "almost" to yourself. Say it while it still makes you laugh, and give someone half of it to hold. It won't slow down. You just won't be carrying it alone, so you'll finally get to enjoy watching it grow.
