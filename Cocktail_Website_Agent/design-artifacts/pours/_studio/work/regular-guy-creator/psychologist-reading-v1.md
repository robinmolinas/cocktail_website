## whoYouAre

There's something you're good at that doesn't match the picture people have of you. A cake nobody believed you'd made. A song you only sing in the car. A drawing on the back of a work envelope. When someone finds out and says "you never said", you laugh and change the subject. Part of you has always believed you had to pick: be good at it, or stay one of them.

What you'd never say out loud is that you want both. And here's the part you've got wrong: when it shows, nobody you love thinks you've got above yourself. They're proud it's one of theirs who did it. That's what your gift gives the people around you, and it deserves to be said: it tells them what someone like them can do.

## yours

1. Tom Nichol grew up in Tullibody, in Scotland, near the distillery where his father worked. He started there himself at seventeen, and by his own account he went in screaming and shouting. For more than thirty years he did jobs nobody outside a distillery hears about: making liquid carbon dioxide, then the plain grain spirit other drinks are built on. He once said that kind of spirit gets little thanks, because it tastes of nothing and smells of nothing.

2. In 2005 he joined the team making gin at the same distillery, where Tanqueray had been made since 1998. Within a year he was Tanqueray's master distiller, the person in charge of how it tastes. He's credited with new gins, with old recipes brought back, and with keeping the famous London Dry the way it should be. When he retired in 2015, the gin trade gave him a lifetime award. He said it was completely unexpected, and that his father would have been very proud of him.

3. He never moved far: he lived about forty years a couple of miles from where he was born. And he was known as a passionate, even short-tempered champion of one idea, that a big distillery doesn't have to make worse spirit than a small one. I like to think that's the part you'd recognise: the talent turned up right where life had put him, and once people noticed, he didn't leave that place behind. He stood up for it.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a gin Old-Fashioned, the classic of spirit, a little sugar and bitters, built in an ordinary tumbler over ice from the freezer tray. The gin is Tanqueray London Dry, and there's nothing to cover it. It's said to be made with only four plants: juniper, coriander, angelica and liquorice, and liquorice is the one you notice last, a hint at the finish. So I've turned the sugar into a liquorice root syrup, as a nod to a talent that was in that distillery long before anyone named it. It adds nothing from outside the bottle. It brings forward what was already there.

5. So show it to the people who knew you first, before strangers. Make the cake for your own table, sing with the window down on the way to your family's. You'll find out you never had to choose, and they'll get to be proud of something that's theirs too.

## Resonance

- **As the guest:** "Part of you has always believed you had to pick: be good at it, or stay one of them." This is the "how did they know" line. The private one is "you laugh and change the subject". The gift is said out loud ("it tells them what someone like them can do"). The position gives something back: their pride, and the end of the choice.
- **As the sceptic:** Is "something you're good at that doesn't match the picture" true of anyone? Half of it is, which is why the three examples are concrete and the line that lands is the choice, not the talent. No "genius", no flattery of the gift itself. The examples are mixed (a kitchen, a car, a work envelope), with no job or class named as lowly.
- **Siblings:** not *As It Was* (no shrug, no "it's nothing", no "showing off"); not *Not Only the Way* (no "stop saying it was nothing"); not *In Your Own Hand* (no "put your name on it"); not *Whoever Comes In* (no eyes or looking; "people noticed" is said once, about Nichol); not *Anyone Would Have* (no "singled out"). Not the Have A Go Hero (he had the training: thirty years of it).
- **Verdict:** nearly ready. Open for the audit: y1 "more than thirty years" (F6, his own "32 years"); y2 "the gin trade" for the Gin Guild; y3 "short-tempered" as my plain word for "irascible"; y4 "the one you notice last" (Oxford's "just a hint of licorice" at the finish); y4 Old-Fashioned as "the classic of spirit, a little sugar and bitters" (Codex p. 5).
