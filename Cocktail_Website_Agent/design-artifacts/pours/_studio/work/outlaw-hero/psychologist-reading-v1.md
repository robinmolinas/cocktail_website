## whoYouAre
Someone sighs: it's a pity, but those are the rules. You've already stopped listening. You're working out which rule it is, who it's really for, and what it would take to get round it by Friday. Not for you. Usually it isn't even your fight. That's never once stopped you.

People put it down to nerve, or a taste for trouble. It's neither. It's that you can't watch. Being told to wait, to go through the proper channels, to leave it to the people who run things while it carries on happening: that's the thing that truly frightens you. And there's a part you'd never say out loud: some of it is for you. Doing something, even something that costs you, is how you stop feeling helpless.

So you fix it, quietly, one person at a time: a rule bent just for them, a cost you cover without mentioning it. It works. What it gives people is real: with you around, "those are the rules" stops being the end of the matter, and that matters more than you let yourself think. But you know the other half. The rule is still there in the morning, and so is the next person it lands on.

## yours

1. For nearly fifty years, Nigeria's own spirit was against the law. Ogogoro is distilled from the sap of palm trees. Under British colonial rule, a treaty of 1919 banned distilling it at home while foreign spirits could still be sold, gin above all. People made it anyway, more and more of them, and it stayed illegal even after independence in 1960.

2. In 1968, a Nigerian educator called Tai Solarin got himself arrested for having some. He did it on purpose, to show everyone how unfair it was to ban a local spirit and allow foreign ones. That September, a Lagos paper's headline read "Solarin Freed of Gin Charge". The ban ended that same year. And Tai Solarin didn't drink.

3. How it went, we don't know. I like to think he didn't need to say much: he just made the rule do, where everyone could see, the one thing it couldn't defend. It was never his drink. He picked the fight up anyway, and he made sure the charge had his name on it, nobody else's. I recognise you in that. Not the trouble. Choosing who it costs.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is stirred, cold and strong, a close cousin of the Manhattan, that great classic. It stands on two spirits in equal measure: Pedro's, a Nigerian ogogoro made from palm sap in villages and refined in Lagos, and a London dry gin. Same jigger, same glass, and neither stands in for the other. I split them as a nod to what Tai Solarin was arrested for: one rule for both. Sweet vermouth brings them together, with a dash of orange bitters. [spark: Tomás's, with its one-clause tie] [glass: Tomás's]

5. You'll go on fixing things one person at a time, and you should. But once, pick the rule that keeps sending you back, and stop working round it on the quiet. Do it where people can see who did it, so the rule has to answer for itself. Not for the thanks. So it changes for everyone, and you get the thing you've wanted all along: never having to watch that one again.
