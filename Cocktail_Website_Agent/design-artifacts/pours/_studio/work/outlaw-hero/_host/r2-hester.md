📜 **Hester:** Wren, I can't give you the second page you asked for, and I'd rather say so now than send you something thin. I've written up the search in `_studio/fact-cards/tai-solarin-ogogoro.md`.

What we have is a little more than one sentence, though. Oxford's own bibliography for OGOGORO (pdf 1418) cites the newspaper: *Lagos Daily Times*, 14 September 1968, p. 3, headline "Solarin Freed of Gin Charge". So in 1968 there was a charge, it was for "gin", and he was freed of it. That's the outcome. Careful with the date: the 14th is when the paper ran, not the day he walked free. Drinkabl's piece mixes the two up and copies Oxford's wording, so I'm not citing it. I didn't find a second account of the arrest itself, in the 1968–69 newspaper archive or anywhere else. Nothing tells us where it happened, what day, or what the court did beyond "freed".

**The only scene, and it's a legend.** *Vanguard* (Josef Omorotionmwan, opinion column, 9 July 2015) says he walked into a police station with a bottle of ogogoro and demanded to be arrested, and that this got the ban lifted. But it dates that to the "early 1960s", one columnist with no source, against Oxford and against the 1968 headline. My lean: leave it out. If you want it at all, it goes in as "the story goes", with no anchor resting on it.

**The sources disagree on when the ban ended.** Olúpàyímọ (*Yoruba Studies Review*, 2017, pp. 204–205) says ogogoro was legalised at independence. A gin charge in 1968 counts against that, and Oxford is the only source that's both specific and tied to a dated record. So Oxford's account stands, but the cause stays at Oxford's strength. The reading can say "it changed in 1968, when he got himself arrested for it", or the plainer "the year he got himself arrested for it". It never says he changed it single-handed, and it names no law and no exact date.

Here's where that leaves your test. Points 1, 2 and 4 hold on Oxford's sentence plus the headline. Point 3 holds at "when" strength. What I can't support is a scene. So the arrest gets told in one plain line, with no station, no bottle in his hand and no courtroom.
