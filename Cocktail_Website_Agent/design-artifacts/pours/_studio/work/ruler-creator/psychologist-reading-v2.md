## whoYouAre

You notice the thing that's slightly off. The line on the page that sits a millimetre low. The dish that should have come out hot. The word on a menu that doesn't mean what it says. Most people let it pass. You can't, and you'd rather seem cold than let it go out like that.

People take it for pride, or for your taste. It isn't, mostly. At the end of every piece of work there's someone who'll never know what you turned down for them, and they're who you're thinking of. You hardly ever mention it, because it sounds like asking to be thanked. So your no arrives alone, without its reason. What you fear is something careless going out under a name that was meant to stand for something. And here's what that care gives: around you, good becomes normal. People raise their own bar near you, often without noticing they have, and whoever's at the end gets it as it should be. That's rare, and it's worth a lot.

## yours

1. In late-1990s London, the fruit "Martini" was everywhere. People loved those drinks, and plenty still do. A bartender from Camden called Wayne Collins hated them, and wrote an article against them in a magazine called *Class*. His quarrel was with the name. As he told Robert Simonson in 2014, he didn't like that it was called a Martini.

2. Here's the part I love. The way Collins remembers it, he hadn't always liked the real one either. He'd ask why anyone would drink gin and vermouth, and his teacher, Lee Chappell, would tell him he'd better start liking it, because it was for the guests, not for him. Chappell, by the way, ran a place in Covent Garden called the Long Island Iced Tea Shop. Nobody in this story is a snob.

3. I can't know what was in Collins's head when he wrote that article, but I like to think it was Chappell's lesson: not his own taste, but a promise made to someone else, about what a name should mean. He didn't stop at no, either. In 2000, as bar manager at High Holborn, he put on a menu of drinks he'd found in old books, ones that weren't on other London menus. That bar influenced a lot of people, and the training programme he went on to build, Mixxit, is credited with driving up service standards in the trade. That's the shape of what you do: the no comes with a better yes behind it, and people around you start holding the same line.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a whiskey Daisy, one of the drinks on his High Holborn menu, and an old classic you'll still rarely find on a list: bourbon, fresh lemon, just a touch of orange curaçao in the old style, then a splash of soda. I serve it up and very cold, in a fine stemmed glass and without ice, the way he served his Sazeracs. And I've added two teaspoons of dry vermouth, as a nod to the gin and vermouth Collins was told to start liking, for the guests. You won't taste it as vermouth. You'll taste a sharper, drier, rounder drink because of it.

5. I wouldn't ask you to lower the bar. Please don't, for anyone. But from now on, when you turn something down, add the why, the way Chappell did. A no that comes with its reason stops sounding cold. It sounds like care, because that's what it is. And the reason travels where you can't: people start keeping your standard in rooms you'll never walk into, because at last they understand why.

**closing line (Wren's words, on Tomás's gesture):** *Measure the vermouth: it's only two teaspoons. And when you turn something down, add the why.*

## Rulings for the room

- **The vermouth spark: yes.** It's the reveal in the glass: a taste learned for someone else, put in a drink made for someone else. The guard: no "pick it out", which is *Making the Calls*' epigraph ("one dash… you'll never pick out. The rest tastes brighter for it"). So y4 says "You won't taste it as vermouth", and never "tastes X for it".
- **Daiquiri, not Sidecar:** accepted, it's Tomás's structure call and he's right on the Codex. The reading only says "a whiskey Daisy", never a family.
- **Closing line:** Tomás's gesture kept, wording changed. "When the answer is no" is too close to *Go On*'s "give your answer", its own sibling's closing line. "Put the why in" is fine as a thought. My version: *Measure the vermouth: it's only two teaspoons. And when you turn something down, add the why.* Tomás, it's your line, so say if "measure" fights your method.
- **Name:** On Their Behalf, agreed. The phrase is nowhere in the reading or the tagline.

## Resonance

**As the guest.** The tagline catches me out: yes, I'd rather seem cold, and nobody has ever said why out loud. The whoYouAre scenes (the millimetre, the dish, the word on the menu) are mine. "It isn't, mostly" is honest: there's some pride in it, and I'm glad it doesn't pretend otherwise. "Someone who'll never know what you turned down for them" is the line. The story surprises me, because the purist started out disliking the real thing, and the Long Island Iced Tea Shop makes me laugh. Nobody's sneering at my friends' pornstar martinis. The vermouth is the lesson in the glass. y5 doesn't ask me to stop. It asks me to explain, and gives me something I want: the standard keeping itself where I can't watch.

**As the sceptic.**
- **Barnum:** "you notice the thing that's slightly off" is common, but it's pinned at once to "you'd rather seem cold" and to the unseen person at the end.
- **Siblings:** the Diva is seen insisting and proved right, and here nobody sees and nobody's proved right. The Judge unmasks fakes, and here the quarrel is with a name, not a lie. *Go On*'s calm listener has a different no ("say it plainly") from this one ("add the why"). Watch the pair in Robin's review.
- **Creator cousins:** the Auteur's "let someone else make the second" isn't here, because the guest keeps the standard. The Craftsman's "about you"/"properly" are absent. *Down the Line*'s "see the finished thing" is absent.
- **History share:** y1–y3 carry most of it, about 45% by my count. To verify after the lint.
