## whoYouAre

"Hold on, don't move," you say, at the kitchen table, on an evening nobody planned. People laugh, because nothing's happening. You know something is: the light across the table, what everyone ordered, the thing someone said that they'll have forgotten by Friday. Other people wait for something worth remembering. You notice it's already here.

Ask you about it and you'll shrug: it's nothing, just a picture. Making a fuss of it would feel like showing off. The shrug covers a fear you don't say out loud: the blur. A whole year going by and looking like one day. Keeping the ordinary is how you make sure it counted. And the people around you get to see their own everyday life the way you saw it: worth keeping.

## yours

1. In 1916, a bartender at a hotel in Times Square published a book of drinks. His name was Hugo Ensslin, and before he came to New York at sixteen he had trained as a painter and, of all things, a photographer. The book didn't sell, and he was never one of the city's famous bartenders. But what he wrote down was plain and exact: the drinks New York was really ordering, just before Prohibition closed the bars. Historians say no other book shows it as faithfully. He even named a brand of gin in one recipe, El Bart, probably because El Bart helped pay for the book. Nobody tidied that out either.

2. One of the drinks he wrote down was the Aviation: gin, lemon, a cherry liqueur called maraschino, and a violet liqueur called crème de violette, which tints it a pale sky blue. In 1930, a far more famous London cocktail book printed the Aviation without the violette. Nobody recorded why. But that colourless version is the one that survived. The colour only came back after his book turned up again in 2003, and the violet liqueur returned to America in 2007.

3. I like to think the violet was the detail that made it true. It wasn't a flourish. It was part of the drink as one bartender wrote it down, and only that plain record had kept it. That's what you do. You don't make things prettier than they are. You keep them as they were, colour, advert and all.

4. So yours is the Aviation with his violette put back: gin, lemon, a little sugar, a teaspoon of maraschino and two dashes of the violet liqueur, as his book gives it, shaken and poured into a stemmed glass straight from the freezer. Two dashes don't turn it purple. They give it a pale blue you only see if you look for it. And many London dry gins carry orris, the root of an iris, whose scent has long been compared to fresh violets, so the colour isn't a stranger in the glass.

5. One thing I'd ask. Stop calling it nothing. Somewhere in everything you've kept this year there's an ordinary day that someone else has already forgotten. Print it, and give it to the person in it. They don't know yet what their Tuesday looked like. You do.
