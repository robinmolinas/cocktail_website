## whoYouAre

On an ordinary day you can seem like too much. You take the long view when the rest of the room is busy with the week, and you mention what could go wrong before anyone else is worried. People nod, and talk about something else. Then the worst day comes, the one where everyone else is lost for words, and they all look at you. You've had the words for a while.

You don't say how much the quiet stretches sting. Seeing it coming isn't much comfort while nobody's listening. And part of you, a part you'd rather not mention, steadies in a crisis, because at last you make sense. The storm doesn't frighten you. Being the one in charge when it wins does, and being remembered for that. So you work out what you'd say long before anyone asks, and on the day people have nothing left to stand on, you hand them a sentence that holds. People get through their worst days because of how you talk them through it.

## yours

1. In the 1890s, Patrick Duffy ran the bar of the Ashland House in New York. Someone who knew him remembered a waxed moustache and a carnation in his lapel. Then he left the trade, and for years he ran rooming houses in Brooklyn while the bars he'd known were closed by law.

2. In December 1933 he told a reporter how his book came about. He'd been watching drink coming back for three years, he said, and with not much to do but look after his two houses, he'd started gathering the recipes. The book was on sale days before the ban ended, and it holds the detail I love most. The mixtures people had got used to in the dry years went in, because people asked for them, but each got a little circle: he wouldn't want it said he'd recommended them. The ones he thought belonged to the best tradition got a star. A cocktail historian counts it among the two or three books that did the most to bring the craft back.

3. Maybe the circles were only good manners. I like to think they were more than that: a man who knew the day had come, cared what would be said about it afterwards, and made sure nobody could say he'd let it slide. You'd have drawn those circles too. You know a moment like that gets remembered, and you want to be remembered for what you stood behind.

4. The cocktail I've made for you starts from his Herald Punch, the one drink of his that caught on, which he dedicated to a newspaper, the *Herald*, in 1901: rye, orange juice, a spoon of sugar and just enough rum to flavour it, shaken well and strained into a sour glass. A cocktail historian calls it a corker. Orange on its own is too gentle to hold a sour up, so I make the juice as sharp as lime, with a pinch of the acids that make lemons and apples tart. You still taste orange. It just holds. I did that as a nod to the work he did before anyone needed it.

5. So I'll take a side. The quiet years aren't the wait. They're where the words come from. Next time you see something coming and nobody's listening, write it down anyway, plainly, and star the part you'd stand behind. You don't need everything to go wrong to be worth hearing. And on the day itself, you won't have to look for anything to say. You'll only have to hand it over.
