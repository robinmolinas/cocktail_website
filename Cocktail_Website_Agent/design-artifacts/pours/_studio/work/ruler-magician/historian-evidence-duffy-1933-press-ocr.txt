Patsy, white-haired and neatly brown-suited, with blue eyes gleaming behind precise gold -edge glasses, tells how he came to write the book 40 Years Behind the Bar "I've been 40 years behind the bar," he says, "and I know every drink they mix. "I had my own experience to go on and I. visited bars and read up on the subject. We Join the Procession For the last few years I have managed to get a vicarious thrill from reading books containing cocktail recipes. Invariably they aspired to be more than recipe books they contained poems, songs and drawings supposed to suggest the exhilarating effect of inebriation. Now comes Patrick Gavin Duffy's "The official Mixer's Manual," a loose-leaf book jammed with brief directions on how to concoct any number of drinks. There is no poetry in it. There are no funny drawings of fellows in evening clothes embracing the props of the elevated. It takes for granted that you want not entertainment but a drink, and it provides the information telling how to make one. Patrick Gavin Duffy was chief bartender of the old Ashland House when Edwin Booth used to go there for pick-me-up, possibly still wearing the winding sheet of Macbeth. He writes sane foreword that gives one respect for his calling. "Bartending," he says. an old and honorable trade.' It 1s not a profession, and have no sympathy with those who try to make it anything but what it was." And | then he goes on to give some good advice. A bartender, says he, should be neatly shaved and have hands and nails immaculately clean. A fresh, white linen coat is to be preferred to a black alpaca-"and I personally fancy a carnation." "I can not too much deplore the custom which has become prevalent of late of free and general conversation between bartenders and patrons," he continues. "The bartender should answer civilly and briefly every reasonable question that is put to him, but he should not enter into protracted conversation with the customers. Cocktails should be stirred and not shaken. "A stirred cocktail is clear and fresh and retains its vitality: shaken cocktail is muddy in appearance and has had so much ice diluted into it that it is a very insipid affair. It is one of my fondest hopes that the highball will, again take its place as the leading American drink."
