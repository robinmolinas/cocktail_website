## The person
The sheet: trusts only what can be tested; measures carefully, writes everything down, follows the evidence when it's inconvenient. "Rigour is how they keep a messy world in order. The result that turns out to be false is the one that haunts them." Ruler underneath: structure, steadiness; fears chaos, vulnerability, failure.

**The one true thing (no sibling has it):** they write it all down *so they can be checked*, including by themselves. Most people keep their reasoning in their heads, where nobody can find the mistake. This person puts it on paper, where anyone can. They'd rather be checked than believed. Being believed on trust scares them a little, because trust is how a wrong result survives.

**The Sage × Ruler knot:** the notes are how they keep the world steady (Ruler), and the same notes are what expose them (Sage honesty). Writing it down is control and vulnerability at once. They choose the exposure every time. That's the part nobody sees.

**What they hide:** the notebook is how they stay calm, not proof that they are. And there's usually one result they still go back to: the one they half-suspect. They don't mention it until they're sure.

**What they fear:** being the source of the wrong result that other people built on, because they trusted *them*. Not being wrong: being wrong *somewhere people stood*.

**What they give (said out loud, per Robin's rule):** when they say "I've checked", other people stop worrying. They make it safe to rely on something. And when they are wrong, they've left the way back marked, so nobody's lost for long.

**Against the cliché:** not cold. The careful ones are often the funniest company (Oxford gives Barnard "persnickety love of detail" *and* "a gregarious man of not inconsiderable humor"). The reading may let them be warm, even a little funny.

## Traps
- The label: "Scientist" is the personality's name, never a job we give them; no lab coats, no "the fact-checker of the group".
- Marie Curie (the sheet's example): radium is health; out.
- Diagnosis words: "control freak", "perfectionist", "obsessive". Say what they do.
- Flattery: "your rigour", "your precision". Recognise what it costs and what it gives.
- A cold reading. This person is often fun.
- A reading about being right. It's about being checkable, which means being wrong in the open.

## Story test
(1) Someone who wrote down exactly what was there where others generalised, with the dull minutiae on show; (2) whose record became what later people check against (still used, still the baseline); (3) best if the record has a gap or error that's on the page, and the record keeps its trust anyway; (4) warmth on the page against the cold cliché. Out: health, lucky discovery, Priestley, Arnold, "perfected", Jackson.

## Verdict on the plan's lead (Alfred Barnard): accept, with conditions
Barnard passes (1), (2) as I've reset it, and (4) on the page: "earliest and most accurate account", "precision in detailing the minutiae of much of the equipment", "the template for numerous books", reprinted; his 1887 counts are still the baseline other entries measure from ("The eighteen working distilleries Alfred Barnard had found in the republic's territory in 1887 were reduced to three", IRISH DISTILLERS pdf 1079; "In 1887, Alfred Barnard found twenty-eight distilleries operating in Ireland", pdf 2153; stills shown "as Alfred Barnard found it", pdf 2150, 2168); and "persnickety love of detail" with "not inconsiderable humor" (pdf 211).

**The plan's limb (2) doesn't survive the library.** The row's mirror was Oxford's inference that casks were "unimportant ... at that time" (BARNARD pdf 211). The same book contests it, and by the same writer (Dave Broom): "Whisky producers in the nineteenth century greatly prized the character given by ex-sherry casks. By the 1870s there was, however, insufficient sherry..." and paxarete "soon became standard practice" (PAXARETE pdf 1483). So no guest line may say casks were thought unimportant, or that Barnard (or his age) missed them.

**What can stand:** the plain fact that his book barely mentions casks ("the virtual absence of any mention of casks", pdf 211), with no reason given. Told as "his book hardly mentions the casks, and we don't know why", that's the Scientist's fear made gentle: the most careful record anyone made still has a gap, and it's still the one everyone trusts, because everything he did write down can be checked. Hester to rule whether the juxtaposition is fair; if not, the gap goes and the story stands on (1), (2) and (4).

**Conditions:**
1. "Regarded as the first 'whisky writer'": keep "regarded". "The earliest and most accurate account": Oxford's words, attributed, never ours.
2. Commissioned by a trade journal (*Harper's Weekly Gazette*), and he also wrote pieces for individual distilleries: never imply he was independent or unpaid.
3. "All of the whisky distilleries in Britain" (pdf 211) vs a UK book that covers Ireland: Hester settles the scope words. 1885 commission, published "two years later"; the reprint cited is of an 1889 edition: dates are Hester's.
4. Out: Caol Ila, Lagavulin and the blending houses (other pours' bottles and brands), Irish nationalist politics, the breweries tour unless it serves, Michael Jackson.
5. The "haunted by the false result" limb isn't on any page: it lives in the guest's half (whoYouAre and the position), never as Barnard's feeling.
6. No interpretation of why the casks are missing (no deception story, no "secret ingredient" angle: paxarete's Christmas-pudding line is charming but it would turn the gap into a mystery).
