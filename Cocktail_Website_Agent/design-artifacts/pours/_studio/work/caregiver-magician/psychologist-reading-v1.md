## whoYouAre

You can tell when someone is going to be all right, usually a good while before they can. A friend swears they'll never get over it, and you think: give it till spring. You don't say so, because it would sound like you weren't listening. You just keep turning up with something easy to say yes to: a walk, an errand, a Sunday that needs a second pair of hands. And one day they notice they've been getting better for a while, somewhere in all those Sundays.

You know that help can taste bitter. Being looked after can feel like being told you're not coping, so you make yours easy to take. What you'd never admit is the other side of it. The few who didn't get better stay with you. You go back over them, wondering what you missed, as if their mending were yours to finish. That's the price of believing in people before they do, and you've never once thought it wasn't worth paying.

## yours

1. In the 1830s, a young man from Marseille, still in his twenties, caught a fever in Algeria. He was treated the way everyone was then, with wine steeped with quinine and other bitters. He knew exactly what that tasted like.
2. The way it's told, that fever is what set Gaëtan Picon trying, from 1837, to make a tonic that tasted better. By 1840 he had a recipe: bitter orange peel and sweet orange peel together, with gentian (a bitter root), the bark that quinine comes from, and rhubarb. He opened a small distillery, and his amer, a bitter orange liqueur, was soon drunk in quantity across Algeria. Later it reached France.
3. I like to think he never forgot being the one handed the glass nobody wanted to drink, and that's why he didn't take the bitter out. He put something sweet beside it, so people would come back for it on their own. I can't tell you whether it made anyone better. I can tell you people kept choosing it: by 1909 it took about three million oranges a year. That's how your help works too. It stays honest, and it still gets taken.
4. So I made yours from his idea, not his bottle. The amer in it is a modern one, made with his recipe in mind, and it sits where a sweet orange liqueur usually would, beside cognac and lemon. That's the place for comfort, and I put the bitter there on purpose, with a spoon of homemade pomegranate syrup next to it. The bitter isn't hidden. It's just in good company.
5. The ones who didn't get better: put them down. Their mending was never yours to finish. You were the sweet beside the bitter, the reason the hard part got taken at all, and that was always the job. Next time you catch yourself going back over one of them, remember the Sundays instead.

## Resonance

**As the guest (a Healer).** Tagline: a little proud and a little caught, because I do think that, and I never say it. ¶1: "give it till spring" is my exact inner voice; the Sundays are how I actually do it. ¶2: "help can taste bitter" is my reason for disguising it; "the few who didn't get better stay with you" is the line I've never heard anyone say to me. That's the "real me" line. The story lets me see myself in the one who knew how the cure tasted and made it welcome, without calling me a patient. y5 tells me to put something down I didn't know I was carrying. Verdict: ready once the drink is fixed.

**As the sceptic.** Barnum: "you can tell when someone's going to be all right" narrows fast (you keep it to yourself, you disguise the help, you carry the failures). Sibling swaps: the Angel (brings calm; hides their own wants) has no belief in recovery and no failures carried. The Reassurer (laughter): no joke here, on purpose. The Understanding Spouse (waits, understands one person): the Healer acts for whoever's mending, and y5 is about outcomes, not saying something back. The Doctor (asks "since when?", says no): the opposite of making it easy to take. The Explorer (keeps the tries that went nowhere, share them): theirs is research shared; mine is self-blame put down. The Rescuer (in the crisis): the Healer works after it, over Sundays. Health: "fever", "tonic", "quinine" only in 1830s past tense; nothing says the drink or anything in it helps. "Easy to take" is about help, never the drink. Frames grepped against every pour: "Someone you love" (Lover, Doctor, Sculptor openers) avoided; "So yours" avoided; "count" (Campaigner's y5) avoided; "So here's where I'd push you" (Overnight) avoided; no rationed phrases. Epigraph avoids medicine: the Doctor's ("The bark… was medicine once") and the Explorer's ("meant to cure sailors") already own it. History: y1, y2 and half of y3, about 40% of yours. One voice, no guest pronouns, no archetype names, nothing in hand.
