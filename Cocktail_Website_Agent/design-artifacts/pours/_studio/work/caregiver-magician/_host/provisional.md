# Pour — Till Spring · The Healer (caregiver-magician)

## Cocktail

- **name:** Till Spring
- **tagline:** You believe they'll be all right before they do.
- **glassware:** a tumbler (about 300 ml), filled with ice
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 45 ml | Bigallet China-China Amer (*recommended*), or any bittersweet French orange amer of 30% or stronger | a modern amer made with the old Amer Picon recipe in mind: bitter orange and bitter roots |
| 1 tsp (5 ml) | pomegranate and orange syrup, homemade (below) | the sweet orange peel sits here, beside the bitter one |
| 120 ml | soda water, very cold | |
| 15 ml | cognac | poured last, so it rests on top |
| 1 | strip of orange peel | |
| | *for the syrup:* 250 g pomegranate juice (unsweetened, from a bottle is fine), 250 g sugar, the peel of 1 sweet orange | adapted from the *Cocktail Codex*'s house grenadine |

**method**
1. Make the syrup ahead: an hour before, or the day before. Peel one orange thinly, just the coloured part, and put the peel in a jar with the sugar. Rub the peel into the sugar with a spoon for a minute, until the sugar smells of orange.
2. Pour in the pomegranate juice and stir, or shake the closed jar, until the sugar has dissolved. No heat. Leave it for an hour, then take out the peel. It keeps in the fridge for about two weeks.
3. When it's time, fill the tumbler with ice. Pour in the amer and the teaspoon of syrup and stir a few times so they're cold.
4. Add the cold soda and give it one gentle lift from the bottom with a spoon, so the syrup doesn't stay at the bottom.
5. Hold a spoon upside down just above the drink and pour the cognac slowly over the back of it, so it spreads across the top instead of sinking.
6. Twist the orange peel over the glass so its oils fall on the drink, then drop it in.

**closingLine:** *Pour the brandy last, over a spoon. How it lands was never all up to you.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*Bitter orange and sweet orange, side by side. Neither one is hiding the other.*

**whoYouAre**
You can tell when someone is going to be all right, usually a good while before they can. A friend swears they'll never get over it, and you think: give it till spring. You don't say so, because it would sound like you weren't listening. You just keep turning up with something easy to say yes to: a walk, an errand, a Sunday that needs a second pair of hands. And one day they notice they've been getting better for a while, somewhere in all those Sundays.

You know that help can taste bitter. Being looked after can feel like being told you're not coping, so you make yours easy to take. But the few who didn't get better stay with you. You go back over them, wondering what you missed, as if their mending were yours to finish. That's the price of believing in people before they do, and you've never once thought it wasn't worth paying.

**yours**

1. In the 1830s, a young man from Marseille, still in his twenties, caught a fever in Algeria. He was treated the usual way then, with wine steeped with quinine and other bitters. He knew what that tasted like.
2. The way it's told, that fever is what set Gaëtan Picon trying, from 1837, to make a tonic that tasted better. By 1840 he had a recipe: bitter orange peel and sweet orange peel together, with bitter roots and a bark. He opened a small distillery, and his amer, a bitter orange liqueur, was soon drunk in quantity across Algeria. Later it reached France.
3. I like to think he never forgot being the one handed the glass nobody wanted to drink, and that's why he didn't take the bitter out, only put something sweet beside it, so people would come back for it on their own. I can't tell you whether it made anyone better. I can tell you people kept choosing it: by 1909 it took about three million oranges a year. That's how your help works too. It stays honest, and it still gets taken.
4. So I made yours from his idea, not his bottle, and long, the way people came to drink his amer in the Basque communities of the American West: with soda, a little grenadine and brandy on top. The amer in yours is a modern one, made with the old Amer Picon in mind, and the rest of the glass is built around it. The grenadine is my own pomegranate syrup, with a sweet orange's peel rubbed into the sugar, so a bitter orange and a sweet one sit side by side, the way they did in 1840. Then soda, and the cognac poured last over a spoon so it rests on top. The first sip is the gentle one. The bitter comes right after, and nothing covers it.
5. The ones who didn't get better: put them down. Their mending was never yours to finish. You were the sweet beside the bitter, the reason the hard part got taken at all, and that was always the job. Next time you catch yourself going back over one of them, remember the Sundays instead.
