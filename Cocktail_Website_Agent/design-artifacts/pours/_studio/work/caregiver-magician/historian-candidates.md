# Hester's mirror hunt: The Healer (caregiver-magician), round 1

Persona: restore and repair; goal service, fear selfishness (Caregiver); transformation, fear unintended results / dullness (Magician). E.T.: heals with a touch.

## A. Gaëtan Picon (person + ingredient: Amer Picon) — my pick

- **Story (three sentences).** A young soldier in Algeria caught a tropical fever in the 1830s and was treated, as was usual, with wine steeped with quinine and other bitters. The experience set him, from 1837, to working on a remedy that tasted better and worked better; by 1840 he had a formula (bitter and sweet orange peel, gentian, quinquina, rhubarb), left the army and opened a small distillery. Soldiers carried it home to France; by 1909 it took some three million oranges a year.
- **Mirror.** The healer was the patient first: he knew exactly how the cure tasted, and made it kinder. Magician's turn: bitter medicine transformed into something people chose.
- **Sources (primary).** Oxford Companion, PICON OR AMER PICON, pdf 1509–1510 (all facts above; ABV history 40 → 38 → 27 → 18%, 1989; Diageo; modern bartenders recreate it). Codex p. 87 (modern Amer Picon "less bitter and flavorful"; Bigallet China-China Amer "crafted with the classic Amer Picon recipe in mind"); Codex p. 260 (China-China, CioCiaro as substitutes). Brooklyn: Oxford BROOKLYN COCKTAIL pdf 360; Imbibe pdf 212.
- **Risks.** (1) "This experience prompted him" is Oxford's account of cause, not his words on record: the reading must say "the way it's told" or keep it as sequence (fever, then the formula). No "he wanted to spare others" as fact. (2) Colonial frame: he was in the army that "executed the French colonization of Algeria" (Oxford's words). Told plainly or left out; never romanticised. (3) He sold it: service and trade together, don't claim he gave it away. (4) Bottle: today's Amer Picon is 18% and hard to find; Tomás would use China-China (Codex p. 87) and say so; never "his recipe".

## B. The Penicillin (Sam Ross, 2005, Milk & Honey) — runner-up

- **Story.** Ross riffed on the Gold Rush with honey and ginger, floated smoky Islay on top, "went to bed that night not thinking much of it". Nine months later a waitress, Lucinda Sterling, came back from a table and told him every table needs to experience one. By 2014 young bartenders assumed it was an old classic.
- **Mirror.** The care you give without noticing; someone else sees it first. Honey, ginger, lemon: comfort.
- **Sources (primary, quotes on record).** *A Proper Drink* pp. 170–171; Oxford MILK & HONEY pdf 1304; CLASSIC COCKTAIL pdf 473.
- **Risks.** No source says why it's called Penicillin: never explain the name as fact. Famous: less discovery.

## Held back

- **Quinine / tonic water.** Bitter bark, used in Bolivia and Peru "since before recorded history" (LI p. 364); fluoresces under UV (LI p. 364: a Magician image, "glowing crystals"). **Conflict:** Oxford TONIC WATER (pdf 2043) credits Schweppes' 1858 patent; Oxford GIN AND TONIC (pdf 898) credits Erasmus Bond, 28 May 1858. Inventor stays out. LI also: it served Europeans "looking to subjugate malaria-ridden parts of the world". The Countess of Chinchón cure is a legend (not in the library; don't tell it).

## Round 2: re-ranked after Wren's card

**Pick is now B, through Lucinda Sterling** (the person), because Wren's line is "you believe someone will be all right before they do". On the record (*A Proper Drink* p. 170, Ross recalling to Simonson): Ross "went to bed that night not thinking much of it"; "nine months later, at Little Branch, one of our waitresses, Lucinda Sterling" came back from a table "and they all wanted Bartender's Choices. She said I should put a Penicillin out because every table needs to experience one of them. It wasn't until she said that, that I was like, Oh, maybe there's something there." Later: bartender, opened Middle Branch with Petraske (*Proper* p. 277; Oxford MILK & HONEY pdf 1303, PERNOD-RICARD pdf 1500 = 2012). Spread: LA consultancy 2007 (p. 170); "as close to a household word as any cocktail since the Cosmopolitan" (p. 171); by 2014 assumed an old classic (p. 171). Codex p. 282 has a spec (Famous Grouse, lemon, honey syrup; ginger per Codex).

Fences: (1) her words reach us only through Ross, as Simonson reports him: "the way Ross tells it". (2) She believed in a drink, not a person: the mirror is her shape, never "she healed" anything. (3) Nothing sourced says why it's called Penicillin, and Wren's no-health-claims rule means the name is never played as a cure. (4) No motive for her beyond what Ross reports she said. (5) "Nine months" is Ross's figure, not a date on record.

Picon drops to runner-up: it's the "it was once medicine" trap Wren named, plus the colonial frame.
