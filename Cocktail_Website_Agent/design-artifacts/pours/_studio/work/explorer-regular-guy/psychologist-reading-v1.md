## whoYouAre

You're halfway through the best meal of the trip, down a street the guidebook didn't mention, and you're already working out who you'll tell. You'll come home with the name of the place, the street it's on, what to order and who to ask for. Friends are still using that list years later.

What you rarely say is that you like the guidebook. You like knowing the way back. Some people make a show of never booking anything, and around them you've learned to keep quiet about the return ticket. And there's something softer still: the best night of a trip is sometimes the one after it, at your own table, telling it. Not because you didn't love being there. Because for you, a find isn't quite finished until someone you love has heard about it. That's why the one trip you'd never book is the one with no way back. Stuck somewhere, even somewhere beautiful, you'd have to keep it all to yourself. So you bring it home, and the people who didn't go get to go a little, and get the directions too. That's what you give them: the world, handed round, and the nerve to try it.

## yours

1. Ron Cooper had been drinking mezcal for years, the everyday kind, and he'd always bring a bottle back for his friends. Then one New Year he took a group of friends to Oaxaca, in the south of Mexico, as he tells it, "to drink a little mezcal". At a checkpoint on the road, a policeman told him his uncle made mezcal up in the mountains and was coming down the next day: come back tomorrow and there'd be some. Cooper came back. It was, in his words, "mind-blowing", unlike anything he'd had.
2. He bought a couple of litres, took them home and poured them for his friends. "Everyone went crazy for it." When he went back for more, the border let him bring in only a little, and he had to pour most of a big jug away, a jug from a village called Chichicapa. He did it "tearfully", he says. He wanted "to share with friends, but I wasn't allowed". So he got the licences to bring it in properly, and he gave each mezcal the name of the village where it was made.
3. He likes to quote a saying from Mexico: you don't find mezcal, mezcal finds you. The villages had it all along. What he did is what you do with every good find: he didn't keep it, and he didn't pass it off as his. He brought it home and put the village's name on the bottle, and anyone who loved it knew exactly who to thank. And I like to think what hurt at the border wasn't the mezcal going down the drain. It was the friends at home who'd never taste it. You'd feel it the same way.
4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Paloma, the long, bright drink people say Mexico orders instead of the Margarita, made here with mezcal instead of tequila, so it's smoky as well as sharp. The mezcal is from Chichicapa, the village on that jug he had to pour away, and now a bottle anyone can buy far from Oaxaca. Then fresh grapefruit, a little lime, and sparkling water, built tall over ice. The sweetness is my change. Bars that make their own grapefruit soda often use sugar. I use honey, and I'd ask for a honey from near where you live, a jar from one place rather than a blend. That's for you. Home is half the trip for you, so it goes in the glass with the same respect as the village. Two places, both named, and neither one the lesser. Any runny honey will do. But if you've never tried the one sold at the next market over, this is your excuse.
5. You don't need a ticket to go one street further. Away, you give a place everything: the market, the side street, the tip from someone who lives there. At home, you take the same road to the same shop. Try the street after it. Ask the person behind the counter where they'd eat. I'd bet there's a find twenty minutes from your door that nobody you know has heard of yet, and you're the one who'll tell them. That's a whole trip, and you'll be home in time to tell it.
