## whoYouAre

You're halfway through the best meal of the trip, down a street the guidebook didn't mention, and you're already working out who you'll tell. You'll come home with the name of the place, what to order and who to ask for.

Truth is, you like the guidebook. Around people who brag about never booking anything, you leave the return ticket out. Softer still: the best night of a trip is sometimes the one after it, at your own table, telling it. Not because you didn't love being there. Because a find isn't finished until someone you love has heard about it. So the one trip you'd never book is the one with no way back: stuck somewhere, even somewhere beautiful, with no one to tell. You bring it home instead, and everyone who stayed gets to go a little. That's what you give them: the world, handed round, with directions.

## yours

1. Ron Cooper had been drinking mezcal for years, the everyday kind, and he'd always bring a bottle back for his friends. Then one New Year he led some friends on a trip through Mexico. At a checkpoint in Oaxaca, in the south, he told a policeman they'd come "to drink a little mezcal". The policeman said his uncle made mezcal up in the mountains. Cooper asked if he could get some. The uncle was coming down the next day: come back tomorrow, and there'd be some. Cooper came back. It was, in his words, "mind-blowing", unlike anything he'd had.
2. He bought a couple of litres, took them home and poured them for his friends. "Everyone went crazy for it." Coming home from a later trip, he was told at the border he could bring in only a little, and he had to pour most of a big jug away, a jug from a village called Chichicapa. He did it "tearfully". He'd wanted to bring it home to share with friends, he said later, "but I wasn't allowed". So he got the licences to bring it in properly, and he named the first mezcals after the villages where they were made.
3. He quotes a Mexican saying: you don't find mezcal, mezcal finds you. The villages had it all along. What he did is what you do with every good find: he didn't keep it, and he didn't pass it off as his. He brought it home and put the village's name on the bottle, and anyone who loved it knew where to send their thanks. And I like to think what hurt at the border wasn't the mezcal going down the drain. It was the friends at home who'd never taste it. You'd feel it the same way.
4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Paloma, the long, bright drink people say Mexico orders instead of the Margarita, made here with mezcal instead of tequila, so it's smoky as well as sharp. I'd pour Del Maguey's Chichicapa: the village on that jug he had to pour away, and one of the first two he named on a bottle. Fresh grapefruit and a little lime give it its edge, and it's built in a tall glass, then filled with ice and lengthened with sparkling water. The sweetening is my change. Many bartenders make their own grapefruit soda, with sugar. I use honey, and I'd ask for a honey from near where you live, a jar from one place rather than a blend. Most of the drink's sweetness is that honey, so the glass doesn't work without it. That's for you. Home is half the trip for you, so it goes in with the same respect as the village. Two places, both named, and neither one the lesser. Any runny honey will do.
5. You don't need a ticket to go one street further. Away, you give a place everything: the market, the side street, the tip from someone who lives there. At home, you take the same road to the same shop. Try the street after it. Ask the person behind the counter where they'd eat. I'd bet there's a find twenty minutes from your door that nobody you know has heard of yet, and you're the one who'll tell them. That's a whole trip, and you'll be home in time to tell it.

## Resonance

- **Line that lands:** "the best night of a trip is sometimes the one after it, at your own table, telling it." **Runner-up:** "That's what you give them: the world, handed round, with directions." **Tagline:** "Half the people you know have eaten somewhere because of you."
- **As the guest:** the opening scene (already working out who you'll tell, halfway through the meal) is recognition, not description. The hidden embarrassment about the guidebook is named gently, and the return ticket is given its dignity. The fear is theirs in their own word (stuck, with no one to tell). The gift is said out loud. The position ends on a gain: "you'll be home in time to tell it."
- **As a sceptic (horoscope check, whoYouAre):** one borderline sentence, "Not because you didn't love being there." It's held by the sentence before it. Everything else fits only a person who travels to share. **Swap test:** *Left Standing* (no café, no "take someone", no "That's your fear"); *For Good* (no "keep quiet", no "You'd know that feeling"); *Four Shares* / *Everybody's* (no bowl, no round, one glass); *As It Was* (no "One thing I'd ask"); *The Other Berry* (no "apologise"); *creator-outlaw* (no "the nerve to"); caregiver-creator and caregiver-outlaw (lint overlaps cleared in v3).
- **Rules:** nothing assumes the drink is in hand (y5 and the closing line point to a street near home). The romance is signposted (y3 "I like to think"). Motives are his own words or attributed ("he said later"). There's no year, number, "discovered", café, art, wife, emigration, plant, bats, bees or salt. History is about 40%.
- **Closing line (Tomás):** *Buy the honey on a street near home you've never walked down. It still counts.* It agrees with y5's position.
- **Verdict: ready.**
