## whoYouAre

Tell you no, and you'll make it anyway. Usually you'd already started. The best things you've made began without anyone's say-so, often on ground that wasn't yours, and they turned out better than what was allowed. A no has never stopped you. If anything, it shows you where to begin.

It's the yes you don't trust. When the people in charge finally want what you made, something in you goes cold, as if being let in were the first step to being owned. And underneath sits a question you've never quite asked anyone: is it good, or only good because it isn't allowed? What I see is this. Much of what everyone takes for granted now, someone like you made first, before anyone agreed it could be done. You show people what better looks like while they're still saying no, and it matters to far more people than ever say so.

## yours

1. By 1822, one of the most sought-after whiskies in Scotland was made illegally, in the glen of the Livet. When the king visited Edinburgh that year, one woman later wrote, his chamberlain was looking everywhere for it. Her father, about to be made a judge, had her empty her own store and send it to the palace. She remembered it as whisky that had waited years in the cask and then in the bottle, "mild as milk", with the real taste of contraband in it. As if being forbidden were part of the flavour.

2. Since at least 1815, one of the glen's illegal distillers had been George Smith, a tenant of the Duke of Gordon. The duke had done much to promote a new law, in 1823, that encouraged distillers like him to "go legal", and he urged Smith to take out a licence. Smith did. In 1824 his became the first licensed distillery in Speyside, and his old colleagues threatened him.

3. I like to think Smith faced the question you carry: was it ever good, or only good because it was forbidden? His answer is on the record. He went on making it, in the open. Within a few years other people were trading on his whisky's name, and within twenty, London was being sold it as "the Real Glenlivet". Being let in didn't make it disappear. It made it known.

4. The cocktail I've made for you starts from the Old-Fashioned, a renowned classic. The whisky is The Glenlivet 12 Year Old, from the distillery he licensed, which Oxford's drinks companion calls "subtle, even delicate". So instead of sugar, it gets [ML] of elderflower liqueur. A well-known cocktail book calls this whisky "elegant and floral", and the elderflower only turns that flower up. Nothing smoky, nothing raw: none of it tries to taste forbidden, as a nod to a whisky that never needed to be.

5. So, one proposal. Make something that isn't against anything: no rule in its way, nobody to prove wrong. Then show it to the people whose approval you've never wanted. If they want it, that isn't the moment you lose. It's the moment you find out it was good. And you'll still be the one who made it.
