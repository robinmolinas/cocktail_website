## whoYouAre

Tell you no, and you'll make it anyway. Usually you'd already started. The best things you've made began without anyone's say-so, often on ground that wasn't yours, and they turned out better than what was allowed. A no has never stopped you. If anything, it shows you where to begin.

It's the yes you don't trust. When the people in charge finally want what you made, something in you goes cold, as if being let in were the first step to being owned. And underneath sits a question you've never quite asked anyone: is it good, or only good because it isn't allowed? What I see is this. Much of what everyone takes for granted now, someone like you made first, before anyone agreed it could be done. You show people what better looks like while they're still saying no, and it matters to far more people than ever say so.

## yours

1. By 1822, the whisky made illegally in Glenlivet already had a name. When the king visited Edinburgh that year, a woman whose family sent some wrote later that his chamberlain was looking everywhere for it. Her father sent word to her at home to empty her own store, and it went to the palace. She remembered it as whisky that had waited a long time in the cask and then in the bottle, "mild as milk", with the real taste of contraband in it. As if being forbidden were part of the flavour.

2. Since at least 1815, one of Glenlivet's illegal distillers had been George Smith, a tenant of the Duke of Gordon. The duke had done much to promote a new law, in 1823, that encouraged distillers like him to "go legal", and he urged Smith to take out a licence. Smith did. In 1824 his became the first licensed distillery in Speyside, and his old colleagues threatened him.

3. I like to think Smith faced the question you carry: was it ever good, or only good because it was forbidden? What's on the record is what he did: he went on making it, in the open. Being let in didn't make it disappear. It made it the one others copied: within a few years, people were trading on its name, and within twenty, London was being sold his as "the Real Glenlivet".

4. The cocktail I've made for you starts from the Old-Fashioned, a renowned classic. The whisky is The Glenlivet 12 Year Old, from the distillery he licensed, which Oxford's drinks companion calls "subtle, even delicate". It's stirred cold over a single big cube with a teaspoon of sugar syrup and orange bitters, then finished with the oil from a lemon peel. The one addition is a tablespoon of elderflower liqueur, to bring up the flowers a well-known cocktail book already tastes in this whisky: "elegant and floral", it says. Nothing smoky, nothing raw: none of it tries to taste forbidden, as a nod to a whisky that never needed to be.

5. So, one proposal. Make something that isn't against anything: no rule in its way, nobody to prove wrong. Then show it to the people whose approval you've never wanted. If they want it, that isn't the moment you lose. It's the moment you find out it was good. And you'll still be the one who made it.
