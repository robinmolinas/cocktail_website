📜 **Hester:** Wren, I accept George Smith too. On your point 4 the answer is no: no page says he kept making it the same way. The nearest is Oxford SCOTLAND AND IRELAND pdf 1769, where the country distillers "who did things the small and old-fashioned way" won out, "Glenlivet, for instance". But that sentence is about country distillers in general, and what it says of Glenlivet is where it stands today. What the pages do give is demand during his lifetime. In the 1820s his Glenlivet had already attracted "free riders", people trading on the name (SPIRITS MEASURE pdf 1879). He opened a second distillery in 1840. By 1844 Usher's were selling "the Real Glenlivet Whisky" in London, the first scotch promoted there (GLENLIVET pdf 917). So your line stands, and it can carry dates: "he went on making it, in the open, and within twenty years London was being sold it as the real one."

A find for your point 2. Oxford's "reportedly" comes from Elizabeth Grant's *Memoirs of a Highland Lady* (1898, p. 373, I read the page on archive.org). The king's Chamberlain was "looking everywhere for pure Glenlivet whisky", and it couldn't be had outside the Highlands. Her father was about to be made a judge, and he had her empty her own store of it, whisky with "the true contraband goût in it". That's your hidden doubt, written down by someone who drank it: part of the charm was that it was forbidden. Two limits on it. Neither Grant nor Oxford names Smith here, because this is the glen's illicit whisky. So no line may say the king drank *his* whisky. Second, after 1823 the country distillers moved to all-malt whisky, and before that theirs often had oats and rye in it (WHISKY pdf 2132). So the illicit spirit is never called a single malt.

Three more fences. The page says his old colleagues threatened him, and has no word like "sell-out", so don't put one in their mouths. Usher's 1853 vatting is his whisky mixed with others, which reads as absorbed, so it stays out. Tomás, *Codex* p. 204 calls the 12 "massively produced" and "not terribly complex". Neither phrase goes in guest text. Use Oxford's "subtle, even delicate" (pdf 918).
