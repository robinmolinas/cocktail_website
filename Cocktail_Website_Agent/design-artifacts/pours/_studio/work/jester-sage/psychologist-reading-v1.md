## whoYouAre

The letter says the changes are "for your convenience". You have a line about it before anyone's finished reading it, and the table groans, then nods, because it's true. You spot what doesn't add up, and you've found the funny side before anyone else has noticed. People assume you expect the worst of everything.

Here's what they miss. You only notice the gap because you still know exactly how it should be. Under every joke is a picture of something better, and you've never quite given it up. You keep that part quiet. A joke is hard to laugh at; a hope is easy. So you'd sooner be caught being kind than caught believing in something, and the kind things happen when nobody's looking. Bad news you can take. The pretending wears you out: a dinner where everyone agrees to call it fine, and it goes on and on. And somewhere further down sits a fear you rarely say: that one day it won't be funny any more.

And this matters more than people know. When something's absurd, you make it possible to laugh at it without giving up on it. After your line, everyone breathes out.

## yours

1. In 1906, after the earthquake and fire in San Francisco, several clergymen said the destruction was divine retribution for the city's sins. A city that had just lost so much was told it had deserved it. One of the few buildings still standing was a whiskey warehouse, A. P. Hotaling's. People had saved it: its own workers among them, with the fire department and the Navy.

2. A journalist and poet called Charles K. Field is credited with four lines about it:
*If, as they say, God spanked the town*
*For being over frisky,*
*Why did He burn the churches down*
*And save Hotaling's whiskey?*
I love that it never argues. It asks one question and lets the whiskey answer. It isn't laughing at anyone's faith. It's laughing at the idea that a city in that state had it coming. People were still quoting it in 1962, and again in 2024.

3. Then his story turns. Twenty-one years later, from 1927 to 1940, Field was on NBC radio with a programme of poetry and inspirational talks, under a made-up name: Cheerio. No press photographs were allowed, and his fee went to charity. The joke is remembered as his. The hope went out under another name. I like to think he wanted it that way.

4. I think you'd understand him. The jibe and the hope have one root: caring how it turns out. Making fun of the claim stood up for the city. The poetry was the same care, without the joke. Usually only one of the two gets seen.

5. The cocktail I've made for you follows a classic Martini's shape: stirred over ice till the mixing glass frosts, then poured, with no ice, into a chilled stemmed glass. Two-thirds of it is bourbon, because the whiskey is the punchline. Dry vermouth and Angostura, just two dashes, keep it lean. Its lemon is just the oil from the peel: the peel itself comes out. Under all that dryness sit one and a half teaspoons of syrup made from a ripe pear, left overnight in sugar. Nothing on the glass gives it away, but it's there if you pay attention, as a nod to the hope Field kept out of sight. It's a sharp, clean drink with something soft underneath.

6. Keep the jokes. Plenty of people get through the week because of them. But once, say the hope out loud, as yourself, before a joke can get there first. Tell someone how you think things could be. It might get laughed at. I'd bet it gets listened to, and I'd love you to find out.
