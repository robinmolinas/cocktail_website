## whoYouAre

Show you almost anything and you see what it could be: the chair someone put out on the pavement, a friend's flat, the haircut they've had since school. Once it's in your hands, you'll change nearly everything, except the one thing that makes it theirs. You find that part first, and build the rest around it. So the words you're hoping for at the end aren't "I'd never have recognised it." They're "That's so you."

What nobody sees is the middle. There's always a point where it looks worse than when you started: the room stripped to the walls, the paint half on, a friend at the mirror not sure what you've done. You're the only one who isn't worried. You've been through enough middles to know the mess is part of how it works. What worries you comes later, once you've gone: that it all slides back, or that it turns out in some way you didn't mean. What stays is how you looked at them: as if the best of them was worth moving everything else for. Most people have never been looked at like that.

## yours

1. In 2012, Dave Arnold made a batch of alcoholic Arnold Palmers, iced tea and lemonade with a spirit in it, for a film crew. Everyone agreed they weren't very good. Afterwards he tried something that sounds like a way to make it worse: he poured his tea-flavoured vodka into milk. The milk curdled, which looks exactly like a mistake. But the curds do the work. They catch the harsh, drying edge of the tea and carry it off with them, and what comes through is smooth. The tea flavour, he wrote, was still very strong. It went on his bar's menu. It had also come out silky, which he hadn't planned at all. He called that an "unintended side benefit".

2. He wasn't the first to curdle a drink on purpose. Milk punch is on record from 1688. In a letter in 1763, Benjamin Franklin wrote a recipe down: boiling milk into the punch, then leave it to stand for two hours before straining it. The part where it looks ruined is right there in the recipe, written as a wait. And it lasted. Bottles of milk punch were still in Charles Dickens's cellar when he died.

3. Here's what I see in it, and it's only my reading. Nobody turned that tea into something else. The harsh part went, and what was left tasted more like itself. And the part that looked like failure was the part doing the work. You've known that for a long time. It's how you can stand in the middle of a mess and not flinch.

4. The one I'd make you starts with tamarind: a brown, sticky pulp, about the least glamorous thing in a kitchen. It's the only sour thing in the drink, and its sour is what breaks the milk. With it go aged rum, strong black tea and a little brown sugar. The night before, all of it is stirred into milk, left to curdle, and strained back through its own curds. What comes through is smooth, softer than what went in, and it still tastes of tamarind. It's poured cold, from the bottle. **[serve]** Whoever drinks it never sees the middle.

5. So here's where I'd push you. Next time something you've changed comes out differently from the picture in your head, don't fix it straight away. Give it a night. Look at it the way you'd look at that chair on the pavement. Arnold was only trying to get rid of a harsh edge, and he got silk. Some of your best work will be the part you didn't mean.

- **Visionary guard:** Down the Line opens "You see the finished thing first." This reading never says they see the end. They see what's *in* the thing and what to keep, and they're calm in the middle because they've been through middles, not because they know the end.
- **History share:** y1–y2 story, y3 meaning, y4 drink, y5 position. About two-fifths history. Fine.
- **Doubt:** whoYouAre p1's scenes (flat, haircut) lean towards the domestic. The persona's own examples are beauty, fashion and design, so I think they hold. The friend at the mirror is the risk: the guest must read it as care, not as "you make people over". "Not sure what you've done" is meant fondly.
