## Title block

- **tagline (pick):** You love every minute. Even the last one, if you let it.
  - alt: A good night gets better just because you're in it, loving it.
- **epigraph:** The Bloody Mary once had a politer name, and no ice at all.
- **closingLine (Tomás's):** There's no ice to keep it going, so it ends at its height. End the night the same way: raise a glass to it, and pick a date before the coats come out.

## whoYouAre

You're the one who says yes first. Yes to the cold sea in May, to the dish nobody else at the table dares to order (and then everyone wants a forkful), to walking on somewhere after dinner because the night's too good to end there. You enjoy things properly, out loud, and it's catching. With you around, people stop feeling guilty about the long lunch on a weekday, or the second pudding.

It's never the bill you mind. It's the lights coming up. You can feel the end of a good night coming long before anyone reaches for a coat, sometimes right in the middle of the best part, and that's partly why you enjoy things so hard. Nobody's there for the ride home, when everyone goes back to their own lives and the noise stops all at once. The fear under it is a plain one: that when the music stops, you'll be the one left in the quiet. Still, look at what you do for people: you make enjoying things feel safe, and they remember who did that for them.

## yours

1. Crosby Gaige was the son of the postmaster in a place called Skunk Hollow, New York, and he became one of Broadway's leading producers, in a career that took in some 150 plays. But his true passions lay outside the theatre: fine wine and cooking.

2. After the crash of 1929, he started making his living from those two loves too. He wrote a monthly column called Cellar and Pantry, and in 1937 NBC started broadcasting his daily radio programme, *Kitchen Cavalcade*. There was a printing press in the barn on his farm, and among the things it printed were dinner menus.

3. I like to think that after some 150 plays, he knew what every good show knows: it ends while people still want more, and that's why they come back. You'd have understood each other. The big party of the Twenties stopped in 1929, the moment you'd dread most, and what he did was lay a longer table, one that people he'd never meet could join every day on the radio. His last book is called *Dining with My Friends*. His own dinners come first, and then his friends' follow.

4. The cocktail I've made for you starts from a recipe in his cocktail guide of 1941, a witty book with recipes sent in by famous names, and one of the ways the Bloody Mary spread. Gaston Lauryssen of New York's St. Regis hotel sent him his hotel's version, under the politer name it went by there: the Red Snapper. It had as much vodka as tomato juice, with a little Worcestershire sauce and a pinch of salt, and it was served straight up, as a cocktail, not over ice. The equal parts and the glass without ice both stay. I took out the Worcestershire and put in a splash of manzanilla, a bone-dry Spanish sherry, so his cellar and his kitchen share the glass. With fresh lemon and a pinch of celery salt, it's savoury, bright and properly cold. And because it's served up, it's short, and it has a real ending. I wanted that for you.

5. So try this, one night soon. When it's at its very best, the table loud and nobody watching the time, be the one who ends it. Not because you're tired, and not to be sensible. Say it out loud: this was perfect. Then name the next one, and the date. You'll leave on the best note instead of the last one, and you'll find out what happens when the music stops. Nobody vanishes. They ask you when. And may the next one be even better.

## Resonance

**As the guest (a Pleasure Seeker).** The tagline makes me smile and then pauses me: "even the last one" is the minute I'm bad at. The epigraph, read cold: the Bloody Mary, of all drinks, once went by a politer name and came with no ice. I didn't know that, and I want the story. whoYouAre ¶1 is me without flattery: the cold sea, the dish nobody orders, the forkfuls. ¶2's opening line is the "how did they know": *It's never the bill you mind. It's the lights coming up.* Then the ride home, which I'd never mention. The ending gives me back something I'm proud of, making enjoying things feel safe. The story is fun (Skunk Hollow, a barn that printed dinner menus). y3 gives me the mirror: the big party stopped and he laid a longer table. The last book, full of his friends' dinners, answers my fear without saying so. The drink ends on purpose, and y5 turns that into something I could actually do on Friday. It asks for no restraint, just a better ending, and it ends on the next one. The closing line hands me the same thing as a gesture: a toast, a date, and the coats still on their hooks.
**The line that lands hardest:** *It's never the bill you mind. It's the lights coming up.*

**As the sceptic.** Barnum: "you don't like nights ending" is true of many people. This reading holds it with the courage-in-pleasure scene (says yes first, the dish nobody dares), the feeling that arrives in the middle of the best part, and the gift named narrowly (enjoying things feels safe). The hedges are kept: "some 150 plays" is the career, "turned those loves into his living" doesn't inflate the crash, the 1929 party is the era's, and each interpretive sentence is signposted or plainly metaphor. The weak spot is the posthumous book: "his last book" implies his death. That's deliberate, and it's gentle (no date, no cause, never that he saw it). It's the truest "the company lasted" on the record. And y4 stands between it and y5, so "be the one who ends it" reads as a night, never as anything darker. y5 names "one night" in its first sentence for that reason. Drinks-site check: no "one more", no morning after, no cure, no brunch. Walking on after dinner is a walk, not a bar crawl. The weekday treat is a long lunch, not a bottle.

**Swap tests.**
- *Hoping You'd Come* (the Geisha): the care happens before anyone arrives, and the position is to ask. Here it's the end of the night, and the position is to end it well.
- *Worth the Trip* (the Reassurer): "Let the quiet after it happen." Here nobody is asked to stay for the quiet. The guest ends the night on a high and names the next one.
- *Anyway* (the Morale Booster): the good night is made on purpose for others. Here the enjoyment is the guest's own, and others catch it.
- *Brought Home* (the Tourist): the find told afterwards at home. Here nothing is told; the whole reading is in the room.
- *Their Night* (the Fairy Godparent): "don't slip out early." Here the guest leaves openly, out loud, on the best note: the opposite gesture, and no "slip".
- *Left Standing* (the Conservationist): "drink it while it's cold". Not used: the drink's ending is its shortness, not a race with the warmth.
- *Off Duty* (the Rescuer), "Everyone else is still deciding. You've already gone.": I dropped "go first" from the gift for this reason.
- The Bad Influence (unauthored; soup for whoever stayed at closing): here nobody stays behind. The night is ended at its height.
- Four Shares' "You're allowed to…", and "apologise" (*The Other Berry*, *Still Yours*): avoided on purpose. The gift is "you make enjoying things feel safe".

**Closing line, tested as the guest's last words.** "Raise a glass to it" is a toast to the night, not another round: it marks the end instead of extending it, and the date is the next one named. I checked it against my trap ("one more") and it passes. "Before the coats come out" answers whoYouAre's coat, so the reading closes on its own first fear, turned.
