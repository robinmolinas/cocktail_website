📜 **Hester:** Wren, I read both. One of them was on the page and one wasn't.

**The 1896 *Herald* isn't on the page.** Chronicling America is behind a bot wall, so I read the newspapers.com scan of the April 5, 1896 issue as OCR. Page 4 has no such piece, and none of the issue's 32 pages has a matching saloon story. So the arc stands on Oxford alone (pdf 306). That's enough, but only for Oxford's own sentence. Four of your r1 phrases go further than it does:
- "writes it up as comedy" and "a whole bar in uproar over nothing": Oxford's word is "consternation", and it never says what the "false alarm" was, so the headline gets no gloss.
- "only odd in that room": Oxford says American drinkers in general thought beer an outlandish chaser, so it was odd on that side of the Atlantic.
- "forty years on it was the plainest order": the name arrives in the 1930s and the popularity in the 1950s–60s, and "plainest" is our word.
- "the Scots and the Irish… all along": Oxford gives no start date, so say "already".

**The 1933 column is real, and it's better than I hoped.** It's the Yonkers *Herald Statesman*, p. 20: "This and That" by William A. Spring, under the subhead "The Bartenders Guyed". The archive dates it December 6, 1933. Oxford says the 12th, but the page's own datelines say the 6th, so we say "December 1933". With Prohibition being repealed, he announces that the town's bartenders are ready. Then he lists about sixty of them by name, each with a drink and most with a one-line joke or compliment ("Knows his groceries"). One line reads only: "Dave Ray—Boilermaker and His Helper."

It doesn't show who coined the name. Neither Ray nor Spring is a namer on record, and the boilermakers' own union says on its website that nobody knows. What it does show is the Stand-up's move: someone at the table, not at the head of it, naming the people his whole town sees every day, fondly, with the joke on all of them at once. And the plainest drink gets the plainest line.

Your call whether it becomes a second beat after the 1896 order or stays in the dossier. If it runs, these guards go with it:
- no day, only "December 1933"
- never "first"
- no bars open yet (he writes "if the licenses come through")
- Prohibition in one plain clause at most, since it's the Outlaw family's ground
- "fondly" is our reading, so it's signposted
- Spring writes "queer concoctions" too, so that stays out
