## whoYouAre

You're the one who says it. Not the clever thing, the true one: that we all wave back at someone who was waving at the person behind us, that we all answer "you too" when the waiter says "enjoy your meal". There's half a second of quiet, then the whole table laughs, and somebody says, "I thought that was just me." That's the sound you're after. An evening where everyone stays polite and nobody admits to anything is your idea of a very long night.

You keep one part quiet. Every one of those starts with you. You're the one who waved back. You say "everybody does this" because it's kinder, and safer, than "I do". So you're always in the joke, and hardly ever what it's about. Somewhere under the laughing is a small worry that one day you'll say it and the table will go quiet, and it really will be just you. It hardly ever is. And here's what it does for people: they go home feeling a little less strange than when they came. That matters more than the laugh.

## yours

1. A whiskey with a beer beside it looks too simple to have a history. In America, though, it came late. Scots and Irish drinkers already followed their whisky with a beer, but Americans used iced water, and thought beer an odd thing to chase it with. Then, in 1896, a New York paper, the *Herald*, ran a story about a man who walked into a saloon and ordered both, and about the dismay of the bartender and the regulars. One of them said he'd heard of strange drinks in his time.

2. He was early, not odd. Habits changed, and by the 1930s the order had a name that stuck: "Boilermaker and His Helper", after a skilled metalworker and the extra muscle who worked beside him. Nobody knows who came up with it. But in December 1933, as his town's bars waited for their licences, a Yonkers columnist named William A. Spring wrote up about sixty local bartenders by name, each with a specialty and, for most, a one-line joke or a compliment. One line reads, in full: "Dave Ray—Boilermaker and His Helper." There's no joke on it at all, and I like to think Spring knew the plainest drink in town didn't need one.

3. I like to think of that man in 1896 as someone you'd get on with. He ordered what felt natural to him, in front of everybody, and the room took it for one man's strange habit. It wasn't. Whole countries already drank it that way, and within a few decades it was common enough to need a name. That's what you do at a table. You say the thing everybody does, and it stops being one person's oddity and becomes everyone's. Spring did it to a whole town in one column, with the joke on all of them at once.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is that order, done properly: a straight rye, which is what the name meant in 1948, and a cold German-style pilsner, a pairing that's back on good bar menus now. Nothing gets dropped in, and nothing gets mixed. My one change comes from the kitchen cupboard: a handful of dried apricots, steeped in the rye and strained out. Beer is one of the surprising partners for stone fruit, so every sip of the cold, bitter beer brings the apricot forward. It's in there because your best lines start in that kind of place: the ordinary stuff everyone has at home and never looks at twice.

5. So swap one word. When you catch yourself saying "everybody does this", say "I do this" instead. The laugh still comes, I promise, and this time you're inside it, not telling it. People enjoy you for the joke. They feel close to you for the confession. And you'll find what that order in 1896 turned out to show: it was never just one person's.

## Resonance

**As the guest (a Stand-up).** The tagline makes me smile: it's what I do, and it names what it's for. The epigraph stands cold: a plain order, a newspaper bothering with it, and I want to know why. whoYouAre ¶1 is a scene I've caused: the wave, the "you too", the half-second, "I thought that was just me". ¶2 is the part I struggle to show: it starts with me, "everybody" is my cover, and the worry under it. Then it names what I give, plainly. The line I'd quote: **"You say 'everybody does this' because it's kinder, and safer, than 'I do'."** The story: a man ordering what felt natural while the room stared, who turned out to be early, not odd, and a columnist who joked about a whole town at once. I laugh with all of them. The drink is the plainest order made worth having, with something from my own cupboard in it. y5 gives me something to do with one word, and what I gain (being in it).
