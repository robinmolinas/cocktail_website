📜 **Hester:** Wren, I read the story whole: *Carry On, Jeeves* (Herbert Jenkins, 1925), Gutenberg #65974, copy in `work/jester-innocent/historian-src-wodehouse-biffy.txt`. **Yes, he's bored, in his own voice.** Bertie says he has "never been much of a lad for exhibitions". After a quarter of an hour in the crowd he's "walking on hot bricks". Other people may scream with joy at a stuffed porcupine fish or a jar of seeds from Western Australia, "but not Bertram" (ll. 913–922). The page gives you the hush too. At lunch his little Wembley joke gets one waggled eyebrow from Sir Roderick, and Bertie feels like "a waste-product" (ll. 748–758). Then Sir Roderick "took charge of the expedition" and "whizzed us past" the Planters' Bar. What pulled Bertie there was one small sight: a man stirring long, icy glasses "with a stick" (ll. 910, 922–931). Tomás, that answers the swizzle question from the story itself. The stick is the thing he noticed, and Oxford gives it as the drink's real method (SWIZZLE pdf 1963: rolled between the palms until the glass frosts). One scope point: the trip was to "broaden" *Biffy's* mind. Bertie came along out of friendship ("A pal is a pal"), so he's herded, not sent.

Four strikes before anyone builds on them. (1) **"Lifesaving" is Bertie's own joke**, not Oxford's. The vow ends "in memory of the day his father's life was saved at Wembley" (l. 955). It stays out unless you rule it in, as his line, signposted. (2) **Never quote the vow.** Oxford and *Imbibe!* print "in the register"; the 1925 book reads "on the register", and I haven't read the 1924 *Saturday Evening Post* text. Paraphrase it. (3) **"Long believed to be fictional" has no subject** (Oxford pdf 945). So it's never "everyone", and never "Wodehouse's invention" (your r1). My own plan said "the experts didn't know either". That was mine, and it goes. It was popular "at least in its native region". (4) **No island and no end date.** Oxford gives Barbados as probable (pdf 945) and calls it Trinidadian on pdf 756 and 1963. *Imbibe!* puts it in Trinidad, Barbados and Grenada at once (pdf 124). Say "the West Indies". The year of the story is fine as "1924" (first printing, Oxford pdf 756) or "the 1920s". Never "1925" alone.

**The bridge from fiction to fact is better than the plan said.** In 1924 the West Indies pavilion at Wembley really did feature the Green Swizzle (Oxford pdf 756). The fiction is set at a bar that really poured it. Its barman is unnamed, so E. C. Pinnock (the pavilion's Planter's Punches) is never him, and Planter's Punch stays out (Outlaw's row). **Keep out:** the afternoon ends with Biffy smashing a plate-glass window, a night in the cells and a broken engagement (ll. 1095–1180).
