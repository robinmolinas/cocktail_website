## whoYouAre

You're somewhere you're meant to sit still: a hotel breakfast with its little rules, a waiting room, the long middle of a speech. Ten minutes, and something small has caught you. The sachet that won't tear. The toaster that sends the bread round twice. You give it all your attention, because right then it's the only problem in the world. It all goes sideways, and nobody there forgets how. You look up, and that's when you find the room.

You never set out to be funny. The part you laugh off is the hush: the "not now", the hand on your arm. Being told to sit still has always sounded like being told to be less, and what you dread is managing it, and nothing happening at all. Hushing you would cost everyone else their best stories, the dull mornings they still tell, fondly, years later. And after you, nobody needs to pretend the rules made sense.

## yours

1. In 1924, at Wembley, the West Indies pavilion of the British Empire Exhibition poured the Green Swizzle. P. G. Wodehouse put Bertie Wooster there that year, in a story, tagging along with a friend who was being taken round to have his mind broadened. At lunch, every joke Bertie started had been stopped by a stare, and the one he finished got a waggled eyebrow. Now it's a stuffed fish and a jar of seeds, and exhibitions, he admits, have never been his thing.

2. Then, rushed past a bar, he spots a man stirring long, icy glasses with a stick, and the urge to see more of him won't go away. He and his friend slip away to it, and the moment the barman tells him what they're called, Bertie vows that any son of his will be named Green Swizzle Wooster.

3. For years, the Green Swizzle itself was taken for fiction. It wasn't: old newspapers show it was real, and for a while quite popular in the Caribbean.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Green Swizzle, a Caribbean classic, rebuilt from the parts listed for an old Trinidad bottled base, discontinued decades ago. Mine are white rum, fresh lime and sugar. The green comes from a barspoon of green absinthe, a spirit made with wormwood, that I steep with tangerine peel for two days. The tangerine is a travel writer's tip for the old wormwood bitters, passed on by a drinks historian; I borrowed it for the absinthe, and I put it in for you: the bright thing that turns up in the middle of something grim. Then the stick does the stirring. You give it your whole attention for about half a minute, and the glass tells you when to stop. It comes out tall, pale and very cold, with four dashes of Angostura on top, and it gets gentler as you go.

5. I like to think you'd have seen the man with the stick before anyone else on the tour did, and stopped. Next time you're being steered past the one thing you'd actually like to see, look round for the other person who's had about enough. There usually is one. Go together. Give it all your attention, and if something wonderful comes of it, make a promise far too big for it. Somebody will be telling that afternoon for years, and it'll all be true.
