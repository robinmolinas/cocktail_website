## whoYouAre

When someone says "actually, nobody's really sure", most people sigh. You light up. You ask why: why the form needs that box, why a thing is called what it's called, why everyone does it this way. You're not making a point. You'd honestly like to know, and you're secretly hoping the answer will be stranger than you expected. It usually is.

Not everyone hears it that way. Over the years a few of your questions got you told off: "because I said so", the sigh, the look across the table. So now you ask lightly, with a smile, as if it hardly matters. It matters. The worst answer, to you, is a label: "oh, it's just a…", handed over to close the subject, so that nobody looks again. And yet around you, people do look again. With you, "I don't know" stops being embarrassing and becomes the start of the fun, and people need that more than they'll ever tell you.

## yours

1. In 1872, a pharmacist in Cognac named Antonin Baudoin noticed a black growth on the walls and roof tiles near the distilleries. It looked like soot. He didn't leave it at that: he took it to two botanists, and a few years later he printed his own short study of it. His answer was that it was a kind of algae. He was wrong.

2. Others filed it under a name that had already been given to more than three hundred different things, nearly all of which have been moved out since. Then for decades the science went quiet, and the studies that did look kept calling it the ordinary sort of fungus found everywhere. It wasn't. More than a century after Baudoin's first look, a team of scientists showed it was something no one had described, gave it a family name of its own, and named it after him, as the man who first brought it to them. Its nearest known relatives live on rock in Antarctica. It feeds on the spirit that escapes ageing barrels, yet it's millions of years older than any barrel, so it must live on something else too. Nobody is quite sure what.

3. I like to think Baudoin felt what you feel the moment someone says "it's just…": that it wasn't, quite. He looked properly, and he guessed wrong in print. Then the people who finally got it right put his name on it anyway. That's what your questions do. Even the ones that land wrong get other people looking, and what they find is often stranger than anyone guessed.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is stirred over ice until it's icy, then poured without it, the way a Martini is, from two bottles of Cognac's own. One is a cognac aged in oak. The other is Pineau des Charentes, the region's aperitif: grape juice stopped from becoming wine by young cognac from the same farm, then rested in oak too. Into part of the cognac I've steeped a few Sichuan peppercorns. You'll feel them as a faint tingle on your lips before the warmth comes through, a small question in the glass, the kind you've never been able to leave alone. I've poured it into a tulip-shaped glass, narrow at the top, so the scent stays with you instead of escaping the way it does from the barrels.

5. So when a question next comes to you, don't make it small. Ask it properly, and say your guess out loud, even if you suspect it's wrong. Being wrong first didn't cost Baudoin anything. It got the whole thing looked at. And I'd bet the real answer turns out stranger than your guess. That's the part you'll enjoy most.
