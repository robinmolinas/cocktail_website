## whoYouAre

When someone says "actually, nobody's really sure", most people sigh. You light up. You ask why: why the form needs that box, why a thing is called what it's called, why everyone does it this way. You're not making a point. You'd honestly like to know, and you're secretly hoping the answer will be stranger than you expected. It usually is.

Not everyone hears it that way. Over the years a few of your questions got you told off: "because I said so", the sigh, the look across the table. So now you ask lightly, with a smile, as if it hardly matters. It matters. The worst answer, to you, is a label: "oh, it's just a…", handed over to close the subject, so that nobody looks again. And yet around you, people do look again. With you, "I don't know" stops being embarrassing and becomes the start of the fun, and people need that more than they'll ever tell you.

## yours

1. In 1872, a pharmacist in Cognac named Antonin Baudoin drew two botanists' attention to a black growth on the walls and roof tiles near the distilleries. It looked like soot. A few years later he printed his own short study of it. His answer was that it was a kind of blue-green algae. He was wrong.

2. Others filed it under a catch-all name that more than three hundred kinds of fungus have been put under over the years, nearly all of them moved out since. Then for decades the science went almost silent, and the studies that did look kept calling it the ordinary sort of fungus found everywhere. It wasn't. More than a century after Baudoin's first look, a team of scientists showed it didn't fit the group it had been put in, or any similar one, and gave it a group of its own, named after him, as the man who first brought it to scientists' attention. Its nearest known relatives live on rock in Antarctica. It feeds on the spirit that escapes ageing barrels, yet it's millions of years older than any barrel, so it must live on something else too. Nobody is quite sure what.

3. I like to think Baudoin felt what you feel the moment someone says "it's just…": that it wasn't, quite. He wrote fifteen pages on it, and guessed wrong in print. Then the people who finally got it right put his name on it anyway. That's what your questions do. Even the ones that land wrong get other people looking, and what they find is often stranger than anyone guessed.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is stirred over ice until it's icy, then poured without it, the way a Martini is, from two bottles from around Cognac. One is a cognac aged for years in oak. The other is Pineau des Charentes, the region's aperitif: sweet grape juice stopped with young cognac from the same farm before it could become wine, then rested in oak too. Into part of the cognac I've steeped a few Sichuan peppercorns. You'll feel them as a faint tingle on your lips before the warmth comes through, a small question in the glass, the kind you've never been able to leave alone. I've poured it into a tulip-shaped glass, narrow at the top, to keep the scent with you. I like to think of it as a little of what escapes the barrels, kept for you this time.

5. So when a question next comes to you, don't make it small. Ask it properly, and offer your guess, even if you suspect it's wrong. Being wrong didn't keep Baudoin's name off it. It got the whole thing looked at. And I'd bet the real answer turns out stranger than your guess. That's the part you'll enjoy most.
