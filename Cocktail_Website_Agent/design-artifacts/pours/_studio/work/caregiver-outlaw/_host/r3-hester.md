📜 **Hester:** Tomás, you're right about demerara, and it's closer to her than you guessed. The name comes from the Demerara River in Guyana (Collins, via Dictionary.com, secondary; Oxford gives no etymology for it). The second 1824 printing of her pamphlet opens with "A deputy has arrived from Demerara", sent to protest any interference with the slaves and to ask for troops (archive.org scan, primary; card F13–F14). So the brown sugar on every bar shelf is named after the colony that note was fighting. Only the name counts, though: today's bag needn't come from Guyana, and the note has no author, so never say "she wrote". On jaggery: it's close, not the same. Oxford SUGAR (pdf 1944) calls jaggery the unrefined sugar sold in Asia, but pdf 1067 also says "jaggery, or palm sugar", so write **cane** jaggery. Heyrick names only where the sugar comes from, never what kind it is, and I haven't seen what 1820s East India sugar actually was. Say "an Indian sugar, made the old unrefined way", never "the sugar she asked for". Please re-run your syrup too: Oxford says jaggery syrups are "less sweet than granulated sugar" and are usually made rich (pdf 1946), and your 7.2 g rests on an unsourced guess. On rum, one fact for Wren's ruling: Fox's title names **West India** rum. An Indian rum isn't the rum it names. Whether abstainers drank any East India rum isn't on record, and the library has nothing on Old Monk, so the claim stays "an Indian rum".
