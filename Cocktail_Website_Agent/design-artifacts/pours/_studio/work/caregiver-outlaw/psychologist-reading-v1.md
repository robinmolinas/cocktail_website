# Reading v1: The Campaigner (caregiver-outlaw), Wren, round 4
Built on anchors draft (card F1–F18) and Tomás's spec v2 (Tom Collins, jaggery syrup the one swap).

- **name:** Not Too Polite
- **tagline:** Someone had to say it. It's usually you.
- **epigraph:** Everything in this glass is ordinary except the sugar. The sugar is the argument.

**whoYouAre**
When something is unfair to someone who isn't in the room, yours is the voice that goes first. At the meeting, at the school gate, at the family table. People think it comes easily to you. It doesn't. Your heart is going before you open your mouth, you rehearsed it on the way there, and afterwards, when somebody says they wish they could do that, your hands are still shaking.

You'd do it again tomorrow. Keeping quiet would be the comfortable thing, and you can't be comfortable while someone is being forgotten. Somebody quieter in that room was relieved it was you. And when it's over, you don't stop to see what changed. You're already looking at the next thing that isn't fair.

**yours**
1. In 1824, a woman in Leicester called Elizabeth Heyrick wrote a short pamphlet against slavery in Britain's colonies. Almost everyone in the movement agreed it should end slowly, step by step. She wrote that disagreeing with them might seem "a most presumptuous, as well as hopeless attempt". Then she spent the rest of the pamphlet disagreeing, and told her own side they'd shown "a great deal too much politeness".
2. What she asked of ordinary households was small. Not to give up sugar, only to change where it came from. She said the sacrifice was so slight it seemed "almost burlesque" to call it one. She asked what "a few individuals, or a few families" could possibly do, and answered: "It can do wonders." She died in 1831. Parliament passed the Act abolishing slavery in the British colonies two years later.
3. I like to think her heart was going when she wrote that first page, and that she wrote the rest of it all the same. You'd know the feeling. And I think you'd know the other part too: she never saw the end. Your wins mostly happen after you've moved on, in rooms you're no longer in.
4. So yours is an everyday drink, the kind anyone can make: gin, lemon, sugar and soda water in a tall glass over ice. I swapped the sugar. This one is jaggery, an Indian cane sugar made the old unrefined way. It's close to what she meant, not the same. But you can taste the difference: it's rounder and a little toasted. Nothing was given up. It just came from somewhere else.
5. Before the next thing that isn't fair, do what you never do, and count. The times you spoke first. The people who didn't have to be brave on their own. The things that are different now because you wouldn't be polite about them. She didn't live to see the end of hers. You're still here to count yours.

## Resonance
**As the guest (a Campaigner).** Tagline: stops me, a little funny, and it's what I say to myself afterwards. whoYouAre ¶1: "you rehearsed it on the way there… your hands are still shaking" is the thing nobody who calls me brave has ever guessed. ¶2 gives me the gift (someone quieter was relieved) without making me a hero, then catches me: I never look back. The story lets me see myself in someone who called her own side too polite, and who never saw the end. y5 asks me for the one thing I'd resist, counting, and ties it to the name. This is the line: *"People think it comes easily to you. It doesn't."* Second: *"Somebody quieter in that room was relieved it was you."*

**As the sceptic.** Barnum: "yours is the voice that goes first" alone would fit the Rescuer or the Trickster, but the frightened heart, the fear of being too much, and never counting narrow it. Sibling swaps: the Rescuer (*Off Duty*) goes out alone and won't ask for help, which is action, not voice; the Doctor (*The Other Kindness*) says a hard no to one person, not a fuss on behalf of others; the Researcher (*Serviceable*) cares about strangers from a distance, and I've kept the distance out; the Nanny imposes structure. None fits "not too polite for people who aren't in the room". The Trickster fears being ignored and wants the attention; this guest hates the attention. History: y1–y2 plus y3's "she never saw the end" is about 150 of 330 words, under half. No "we", no guest pronouns, no archetype names, no drink in hand (y4 describes the recipe; y5 points to life). Rationed phrases unused. Signposted: y3.

**Verdict:** ready on v1, pending Hester's audit and the lint. Would change my mind: a Campaigner who reads "your hands are still shaking" as being called weak. I read it as recognition, because it sits next to "you'd do it again tomorrow".
