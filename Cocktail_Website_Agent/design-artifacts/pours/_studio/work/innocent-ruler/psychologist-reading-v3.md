# Reading v3: The Little Prince (innocent-ruler), Wren r4 step 3 (Hester's H1–H6 landed)

## whoYouAre

Someone asks what you've been up to, and you say "nothing much, just the usual". You mean the herbs on the windowsill, the friend you've rung every week since school, the old bike you keep running. To anyone else it looks small. It has never once struck you that small makes it matter less. Other people ask how big it is. You ask whether it's all right today.

You keep the small jobs small by doing them early: the drip seen to the day it starts, the hurt feelings sorted out before dinner. Nobody notices those. They aren't meant to be noticed. And you never mention the fear. Miss one day, and it feels as if all of it could slip. So you shrug and call it your little thing, because the seriousness gets smiled at. But look at what that care does for everyone near you. Whatever you're looking after is safe, and anyone close to you learns that a small thing taken seriously is worth more than a big thing taken lightly. With you, nothing small is ever only small.

## yours

1. Boadas opened in 1933, a few steps from the Ramblas, Barcelona's most crowded street. It was barely more than a corridor with a bar in it. After the civil war it gained a few metres and became a little triangle, and it has stayed that size ever since. Maria Dolors Boadas, the owner's daughter, grew up in it: she learned to walk there, and did her homework to the sound of the customers. By 1956 she was looking after the bar alongside her father, and from 1967 she ran it herself, for nearly fifty years.

2. Boadas threw its drinks: poured them from a tin full of ice into an empty tin and back, in a long stream, until they were cold and watered down just enough. The technique had come from Cuba, and elsewhere it had fallen out of use; for a time, it was said, Barcelona was the last place still doing it. The family, and the bartenders after them, kept it going, and paved the way for its return. A Barcelona bar connoisseur once said watching her was like a show. A writer who knew her put it more exactly: no spectacle, just a highly professional person. Her best teachers, she told him, were always the guests, and she read it in their faces after the second sip.

3. I like to think the throw was never about the stream, but about the second sip: the drink being right, for this person, tonight. That's how you measure things too. Not by size, or numbers, or who's noticed, but by whether it's all right today. And there's something in her story for the part of you that's scared. A gesture that had fallen out of use almost everywhere was kept alive in that small room, and as of 2021 it could be seen in many of the city's bars, and an apprentice who started there in 1970 owned the place. In the end, what she looked after grew sturdier than any one keeper, and all the days she gave it are part of why.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a dry gin Martini, the great classic, made with the throw Boadas kept going. Three tablespoons of gin and one of dry vermouth, measured with a spoon as a nod to a bar whose founder shunned the jigger, as its bartenders still did in 2021, then thrown between two tins four or five times, till it's cold, and poured through the strainer into a little wine glass, with a thin curl of lemon peel dropped in. Which drinks Boadas throws, I can't say, so this one is mine. I kept it to two bottles because, for you, the care should be the whole of it: nothing in it is there to impress. It's bright, cold and clean, and it tastes that way because someone paid attention.

5. So try this. You look after it so closely that you mostly look for what might be going wrong. Next time, before you check what needs doing, stop and look at it the way someone new would, as if you'd never seen it. It's been all right for years now. That isn't luck. It's every one of those days, and you're allowed to take pleasure in what they built.

**closing line:** Tomás's: *Four or five throws, then stop while it's cold. Make it for someone, and watch their face at the second sip.* It agrees with y5's position (look for what's going right) and borrows none of y5's words.

## Resonance

**As the guest.** The windowsill, the friend from school and the old bike are three small worlds, so most readers find theirs. "You've never once thought that made it matter less" is the first "how did they know". The this-is-me line: ***"Other people ask how big it is. You ask whether it's all right today."*** The second paragraph names the hidden part (the fear of one missed day, the shrug) and says the kindness out loud (Robin's rule). y3 turns Maria Dolors's second sip into the guest's own measure, then answers the fear with the record: what she kept outgrew any one keeper. y5 gives them something: stop scanning for trouble, and enjoy what the days built.
