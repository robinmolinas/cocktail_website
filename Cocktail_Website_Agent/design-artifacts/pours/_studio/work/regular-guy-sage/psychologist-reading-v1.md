## whoYouAre

It's the night before a friend starts a new job, and they're sure they'll get it all wrong. You don't give a speech. You tell them what an old neighbour used to say: learn everyone's name on the first day, and the rest can wait. It's exactly the right thing, and they write it down. Half of what you know came to you like that, with somebody's name on it, and you can still hear their voice when you say it.

Here's the bit you don't say: you didn't hold on to all of it. Some of what you were told turned out to be wrong, and you let those bits go quietly, because saying so out loud would feel like letting the person down. What's left, you've tried for yourself. You can't always say why it works, only who told you, and when someone asks for the reason, that's when you doubt yourself. The real fear is the day nobody asks at all. If it stops with you, a little of them stops too.

But each time you say "as my neighbour used to say", that neighbour is in the room again, and someone leaves with something they can use tomorrow. You do a kindness to two people at once, and that's rarer than you'd think.

## yours

Since the 1800s, households in rural Britain have made sloe gin: the small dark berries of the blackthorn, left in sweetened gin and opened at Christmas. Along with damson gin, it was simply something you did at harvest.

The shops caught on by the end of that century, and America had a short fling with it. Then for decades it went missing from drinks menus. In the countryside, the recipes went on being handed down through the generations, and they still are. Every winter, pubs and fairs hold contests to settle whose is best. One pub, the George Inn at Frant, called its contest the World Sloe Gin Championships, and in 2016 the judges, tasting blind, gave all of the top six places to sloe gins made at home. One maker, who says she won it twice, leaves hers in the sun, the way she says fruit liqueurs are made in Croatia.

That's my favourite part. A contest like that never asks who you got your recipe from. It only asks whether it's good. I like to think every gin on that table began as somebody else's and ended up as its maker's own. That's what you do with what you were given: every name attached, every piece tried, and only what survived handed over.

The cocktail I've made for you is a Blackthorn, a classic with a past: by 1947 that one name covered five very different recipes, and people have argued over it for most of a century. Of the five, I kept the one built from gin, sloe gin and dry vermouth, with orange bitters, because the sloe gin brings its own sweetness and the dry vermouth lets the fruit show. It's stirred until very cold, served small with no ice. At the end, a little lemon zest is pinched over it. Use whichever sloe gin you have, bought or made by someone you know. Taste it first, and if it's very sweet, use a little less and a little more gin. I left that call to you as a nod to those pub contests: whatever the recipe says, your own taste decides.

You're afraid it ends with you. It doesn't. Somewhere, someone already starts a sentence with your name, and you just haven't been there to hear it. So don't wait for someone to ask, or until you're old. This year, teach one thing you were taught to someone who'll keep it, and tell them the bit you changed, and why. That's how you end up among the people you quote.
