## whoYouAre

A friend starts a new job tomorrow and is sure they'll get it all wrong. You tell them what an old neighbour used to say: learn everyone's name on the first day, and the rest can wait. It's exactly right, and they write it down. Half of what you know came like that, with somebody's name on it, and you still hear their voice when you say it.

Here's the bit you don't say: you didn't hold on to all of it. Some of what you were told turned out to be wrong, and you let those bits go quietly, because saying so would feel like letting them down. What's left, you've tried for yourself. You can't always say why it works, only who told you, and when someone asks for the reason, you doubt yourself. The real fear is the day nobody asks. If it stops with you, a little of them stops too.

But each time you say "as my neighbour used to say", that neighbour is in the room again, and someone leaves with something to use tomorrow. You do a kindness to two people at once.

## yours

Since the 1800s, households in rural Britain have made sloe gin: the berries of the blackthorn bush, left in sweetened gin and poured at Christmas and New Year. Along with damson gin, it was simply something you did at harvest.

By the end of that century it was being made to sell, and America had a short fling with it. Then for decades it went missing from drinks menus. In the countryside, the recipes went on being handed down through the generations, and they still are. Every winter, pubs and fairs hold contests to settle whose is best. One pub, the George Inn at Frant, hosted one called the World Sloe Gin Championships, and in 2016 the judges, tasting blind, gave all of the top six places to sloe gins made at home. One maker, who reports winning it twice, wrote of trying the way fruit liqueurs are made in Croatia: leaving the jars in the sun.

That's my favourite part. A blind tasting can't ask who you got your recipe from. It can only ask whether it's good. I like to think every home-made gin on that table began as somebody else's and ended up as its maker's own. That's what you do with what you were given: every name attached, every piece tried, and only what survived handed over.

The cocktail I've made for you is a Blackthorn, a classic that a barman in London was already calling very old in 1922, when it came two different ways. Of the five a 1947 bar guide printed, I kept the one built from gin, sloe gin and dry vermouth, with orange bitters, because the sloe gin is already sweetened and the dry vermouth lets the fruit show. It's stirred until very cold, served small with no ice. At the end, a lemon peel gets one pinch over the glass, for the scent. Use a traditional sloe gin, bought or made by someone you know, and keep one rule: the two gins always make 60 ml between them. Taste the sloe gin first. The sweeter it is, the less of it and the more of the plain gin. I left that call to you as a nod to those pub contests: whatever the recipe says, your own taste decides.

You're afraid it ends with you. It doesn't. Somewhere, someone already starts a sentence with your name, and you just haven't been there to hear it. So don't wait for someone to ask, or until you're old. This year, teach one thing you were taught to someone who'll keep it, and tell them the bit you changed, and why. That's how you end up among the people you quote.
