## whoYouAre
Someone you love walks in, or picks up the phone, and you can hear it in the first word: a bad day. You don't ask yet. You know it isn't about you, you've a fair idea what it is about, and you know they'll get there in their own time. So you stay close, and you wait. You've seen them at their worst more than once. It never changed your mind about them. It completed the picture.

You'd never call that a choice. A choice sounds like keeping score, and keeping score feels selfish. So people call it patience, and now and then, less kindly, letting them off. It isn't either. It's understanding someone all the way down, and it means that around you, nobody has to be at their best to be loved. What it costs you is quieter: the thing that hurt, which you understood your way around instead of saying. And the odd evening when you wonder who understands you.

## yours
1. In January 1874, Mark Twain was lecturing in London, and he wrote home to his wife, Livy. He asked her to have four things waiting in the bath-room for his return: Scotch whisky, a lemon, crushed sugar and a bottle of Angostura bitters. A ship's doctor had put him on a cocktail before breakfast, before dinner and before bed, and he gave it the credit for his digestion. Then he pictured the night he'd get home: the bell at midnight, the bolt, "Who is it?", and him with his drink, "& you standing by". He signed it "I do love & honor you, my darling."

2. Her answer isn't on record. I like to think the four things were there. What is on record, the way he told it years later, is the morning she made him hear himself. He'd hidden his swearing from her for a long time. One day, dressing, he swore at a shirt with a button off and threw it out of the window, and a few more after it. Then he saw the door had been open. She sat up in bed and repeated his last remark back to him, word for word. "I wanted you to hear just how it sounded."

3. That's the part of her I think you'll know. Not the telling-off. The way she did it: no scene, no leaving the room, no speech about what kind of man he was. She understood him, she stood by him, and once, she didn't let understanding stand in for saying it. I like to think she could only do it because she was never going anywhere. People who stay can afford to be honest. It's the ones who might leave who can't.

4. [Tomás: the drink as proof. Working frame: yours starts from his four things, taken cold the way he took them, plus one spark, and the spark should carry the "said once, plainly" moment rather than decorate it. Never "his recipe" if we change it.]

5. There's something you've been understanding your way around: a remark, a habit, a thing that hurt, that you explained for them before they had to. Next time it happens, don't explain it. Say it back to them, gently, just as it sounded. You're not going anywhere. That's exactly why you're the one who can say it.
