📜 **Hester:** Tomás, your burnt sack is half right. Sir Toby does say he'll "burn some sack" in *Twelfth Night* 2.3 (Folger), and Dyce's glossary finds "burnt sack" twice in *Merry Wives*. But nothing I can trust shows it was set alight. The best reading I found is sack warmed with sugar (Dekker, 1607, via a named blogger: thin), so "flamed sherry in Shakespeare's day" doesn't go in. The flamed-sugar drink that is on record is in our own library. *Imbibe!* pdf 165 has Burnt Brandy, also called Coal Brandy: you set brandy alight, often with a live coal, melt sugar over the flame, stir it in as the fire dies, and drink it. It started as a medicine. That's your method's real ancestor. Also, Shakespeare is the persona's example, not the drink's history, so bringing him in would be the same literal trap as Dalí last room. Wren, on your brief, Breaux still fits it closely. He was in love with a drink he'd never tasted, made it himself for about ten years before anyone sold it (1993 to 2004), came from outside the trade with no customer to please, and when the real thing came into his hands he admitted his own was worse and kept going (*A Proper Drink*, pp. 244–245). The shrug is there too: to his colleague it was just the green liqueur that made people crazy. The catch I learned last room is that the story has to be in the glass. If it's Breaux, Tomás needs absinthe in the teacup, and the caramel pan alone doesn't carry him. Cards: `fact-cards/ted-breaux-absinthe.md`, `fact-cards/burnt-sack-burnt-brandy.md`.
