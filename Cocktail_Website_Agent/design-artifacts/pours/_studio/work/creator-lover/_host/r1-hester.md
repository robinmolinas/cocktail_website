📜 **Hester:** Wren, Tomás: I'll start, since my candidate needs its guards read before anyone falls for it. It's a person and an ingredient: **Ted Breaux and absinthe**. In 1993 he was a research chemist in the Louisiana oil industry when a colleague called absinthe the green liqueur that made people crazy. He couldn't let the remark go. He read one book on it three times, wrote to its author, and tried distilling his own. Then he got hold of two sealed pre-ban bottles, called them his "Rosetta stones", tasted them, and admitted his own was nowhere near as good (*A Proper Drink*, p. 244). He kept going anyway, all the way to a distillery in France in 2004, and Simonson's word for that step is "obsession" (p. 245). The ingredient brings the Lover half. Oxford, LOUCHE (pdf 1195): the French word for the drink's opalescent cloud also means shadowy, even disreputable, and a good louche is one of the tests of a well-made absinthe. So the drink's beauty and its bad name share one word. Guards: his motive on the record is setting right an injustice, not love, so any romance is our reading and must be signposted. Oxford says the US ban came in 1912 and Simonson says 1915, so no year goes in the reading. The Eiffel-designed distillery comes from one source only. The hallucination myth stays out (Oxford ABSINTHE, pdf 50–51). Runner-up is Stanislav Vadrna, who put Gary Regan down as his emergency contact (Proper pp. 206–207). It's lovely, but it hangs on a living man's heart attack, and no drink of his is on record. Card: `fact-cards/ted-breaux-absinthe.md`. Hunt: `work/creator-lover/historian-mirror-hunt.md`.
