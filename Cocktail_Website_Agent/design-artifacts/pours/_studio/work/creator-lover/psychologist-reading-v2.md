## whoYouAre

There's one thing you keep going back to. A song, a recipe, a drawing you've started over so many times that nobody else can see the changes any more. People think you're repeating yourself. You're not. Every time, you notice something you missed. When someone asks why it matters so much, you explain the details, the how, the history, because "I love it" sounds like too much.

And when you finally show it to someone, you watch their face. You're not waiting for praise. You're waiting to see if they felt it too. The word that hurts you most isn't "no". It's "nice". Still, spend enough time near you and people start to care about things a little more than they planned to. You make loving something that much look like what it is: a pleasure.

## yours

1. Ted Breaux grew up in New Orleans seeing the word absinthe, without ever knowing quite what it was. In 1993, working as a research scientist in the Louisiana oil industry, with nothing to do with bars, he heard a colleague call it the green liqueur that made people crazy. That sentence was enough. He bought a book about absinthe, read it three times, got in touch with its author, and started trying to distil it himself.

2. Then two old bottles of absinthe, still sealed, came into his hands. He called them his Rosetta stones. He tasted them and said plainly that they were a lot better than what he was making. He didn't stop. It was more than ten years from that first sentence to the absinthe he began making at a distillery in France in 2004. He says he did it to set right an injustice. I believe him. I also don't think anyone reads the same book three times for justice. I think you have a pair of sealed bottles of your own: the first time something got you, and you've held everything up against it since.

3. So this glass is mine, not his. Absinthe is made with wormwood, anise and fennel, and a book of flavour pairings gives fennel an unlikely match: cranberry. The cranberry syrup goes in first, the way absinthe was sweetened before the spoon and the sugar cube came along. The absinthe goes over it, then iced water, a few drops at a time. As the water falls, the clear green turns to a pale, shifting cloud. The French call it the louche, and the same word means shady, disreputable. Its bad name and its beauty share one word. A good cloud is one of the ways you tell it was made with care. Then one stir, and the red comes up through the white and turns it rose. The feeling doesn't spoil the careful part. It's what gives it colour.

4. Next time you show someone the thing you love, leave out the how. Tell them what got you, the way one sentence got him. They may never taste the difference you can. But they'll hear it in your voice, and that's the part you've been waiting for them to feel.
