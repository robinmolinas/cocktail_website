| kind | fact | meaning | speaksTo |
|---|---|---|---|
| person | James "Jimmie" Charters, barman at the Dingo in Montparnasse, Paris, from 1924 (he came "as assistant barman") (F2). | A real room with a real person running the bar: standing, not a title. | Authority on loan: the one everyone in the room looks to. |
| motive (on record, ghost-told) | His credo: success behind the bar comes from "an ability to understand the man or woman I am serving, to enter into his joys or woes", so they feel "the need of me as a person rather than a servant" (F1; *Joy* pdf 114–115 credits Cody's book). | The authority is spent on understanding people. Use a paraphrase in the reading and quote at most "joys or woes". | The one true thing: they give people a hearing. |
| fact (his own list) | His job, as he listed it: "settle fights, listen to intimate confidences, prevent jealousies, arrange parties, and crack jokes. I never worked so hard in all my life" (F4). | Listening sits right next to settling fights, and he calls it work. | Hidden: the patience takes effort and isn't temperament. |
| method (his rule) | Fights were "to be avoided at all costs". He could "usually" smell one coming (his "usually") and would try jokes or a drink on the house. "Usually the free drink works": they "forget their quarrel and drink to each other, or both drink to me, or in some way forget to be angry" (F5). | He answers heat with a welcome, not force, and the angry ones stay. ("Usually" is his word: keep it, it isn't "always".) | Gift: even the angry one leaves still welcome. Fear: chaos on their watch. |
| cost (on record) | He couldn't stop a regular's credit "for fear he will be angry, walk out, and everything will be gone" (F7). | Keeping people in the room cost him money and nerve. | It visibly costs them something. The private fear is that it comes apart anyway. |
| counterweight | It didn't always work: once he "had to carry" the brawlers "out, one at a time" (F9). | A calm that sometimes failed. It isn't a saint's story. | "I listened too long and it came apart anyway": honest, and gentle. |
| habit (on record) | When customers bought him drinks, he kept look-alikes under the bar (bitters and water, cordial and water) and raised "the appropriate glass", because "I believe in always drinking what the client drinks!" (F12). | He matched them glass for glass while keeping a clear head. *Our reading:* the steady face kept for everyone. | Hidden: people check their face first, so they keep it steady. **Guard C8:** never "fooled them". |
| ingredient lineage (his own drink) | A cocktail he invented at the Dingo: cognac, with a sixth each of Pernod, Amer-Picon, Mandarin and sweet cherry brandy, shaken, "either drunk straight or diluted with a little soda" (F11). The cognac share is OCR-garbled; 2/6 is our inference. | A real house drink to riff from. Only "a cocktail he invented at the Dingo" is fact; the proportions are not his unless confirmed, and anything we change is ours. | The glass as proof (Tomás's call). |
| method (the drink's gesture) | Arnold's "Languishing Ice Manhattans" (*Liquid Intelligence* pp. 87–88): one Manhattan stirred 15 seconds, then left on its ice 90 seconds before straining, came out "almost identical" to one stirred and strained at once. He says letting ice sit "for upward of 2 minutes won't ruin the drink"; the extra dilution is "remarkably negligible" (rye, not rum: applying it to our drink is Tomás's craft call). Condition (p. 87): "reasonably short (a couple minutes)" and ice "fairly large (not shaved)". | The stirred drink can wait while someone finishes. | The one true thing: let them finish. |
