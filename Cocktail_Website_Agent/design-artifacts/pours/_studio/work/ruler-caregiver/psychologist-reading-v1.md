## whoYouAre

You hear it before anyone else does. A joke at dinner that lands a little too hard. A voice going up half a note in a meeting. Two friends going quiet in the wrong way. By the time the others notice, you've already pulled up a chair, asked the one who's upset what they meant, and let them say all of it. Half the rows you've ended never got started, and nobody ever knew they were coming.

People take you for easygoing. You aren't, not really. You're listening the whole time, because when people are in your care, you'd rather hear the hard thing tonight than watch it break something later. You could settle most of it with one word, and you rarely use it. You'd rather they finish. It's worth more than you think: people say the real thing around you, because they're sure you'll hear all of it, and it's why they trust you with the hard calls. You never mention how hard you work at it, or the fear underneath: that the one time you miss it, it all comes apart, and someone you were looking after pays for it.

## yours

1. In 1924, a barman called Jimmie Charters started at the Dingo, a small bar in Montparnasse, Paris, as the assistant. Years later he told his story to a writer, Morrill Cody, and the job he described had very little to do with drinks. He was there to settle fights, to listen to people's secrets, to stop jealousies before they started, and to crack jokes. He'd never worked so hard in his life, he said.

2. Fights, he said, were to be avoided at all costs. He could smell one coming, and then he'd do anything to stop it. Usually that meant a drink on the house. And this is what gets me: the two who'd been about to fight would drink to each other, or to him, and somehow forget to be angry. It didn't always work. Once, when it didn't, he had to carry one side of the fight out, one at a time.

3. I like to think the drink was never the point. He wasn't deciding who was right. He was turning two people back towards each other before anyone got hurt, and that's what you do. He also admitted what it cost him. When a regular ran up a big tab, he couldn't stop the credit, for fear they'd get angry, walk out, and everything would be gone. I think you know that fear. It's the no you swallow to keep the room together.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is [TOMÁS: the stirred classic, named as famous, in appealing words], and it's stirred, not shaken, on purpose. Dave Arnold, who wrote a whole book on the science of cocktails, once left a stirred drink sitting on its ice for nearly two minutes before straining it, and it took on surprisingly little extra water. A shaken drink can't wait like that. So stir it, and if someone's still talking, leave the spoon where it is and let them finish. [TOMÁS: the spark, one clause, linked in words to Jimmie]

5. So here's my one push. You're right to hear people out, and they're right to trust you for it. But one day the kind thing will be the no: the tab stopped, the plan called off, the decision made before everyone agrees. Say it plainly, once they've finished. The people you listened to won't walk out. They stayed because you listened, and that's exactly why your no will hold.

## Resonance
**As the guest (a Caring Leader).** The tagline stops me: I've never counted the fights that didn't happen, and nobody else has either. whoYouAre ¶1 is three scenes I've lived, with no job and no title. ¶2's "You'd rather they finish" is the line: it's exactly what I do and never say. "Easygoing" is what people call me, and it stings a little, because it isn't ease. The fear is right and private. The story doesn't make me a saint: Jimmie fails once and carries them out, and he can't stop a tab. y3's "the no you swallow" is the hard thing I'd recognise. y5 asks me to do the one thing I avoid, and pays me back: my no will hold *because* I listened.

**As the sceptic.** Barnum check: "you hear it before anyone else does" could fit any sensitive person, but the next lines make it about stopping rows and letting people finish, which isn't the Angel's mood or the Gentle Giant's size. Sibling swap: the Judge would want the verdict; this reading refuses it ("He wasn't deciding who was right"). The Nanny's limit-setting is close to y5's "no"; the difference is the reason (keeping people from walking out vs keeping the fun lasting), and no word of fun appears. Risks: "everything would be gone" is his, about money and clients; I've widened it to "the room", signposted with "I think you know that fear". Open until Tomás fills y4 and Hester audits.
