## whoYouAre

You're easy to talk to and hard to finish. After an evening with you, people have said more than they meant to, and knowing a little less about you than they'd like. So they come back. Each time you give them a bit more: a story you don't tell at work, a look that says you noticed, a door left slightly open.

Here's the part they miss. You don't keep something back to be chased. You keep it back so there's still a reason to stay. At some point you decided that once someone has seen all of you, the finding is over, and they'll go. So there's always one thing you haven't said yet. And it works: people feel they've found something in you that no one else has. They're right. That's what you hand them: a sense of discovery most people never get from anyone. It's worth a great deal. But behind that last thing, you're on your own.

## yours

1. In 1994, on a second floor in New York's East Village, behind a door at the back of a Japanese restaurant, Angel's Share opened. Its owner wanted a Tokyo-style bar: small, intimate and formal. There was no standing, no big groups and no small talk, partly because many of the bartenders had little English. If you found the door, you'd found something.

2. Two years in, the people there didn't really know how to run it. Shinichi Ikeda had come over from Japan to play music. He'd been a bartender back home, and one day the owner told him to run the bar. Ikeda said the owner somehow knew. I like to think that's the moment you'd notice: someone saw what he hadn't put forward, and asked for it.

3. The writer who told its story says no New York bar opening in the 1990s mattered more to the cocktail revival's future. Yet it never grew and never put out a book, and the movement it helped inspire largely forgot it. Shingo Gokan, who came from Tokyo in 2006, worked there six years before he had a single friend who was a bartender. "We were always outside," he said. The same writer is careful to add that the door was shut from both sides. I recognise you in that. What you hold back stays whole, and it keeps you just outside.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Martini, the renowned classic, built from two things Ikeda put on that bar's menu: Martinis stirred the way he'd learned them in Japan, and his own drinks made with shochu and shiso leaf. Joining them in one glass is my idea. Shochu is a clear Japanese spirit, softer than gin, so gin takes the lead, with a splash of pale vermouth. A torn green shiso leaf is stirred with them, then lifted out and set aside, as a nod to that door at the back. Shiso and juniper share a piney note, so the leaf tucks itself in behind the gin, and what you get is clean, cold and green in a way you can't quite place. The leaf is there to be handed over.

5. So tell one person what you've been saving. Not when they've earned it: before. You're afraid there'll be nothing left to stay for. I don't think that's how it works with you. Being known completely is the one kind of closeness you've always kept from yourself, and it's the kind that doesn't need a reason to stay. They'll stay anyway. Let yourself find out.
