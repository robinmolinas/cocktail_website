## Title block

## whoYouAre (unchanged)

You can tell in a second when something is slightly off: the one wrong word on a page, the lamp in the wrong corner, the thing nobody else would ever notice. And you can't leave it. You send it back, with a note, and then again, until it's right.

People say you're hard to please. The ones who stayed know it's simpler than that: you'd rather be blamed for all of it than praised for part of it. What you rarely admit is the other habit, the one after everyone's gone home: quietly redoing someone else's part yourself, because fixing it felt quicker than explaining. Nobody asked you to carry the whole thing. You just can't bear to see it come out ordinary. And the people beside you end up doing the best work they've ever done, sometimes without knowing why.

## yours

1. Before Pegu Club opened in New York in 2005, a bartender named Audrey Saunders was clear about one thing. In her words: "I want a project where I'm making the calls." The logo alone took months. Nothing reached the menu, not even a classic, until it had passed her tasting.

2. The people who worked there remember how that went. She'd give them the measures and taste what they made. Then she'd set the glass down and give them new measures, and they'd make another. "Little tiny tweaks," one of them called it. The book that tells the story says an eighth of an ounce could turn into a federal case. Later, another of her bartenders named the flaw in working like that: there is no perfect drink. And in the same breath: "That is the way I develop cocktails now."

3. I think you'd have known that bar the moment you walked in. Not for the fuss. For what sat under it. The part I keep coming back to is who made the next drink. It wasn't her. She tasted, she gave new measures, and they made it again. I like to think that's how, for one of them at least, her way of tasting became his.

4. This drink borrows the frame of one of hers. She started one of her drinks from her own measures for a Sidecar (brandy, orange liqueur, lemon), and split what went inside each part. This one keeps those measures to the millilitre; what's inside them is mine. Cognac. Orange liqueur, with a little honey inside the sweet part. Lemon. And one dash of brine from plain green olives: too little to taste as salt, enough to make everything else sit right. Nobody will ever name it.

5. Next time someone's part comes back not quite right, you'll feel the old pull to stay late and redo it yourself. Leave it on the table instead. Point to exactly what's wrong, and let them make it again. You'll still be making the calls. You just won't be the only one who knows why.
