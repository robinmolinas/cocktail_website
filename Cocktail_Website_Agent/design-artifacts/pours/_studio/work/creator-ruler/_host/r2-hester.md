📜 **Hester:** Wren, both of your Donn Beach claims are sourced, and one needs correcting. **The name:** Oxford gives "né Ernest Raymond Beaumont-Gantt" (pdf 240). That he *legally* changed it is secondary: Wayne Curtis, *American Heritage*, 2006, "the joint became so much part of his personality that he legally changed his name". Curtis gives no date, so there's no year in the reading. **The code:** Oxford says he swapped ingredient names for numbers that matched the labels on his bottles, to stop rivals poaching the recipes through his staff (TIKI pdf 2024). Curtis quotes a period *Post* report: "the service-bar help cannot memorize Don's various occult ingredients". So it isn't that his bartenders couldn't read the recipes. They read the code every night; what he kept from them was what the numbers stood for. That's why I'd still choose Saunders, and it's your own position that decides it. Donn kept his crew in the dark. Saunders sent the drink back and told them what she tasted. Maloney: "She would take a sip and put it down, and she'd rattle off some more specs, and you'd make another one ... Little tiny tweaks" (*A Proper Drink*, p. 163). And the crew carried it on: Meehan, "That is the way I develop cocktails now" (same page); Oxford says her opening bartenders all went on to open influential bars (PEGU CLUB pdf 1490). Donn's ending is the fear coming true. Hers is the standard continuing in other people's hands. Tomás, Donn fits your "to the last drop" more literally (Oxford's Zombie: blend "no more than five seconds", pdf 2229). But Saunders is on the record too: "an eighth of an ounce" treated as a serious matter (p. 163), consistency as her watchword, off-menu orders discouraged (p. 165). Your measured water suits her. One condition: the freezer method is Arnold's and modern, so it's never credited to her. The rows are added to `historian-candidates.md`.
