📜 **Hester:** Wren, I'll put two on the table before you open. I'm hunting for leaving things out as the creative act, and as a kind of honesty. **A (my pick): Julio Bermejo and the Tommy's Margarita.** By the late 1970s the ordinary Margarita was sour mix, cheap triple sec and not much tequila (Oxford, MARGARITA pdf 1237). Bermejo was back behind the bar of his family's very ordinary restaurant after his own plans had failed. He switched to 100% agave tequila at four times the cost, a call he describes as made "as a young person with no authority" (*A Proper Drink* p. 81). Then he threw out the sour mix, and over the years he dropped the orange liqueur too. What was left was tequila, lime and agave syrup, a drink that shows the spirit instead of covering it. He called it the house Margarita, and other people gave it the restaurant's name (Proper p. 84). Nothing was added. Three things were taken out. For Tomás, a spark: agave syrup is *commonly* made from blue agave, the same plant as tequila (Oxford, AGAVE NECTAR pdf 79). So apart from the lime, nearly everything in the glass comes from one plant. There's also a gesture on the record: he thins his syrup so none clings to the jigger (*Joy* pdf 397). Guards: no year, because 1990, c. 1990 and "early 1990s" all appear, and Proper says the syrup only reached the US in the late 1990s. Don't say "invented", because it evolved. His father's reaction stays out ("slapped his son's ears back" may be only an idiom). **B: Dick Bradsell's Bramble.** He dropped the soda and Bénédictine from a Sling, and in his own words the blackberry liqueur put him back in a childhood blackberry patch (Oxford pdf 336; Proper p. 31). But only Oxford tells it as a subtraction, and the dates disagree (1989 against 1991 or '92). One more thing Wren should weigh: Proper p. 84 says the drink is so simple that a Denny's served it in 2014. For someone who fears the ordinary, that's either the sting or the point.
