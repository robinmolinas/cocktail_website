## whoYouAre

You're the one holding all of it. The trip, the move, the wedding, the project at work: you know where every piece is, who arrives when, what happens if the train's late, and how each part leans on the others. Everyone else just turns up, and it works. Some of them call it luck.

You don't mention it, but the evening has already happened once, in your head, every way it could go wrong. And while everyone else enjoys it, part of you is still on duty, watching for the one thing you didn't put there: the guest nobody told you about, the plan someone quietly changed, the piece that moves by itself. Not because it would ruin everything. Because it would mean something in your world had a mind of its own.

It gives people more than they know. Because you hold all of it, the rest of them can put it down. A whole room gets to be carefree for a night, and that's your doing. Hardly anyone can give that.

## yours

1. In 1731, James Ashley took the lease of a big old tavern on Ludgate Hill, in London, and renamed it for four things at once: coffee, punch, beer and ale. He had extensive cellars, where he aged spirits bought in large lots. By his advertisements and accounts of the bar, it seems every punch began from the same base: a shrub of sour Seville-orange peel and juice, bottled and kept in the cellar. With that ready, any amount of punch could be made as quickly as wine is poured. The house was always crowded.

2. The hands at the counter were mostly someone else's. His chief bartender, Mrs. Gaywood, and the young women under her put each drink together in front of the guest. The plan was his, the cups went through other people's hands, and it held. Something else moved too, and who moved it is anyone's guess. Before long the coffee, beer and ale stopped being the heart of the business, and the place was known simply as the London Punch House. Of the four things he'd planned, one became what the house was known for.

3. Then the bit I can't help smiling at. In the 1750s he handed the bar to his son and turned to dealing in spirits from his cellars. When he died, in 1776, he was back running the bar, with his son. Maybe his son asked him to. I like to think he missed holding it. You'd understand that. And I like to think you'd see what I see in his house: the pieces he didn't hold himself, the hands at the counter and the trade that outgrew the other three, were what made it famous.

4. His recipe is lost, so the cocktail I've made for you is mine, built from what's known of his: one part spirit to two of everything else, which was his standard, and, it seems, a Seville-orange shrub. The spirit is Batavia arrack, from Java, distilled mostly from cane and rice: pungent, full of flavour, and the dearest spirit he made punch with. I chose it as the loud piece, the one with a will of its own. What holds it is the shrub: orange peel rubbed into sugar until the oil comes out, then the juice, bottled and kept cold, with a bottle of water beside it. Arrack, shrub and cold water go straight into the cup, a few turns of a spoon, no ice. It's sharp, bright and warm at once, and the arrack still sounds clearly through it. The order doesn't quiet the loud part. It gives it somewhere to stand.

5. Then one request. Keep holding it all: everyone near you is lucky you do. But next time a piece moves by itself, a guest you didn't plan for or a change you weren't warned about, don't put it back at once. Give it the evening. What you built is stronger than you fear, and the piece that moved may turn out to be the part everyone remembers. And you'd finally get to be surprised by your own world, like everyone else in it.
