| the build | In 1731 Ashley leased a large old tavern on Ludgate Hill and renamed it for four things at once: coffee house, punch house, Dorchester beer, Welsh ale (Oxford ASHLEY pdf 160; F2). | The whole thing laid out before the doors opened, every trade in its place. | holds the whole thing at once |
| the work behind the counter | He had extensive cellars, bought his spirits in large lots and aged them himself, and that is why the punch could be cheap (Oxford pdf 162; F11). | The part nobody drinking upstairs ever saw is what made it work for them. The page ties the cellars to the low price, not to anything else. | the map never switches off |
| the base, bottled and cellared | By the evidence of his advertisements (Oxford calls it circumstantial), every punch was made from a shrub of sour Seville-orange peel and juice, premixed, bottled and kept in the cellar; Oxford's SHRUB entry says he sold it too, up to whole hogsheads (pdf 162, 1793–1794; F12–F13, C1). | The one piece prepared in full before the evening starts, so that nothing during it has to be thought out again. Labelled "it seems". Say "bottled and cellared", never "made before anyone asked" (G3). | provide structure |
| what others got | Because of the shrub, his bartenders could make punch of any size as fast as a glass of wine is poured, and the house was always crowded: big, well placed, good and cheap (Oxford pdf 162; F14–F15). | Everyone else got speed and ease; the crowd never had to think about why it worked. The speed comes from the shrub, not from mixing in front of the customer (C3). | a whole room gets to be carefree because one person held it all |
| the hands | Oxford's PUNCH entry corrects itself: Ashley, "or rather" his chief barkeeper Mrs. Gaywood and the young women under her, put each drink together in front of the guest (pdf 1328; ASHLEY pdf 162; F8–F9). | The plan was his; the cups went through other people's hands (Oxford's "or rather"), and it held. | the piece that moved | Before long, the coffee, beer and ale stopped being central, and the house was known simply as the London Punch House (Oxford pdf 160; F3). | Of the four things he planned, one became what the house was known for. Told as what people came for, never as a name the public took (Wren's ruling 1). | fear: the one piece that moves on its own; it may be what people came for |
| the return | For a time in the 1750s he handed the bar to his son and dealt in spirits from his cellars; when he died in 1776 he was back running the bar, with his son (Oxford pdf 163; F19). | The hidden truth, gently funny: the planner never quite stood behind the counter as a guest of his own house. | the mark | He had an inscription painted across the front of the house telling how he'd lowered the price of punch and made it popular, "Pro Bono Publico", for the public good (Oxford pdf 163; F18). | His mark was about what the public got.
