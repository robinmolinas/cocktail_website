## whoYouAre

Most days you're easy to miss: quiet in a meeting, with a joke that lands a second too late. Then something turns up that only you can sort out, like a friend's figures that won't balance the night before they're due, or a letter that has to be exactly right. For an hour you're someone else entirely. Afterwards you go back to being easy to miss, and that part is on purpose. You'd rather nobody near you felt outshone.

There's a question you never ask out loud. When your phone rings, you can usually guess what it's about, and you've never quite known whether people come for you or for what you can do. So you keep it sharp, every day, because going rusty is the one thing that really frightens you. And this is what it gives the people you're with: someone who could make them feel small and chooses, every time, to make them feel capable instead. That's rarer than the ability.

## yours

1. Constante Ribalaigua came to Havana from near Barcelona as a child. At sixteen, as he told it later, his father, a bartender himself, asked if he'd like to learn the trade, and he said yes. He made his name at La Florida, the Floridita. In 1935 he bought it, and by the end of that decade he was rich and known far beyond Cuba. He's remembered as quiet and reserved, and not much to look at. And every morning he still put on the white jacket and went behind the bar to mix drinks.

2. He squeezed every lime by hand, with his fingers only, though he was one of the first to put electric mixers on a bar. He told a journalist in 1935 that he made up a new drink every day, "as self discipline". Among the drinks he created was the Floridita: rum, red vermouth, a little chocolate liqueur and lime. He put out booklets of the bar's recipes, and for reasons nobody knows, that one isn't in any of them. A visiting bartender, Trader Vic, came across it in 1938, put it on the first menu of his own place, and printed it years later. Without him, it might have been lost.

3. I like to think the jacket was the point: he no longer needed it, and he chose it anyway, morning after morning. I recognise you in that, with one difference. You've come to think of the ordinary you as the cover and the ability as the real thing. I see it differently. What you can do is the jacket: you put it on for other people. Who you are is the one who keeps choosing to. And of everything he made, one of the drinks that lasted is the one his booklets never printed.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is the Floridita, as Trader Vic wrote it down, with two changes of my own. It's white rum, red vermouth, a little crème de cacao (a chocolate liqueur as clear as water), and grenadine and orange curaçao, one spoonful of each, shaken hard and strained into a slim stemmed glass that's been in the freezer. My first change is half a lime instead of a whole one, because a whole one makes it too sharp. Squeeze it with your fingers, as a nod to the man in the white jacket. The second change is the twist: the lime's own peel instead of orange, so the lime that balances the drink scents it too. You'd only notice it if you knew, and that felt like you. It's a cocktail almost nobody has heard of, chocolate and lime, and a delicious one.

5. One thing to take with you. People will keep introducing you by what you can do: "this is the one who can sort anything out." When it happens again, let them, and then add something about you that has nothing to do with it: the song you can't stop playing, the town you'd move to tomorrow, something you're hopeless at. Watch which one they ask about. I suspect it won't be the ability. That answers what you've wondered for years. They like the person wearing the jacket.

**closing line:** Tomás's. His draft's second sentence repeats y5's proposal almost word for word ("introduces you by what you can do, add one thing… nothing to do with it"), so one of the two has to move. y5 needs the scene. A suggestion only, since the line is his: *Half the lime, squeezed by hand. Next time you're introduced, give them the other half.* It keeps his physics, agrees with the position, and "the other half" lands twice without being explained.
