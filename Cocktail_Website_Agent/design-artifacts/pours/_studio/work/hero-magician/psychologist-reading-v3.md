## whoYouAre

Most days you're easy to miss: quiet in a meeting, with a joke that lands a second too late. Then something turns up that only you can sort out, like a friend's figures that won't balance the night before they're due, or a letter that has to be exactly right. For an hour you're someone else entirely. Afterwards you go back to being easy to miss, and that part is on purpose. You'd rather nobody near you felt outshone.

Ask which of the two is the costume, and you'd hesitate. Years ago, without quite noticing, you decided the quiet one was the disguise, and the ability was the real you. So you keep it sharp, every day, because going rusty would feel like disappearing. And this is what it gives the people you're with: someone who could make them feel small and chooses, every time, to make them feel capable instead. That's rarer than the ability.

## yours

1. Constante Ribalaigua came to Havana from near Barcelona as a child. At sixteen, as he told it later, his father, a bartender himself, asked if he'd like to learn the trade, and he said yes. He made his name at La Florida, the Floridita. In 1935 he bought it, and by the end of that decade he was rich, and known abroad too. He's remembered as quiet and reserved, and not much to look at. And every morning he still put on the white jacket and went behind the bar to mix drinks.

2. He squeezed every lime by hand, with his fingers only, though he was one of the first to put electric mixers on a bar. He told a journalist in 1935 that he made up a new drink every day, "as self discipline". Among the drinks he created was the Floridita: rum, red vermouth, a little chocolate liqueur and lime. He put out booklets of the bar's recipes, and for reasons we don't know, that one isn't in any of them. A visiting bartender, Trader Vic, came across it in 1938, put it on the first menu of his own place, and printed it years later. Without him, it might have been lost.

3. I like to think the jacket was the point: he no longer needed it, and he chose it anyway, morning after morning. I recognise you in that. You'd probably call the ability the real you. I'd call it the jacket: you put it on for other people. Who you are is the one who keeps choosing to. And of everything he made, one of the drinks that lasted is the one that isn't in any of his booklets.

4. The cocktail I've made for you starts from the Floridita, with two changes of my own. It's white rum, red vermouth, a little crème de cacao (a chocolate liqueur as clear as water), and grenadine and orange curaçao, one spoonful of each, shaken hard and strained into a slim stemmed glass that's been in the freezer. My first change is half a lime instead of a whole one, because a whole one makes it too sharp. Squeeze it with your fingers, the way he squeezed his limes. The second change is the twist: the lime's own peel instead of orange, so the lime that balances the drink scents it too. You'd only notice it if you knew, and that felt like you. It tastes of chocolate and lime, and it's a delicious one.

5. One thing to take with you. People will keep introducing you by what you can do: "this is the one who can sort anything out." When it happens again, let them, and then add something about you that has nothing to do with it: the song you can't stop playing, the town you'd move to tomorrow, something you're hopeless at. Watch which one they ask about. And let the quiet one be introduced too. It was never the disguise.

**closing line:** Tomás's: *Half the lime, squeezed by hand. When you're next introduced, bring up something the introduction doesn't cover.* It agrees with y5's position and borrows none of y5's words (R4).
