## whoYouAre

You walk in and you know at once. Wrong table, the light's too harsh, and that song isn't helping. So you say so, kindly, usually. If nothing happens, a little less kindly. And it's never only your own evening you're asking for. You want all of it right: the room, the glass, the music, everyone at their best.

People hear a demand. What you're really saying is that you still think it can be lovely. Most people stopped expecting that years ago. You didn't. You rarely let that part show, because hope is easy to laugh at, and a demand at least sounds strong. And you mind being called difficult more than you let on. What you dread is asking, watching nobody move, and then the whole thing sliding, bit by bit, into a mess. So here's what your asking gives. Because you said something, everyone gets the better night, including the ones who'd never have asked. Most of them wanted it too. It matters more than anyone thinks to tell you.

## yours

1. In 1997, Vernon Chalker opened a bar on a small laneway in Melbourne and gave it over entirely to gin. It was an odd choice then. Chalker drank vodka himself, like nearly everyone, and hardly anybody cared about gin. But he reckoned the Martini and the gin and tonic were the two best drinks ever made, and that settled it.

2. What he asked for was the whole picture. In his words, it had to look like it had always been there: a Budapest bar of the 1890s, done up in the 1950s and still going. Heavy old sofas went in around a sleek bar. He bought every gin he could find, about fifteen back then, and drove out of his way for some of them. He called it Gin Palace, because the name sounded, as he put it, funny, fun and "a bit overblown". The writer who tells the story calls the bar bizarre. It was also an instant success.

3. This is the bit I like best. He called it a bit much himself, first, and then did it anyway, right down to the age of the furniture. And people came. By his account, most bars then sold nine vodkas for every gin. At his, orders split down the middle, and even the vodka drinkers had two Martinis of their own on the list. I like to think that's insisting done for the right reasons: nobody gets left out of the better version. It's what you do too.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Martini, one of the two classics he built that bar on, made the generous way: more vermouth than gin, like one of the ten Martinis on his first list. (Vermouth is wine steeped with herbs and spices.) A bartender from New Zealand, Jacob Briars, remembered Gin Palace as the first place he'd seen orange bitters, and also the first time he'd seen anyone use "more than a dropper" of Noilly Prat, a French dry vermouth. So both are here. It's stirred long over ice, then poured into a big, chilled V-shaped glass. Then one thing of mine: a spoon of Tokaji, Hungary's golden sweet wine, as a nod to the Budapest he imagined. It rounds the dry edge, so the drink comes out gentler and silkier than most Martinis.

5. So keep asking. Please don't stop. But borrow his trick. You and I both know it isn't too much, so call it a bit much yourself, with a grin, and then ask anyway. It's hard to call someone difficult when they've laughed first, and once people are laughing with you, they'll help you get it right. And when it's right, enjoy it as much as you knew you would.

**closing line (suggestion for Tomás, his to write):** *Stir it until the glass mists. Then call it a bit much yourself, and ask anyway.*
