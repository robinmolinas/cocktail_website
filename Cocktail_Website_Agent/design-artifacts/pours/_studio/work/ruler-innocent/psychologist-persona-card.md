# Persona card: The Diva (ruler-innocent)

Wren, round 1, 2026-10-01. Read from `persona.py ruler-innocent` (essence, story, both archetypes' goals, fears and tone), the ten siblings (five authored readings in this family, the Perfectionist's read in full) and the near neighbours in other families. Story checked against *A Proper Drink* pp. 139-143, read whole.

## Core truth
**Somewhere along the way most people agreed to expect less. The Diva never agreed.** The insisting (the table, the light, the song, the glass) isn't about being difficult or grand. It's a grown-up still holding a child's certainty that things can be beautiful, and that somebody has to say so out loud or it won't happen.

## Shows / hides
- **Shows:** the request. Out loud, specific, often more than once. "Not this table. That one." Sends things back. Knows it can look like a fuss, and makes it anyway.
- **Hides:** how much it matters. A wrong detail can really spoil a night for them, and they hide that it's hope and not snobbery, because hope sounds naive and demand at least sounds strong. Also hidden: being called difficult stings. They act as if it doesn't.

## Hidden fear → hidden gift
- **Fear:** asking and watching nobody move. The request that isn't heard, and then the slow slide into a mess because nobody cared enough to insist. When they're not heard, it comes out louder than they meant (that's the "petulant" in the sheet: fear, not entitlement). Underneath, the Innocent's fear of punishment: being told off for wanting too much.
- **Gift:** because they asked, everyone gets the better night. Most people wanted the window table and the good glasses too; they'd just stopped expecting them. The Diva's insisting gives everyone else permission to have wanted it. Say this out loud in whoYouAre (Robin's rule: name what their way gives, and that it's worth something).

## The "real me" line to aim for
- *"People call it demanding. You just never agreed to expect less."*
- Tagline direction (invites, positive, fun): *"You were never asking for too much. Everyone else was asking for too little."* ("too little" appears in three pours' text; check contexts before it ships.)

## Truth vs. legend
The true story, told with relish. They like a big story but they'd smell flattery. They want to hear that the insisting was right, and why, from someone who enjoyed it.

## Tone
Confident and decisive (primary), honest and optimistic (secondary). The bartender should sound delighted, a little theatrical, and on their side: laughing *with* them at how much it is, never *at* them. Funny is allowed and wanted. Never gushing (they'd distrust it), never solemn.

## Distinct from siblings (and neighbours)
- **The Perfectionist (*On Their Behalf*)** is the closest. That one's word is *no*, said quietly and without its reason, so nothing careless goes out; it protects a standard for someone at the end. The Diva's word is *yes, this, more*, said loudly; it's about beauty and occasion, and the Diva is in the room to enjoy it. Never "careless version", never "add the why", never "turn something down".
- **The Noble Ruler (*A Place Kept*)** makes room for everyone; **the Caring Leader (*Go On*)** stops the fight. The Diva isn't keeping peace or making room: the Diva is raising the evening, and doesn't mind a little friction to get there.
- **The Magnetic Leader (Ciro, unauthored)** gets their way by charm, so people want to follow. The Diva gets it by asking, plainly. Keep charm out.
- Across families: **the Morale Booster (*Anyway*)** decides it'll be a good night and builds the fun for a group (owns "decide", "grey", "flat"). **The Stand-up (*Is It Just Me*)** says what everybody's thinking (watch "everyone wanted it too, you said it"). **The Bumpkin (*Good as It Is*)** is easy to delight and owns "let the whole table see you enjoying it". **The Trend Setter (*Still Yours*)** owns "liked it first" and choosing the unfashionable thing. **The Trickster (*No Accident*)** owns "being ignored" as the fear: the Diva's fear is written as *unheard*, the request nobody acts on.

## Traps
- **Flattery:** "you have impeccable taste", "you deserve the best", "you know what you want". The compliment the Diva hears all day. Recognise what the asking is *for*.
- **Status and luxury** (the sheet's Rolex and Mercedes): never price, brands for show, exclusivity, VIP. The want is beauty, not rank.
- **Mocking:** tantrum jokes, "high maintenance", "drama", "petulant". Humour with them only.
- **Gender:** "diva" is gender-coded and the sheet's example is Mariah Carey. No celebrity, no gendered imagery or words, no "camp" (Briars's label for Chalker stays out: it can read as a label about sexuality).
- **Telling them to lower the bar or let it be messy.** That's their fear, not a gift. The position can soften *how* they ask, never *whether*.
- **The tantrum with nothing beautiful behind it.** Every insistence in the reading needs its beautiful result.
- **A drink you perform** (lesson so far): glamour in the glass, not theatre at the table.
- **Owned motifs:** above, plus "too much" (in many pours: fine as a phrase, never a frame).

## Story test (what the story must show)
Someone who insisted on how a whole thing should be, in detail, when the safe choice was easier; was thought odd or too much for it; and was proved right because people loved the result. The insisting must come from delight, not snobbery. Comedy welcome. Out: a refusal story (the Perfectionist's), a contrarian "nobody else liked it" story (the Trend Setter's), a tantrum, punishment or humiliation.

## Verdict on the plan's lead: Vernon Chalker and Gin Palace, Melbourne, 1997 (*Proper* pp. 140-142)
**Accept, with fences.** Re-read whole.
- **The mirror is the whole picture insisted on.** His brief: make it "look like it had always been there", a Budapest bar of the 1890s, done up in the 1950s and still going (p. 141). Heavy old sofas round a sleek bar; every gin he could find, some fetched out of his way; ten Martinis on the list. His own word for the name: funny, fun and "a bit overblown". Then: "bizarre", and "an instant success", and orders split half and half where bars sold nine vodkas to one gin (p. 142). That's the Diva: knows it's a bit much, does it anyway, and everyone comes.
- **The drink is the proof:** Briars had never seen anyone use more than a dropper of Noilly Prat, or orange bitters (p. 142); the list had a Martini "with more vermouth than gin" (p. 141). No reason to accept less: the vermouth everyone else rationed.
- **Fence 1, the reason for gin.** The page says Chalker drank vodka "like everyone", and vodka had "been done" by the Mink Bar; he chose gin "exactly for the reason, perversely, that nobody then cared about it" (p. 141). So never say he loved gin all along, and keep the contrarian reason to one clause at most: it's the Trend Setter's ground. His belief is quotable: the Martini and the G&T are "the two best drinks ever made". That's the Diva's line.
- **Fence 2:** "bizarre" belongs to the bar (p. 142). "A fair description of Chalker himself" is Simonson's line about the man, and "odd ducks", "flamboyant" and "camp" are labels: none in guest text. His own words about his own bar are fine.
- **Fence 3:** "the first cocktail bar" is Bax's opinion (p. 142). No "first" claim. The casino, the 1998 Act (and its date sitting after the 1997 opening) and six o'clock closing stay out.
- **Gap, owned:** nobody on the page calls him demanding or difficult. Don't invent the pushback. "People call it demanding" lives in the guest's half; the story carries "bizarre, and an instant success".
- **Correcting my own plan note:** I wrote that gin was "the ignored thing given the spotlight". On the whole page that's a concept choice, not a rescue. At most an "I like to think", and probably not at all.
- **Backup:** Bix (Oxford pdf 277) stays the backup: insistence on occasion, but nobody thought it odd, so the reveal is weaker.

## Position, two directions (ruled once the story is)
- **A. Say the delight as loudly as the request.** People hear what you send back; they rarely hear when it's right. Tell whoever made it right, as loudly. (Keep apart from the Bumpkin's "let the whole table see you enjoying it".)
- **B. Say it's a bit much before anyone else can, and ask anyway.** Chalker's own "a bit overblown". Owning it out loud takes the sting out of "difficult" and keeps the joy.
