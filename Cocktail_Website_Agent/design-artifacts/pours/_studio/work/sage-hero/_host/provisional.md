# Pour — At the Time · The Grandmaster (sage-hero)

## Cocktail

- **name:** At the Time
- **tagline:** You'll let a good move look bad, and not say a word.
- **glassware:** heavy double old-fashioned glass (about 350 ml), one large block of coffee ice
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 50 ml | barrel-proof (cask-strength) straight rye whiskey | any, 55 to 62%; if yours is 63 to 68%, use 25 ml of water below instead of 15 |
| 12.5 ml (2½ tsp) | demerara syrup | equal weights demerara sugar and hot water, stirred until dissolved, cooled |
| 2 dashes | Angostura bitters | |
| 15 ml | cold water | |
| 1 large block | coffee ice | ordinary black coffee (filter or cafetière, no sugar, no milk), brewed the way you'd drink it, cooled, then frozen overnight in a large silicone cube mould (about 5 cm) |
| 1 strip | orange peel | |

**method**
1. The day before, brew the coffee, let it cool, pour it into the mould and freeze it overnight.
2. Put the rye, syrup, bitters and water in a mixing glass or a jar, fill it with ordinary ice and stir for about 30 seconds, until it's cold and the whiskey's burn has softened but not gone.
3. Put the coffee block in the glass and strain the drink over it.
4. Squeeze the peel over the top so its oils fall on the drink, then drop it in.
5. The first sips are the Old-Fashioned alone. As the block melts, a dark layer gathers around it; give the glass a slow swirl whenever you like, and the coffee comes into the drink.

**closingLine:** *Swirl it once the coffee starts to show. When you miss one, make the next move just as steadily.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*Rye, sugar, bitters, peel. The coffee is in the ice, and arrives later.*

**whoYouAre**
You turned down the offer everyone said you'd regret. Or you let the first chance go by, or gave up a point in an argument you could easily have won. People shook their heads, kindly, and it looked as if you'd got it wrong. You let it look that way. You couldn't prove it yet, and you won't promise what you can't show.

It costs you, too. Letting go of the sure thing never feels clever. It's a loss, and you carry it, saying nothing, until the move plays out. You don't mind looking as if you've got it wrong for a while. What you mind is the move you didn't see, after people trusted you to have read it all.

It gives something to whoever is with you. When everyone wants the quick win, you can sit through the slow one, and everyone else can stop panicking. That steadiness is a kindness, even on the days it gets called stubbornness.

**yours**

1. By the late 1990s, Cedd Moses had worn himself out at work and sold his company. He turned to property. Going out to bars and restaurants in Los Angeles had left him in love with the city's rundown old centre, which, in the history that tells his story, was dead. He says he went downtown to open bars and bring the area and the city's cocktails back, out of pride in the city and a love for its old buildings. He started buying property there while it was cheap. "Everyone at the time thought I was insane," he remembers.

2. His first bar was Golden Gopher, but the same history says it was a later one, Seven Grand, that made the difference: mostly a whiskey bar, where a lot of promising bartenders worked. Chuck Taggart, an early cocktail lover in the city, called its opening the big turning point, "to me": it wasn't the first, he said, but it seemed to set off so many others. Moses kept opening bars after that.

3. We don't know how it felt to him while he was buying. I like to think the buying didn't feel clever then, more like a price paid early, with nothing yet to show for it and everyone shaking their heads. That's the piece of this story I'd hand you: the move made before it could be judged, the stretch of looking wrong, and a first try that came before the one that made the difference. I like the size of the ending, too. We don't know whether he ever got everything he went downtown for. The record is smaller: a history that says Seven Grand made the difference, and one man who calls it, "to me", the turning point. I suspect you trust that ending more than a triumphant one.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a famous classic, the Old-Fashioned, built strong on a barrel-proof rye, because Seven Grand was a whiskey bar (the rye is my choice). It's sweetened with demerara, seasoned with Angostura, stirred cold, poured over one block of frozen black coffee, with the oil of an orange peel added last. Anyone who knows an Old-Fashioned may think I've got the ice wrong. The first sips are the Old-Fashioned alone, strong and good on their own. Then, as the block melts, the coffee gathers in a dark ring around it, and one slow swirl brings it in, its roast meeting the toasted side of the grain. The coffee is in the ice because of those years downtown: the move is made first, and nobody can taste it yet.

5. Now, the thing I'd want you to hear. One day there'll be a trap you don't see. Not because you weren't paying attention: you were, more closely than anyone at the table. Some things can't be read from where anyone is sitting. When it happens, it doesn't mean the people who doubted you were right. Take that loss as plainly as you take the ones you choose, and carry on. You'll have lost a piece you didn't mean to give. You'll still have everything that made you good at this.
