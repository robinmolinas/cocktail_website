## whoYouAre

When you love something, people hear about it. A person, a band, a team, the town you grew up in: it gets all of you, out loud, usually before anyone has asked. You're the one who makes the playlist, writes the birthday card that runs onto the back, and says the big words before anyone else dares to.

People assume you don't notice how it looks. You do. Nobody knows the pause after the big words better than you: the half-second before the room decides whether to smile with you or at you. You step into it regardless. The part you hide is the walk home afterwards, going back over the one face that smirked. A no, you'd survive. Being laughed at for meaning it is what stings, because it tempts you to say it a little smaller next time. Still, there's a gift in it: everyone you love has proof. Most people go their whole lives without being loved out loud, in front of others. Whoever you love never has to wonder.

## yours

Manhattan had a cocktail, and so did the Bronx, so for well over a century Brooklyn has wanted one of its own. The earliest on record is from 1883, a rum Manhattan made at the Brooklyn Club, and it apparently never left the club. In 1908 Jack Grohusko, a Manhattan bartender, printed a Brooklyn in the book he wrote. He never lived in Brooklyn. The man who owned his restaurant did.

In the summer of 1910, the papers reported, a man living in Brooklyn couldn't sleep, so he got up and mixed a new one: a long glass of cider, absinthe and ginger ale. Asked what excuse he had for calling a pint a cocktail, Maurice Hegeman said there was no law about it, and that he wanted Brooklyn to have a cocktail people would recognise it by. That September, papers from Kansas City to Washington printed another Brooklyn, someone else's, offered with a tribute so flowery the bartenders couldn't resist. One of the Waldorf-Astoria's bartenders said all it was missing was the ice cream. Another said that if he lived in Brooklyn, he'd stick to beer.

Brooklyn kept at it. Less than two months after Prohibition ended, a reader wrote to the *Brooklyn Eagle*: there's a Manhattan and a Bronx, so why not a Brooklyn? Recipes came in for weeks. In 1945 the Bronx borough president teased that Brooklyn still had no cocktail, and offered one made with vinegar and DDT. Brooklyn answered so fast that within days the *Eagle*'s columnist heard thirty-three recipes in a single day, and gave up on the subject because more kept coming. Bartenders today are still naming new ones after Brooklyn's neighbourhoods.

That, to me, is you. Not the winning, because no Brooklyn has ever really stuck. The not stopping. I like to think Hegeman knew exactly how a pint of cider sounded when he called it a cocktail, and said it plainly because he meant it. Each time someone laughed, somebody else in Brooklyn picked it up, at full size.

The cocktail I've made for you is a close relation of the Manhattan, one of the world's best-known cocktails. It starts from the Brooklyn Club's: aged rum with sweet vermouth, the version that apparently never left the room. Where Grohusko's had a little bitter orange aperitif, mine has a spoonful of a dark Italian amaro that tastes of burnt orange. It's stirred cold, rich and warm from the rum, with a bitter-orange edge, poured into an ice-cold cocktail glass. I've added one thing, and it comes last: a wide orange peel, squeezed high over the top, so the orange is the first thing anyone meets. As a nod to you, the Brooklyn that stayed indoors is the one that now announces itself.

Here's my proposal. Whenever you say the big words and somebody laughs, don't take them back, and don't trim them for next time. Same words, same size. Somebody usually joins in. In Brooklyn, it was thirty-three recipes in a day.

## Resonance

**As the guest.** The line that lands is *"Nobody knows the pause after the big words better than you"*, with *"People assume you don't notice how it looks. You do"* just before it. It answers the jibe this guest has heard all their life (naive) and names the private half they never show (the walk home). The gift is said plainly, once: *"everyone you love has proof."* The proposal gives something back: company, and a laugh at the thirty-three recipes.

**As a sceptic.** Is the whoYouAre true of anyone who's ever been teased? The playlist, the card that runs onto the back and the big words said first pin it to this person, and the fear is ridicule, not hurt, which keeps it off *Wide Open* (no hurt, no reply timing, no "careful"). Is the Brooklyn a stretch for love of a person? It's love for a place, so it holds for any guest, and Hegeman's own words carry the motive. Does the story pity the guest? No: it never says "unreturned". The beat is the not stopping, and the payoff is the joining in, not a vindication (the Bronx man's retraction stays out).

**Guards checked:** no "careful", "hurt", "reply", "anyway", "known by", "fall for", "first, then everyone", "say it again" as a position phrase (*sage-outlaw* owns "say it again, with more of it"; none left in the reading). The jokes belong to the bartenders, and the papers only printed them. Grohusko and Baracca stand side by side with no "for". The 1883 drink is "on record" and "apparently". No "first Brooklyn" for Grohusko. The lawyer is unnamed. Hegeman's drink is never the one laughed at ("someone else's").
