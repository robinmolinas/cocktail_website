# Pour — The Big Words · The Teenage Heart-throb (innocent-lover)

## Cocktail

- **name:** The Big Words
- **tagline:** You've never loved anything a little.
- **glassware:** a cocktail glass (about 180 ml), chilled
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 ml | aged Jamaican rum, about 50% (any aged Jamaican rum from 40% to 57% works; not spiced) | the style and strength of the rum in the Brooklyn Club's drink, as the *Eagle* printed it in 1945 |
| 30 ml | sweet vermouth (Italian style) | the club poured as much vermouth as rum; at half the rum the drink stays bright |
| 7.5 ml | Amaro CioCiaro (*recommended*), or any dark, bittersweet Italian amaro (herbal liqueur) with a burnt-orange taste, about 30% | in place of the 1908 Brooklyn's French orange bitter, Amer Picon; the stand-in Oxford and *Imbibe!* both name |
| 1 dash | Angostura bitters | the club's dash |
| 1 | wide strip of peel from a fresh, thick-skinned orange, about 2.5 cm across | squeezed high over the top, then dropped in |

**method**
1. Put the cocktail glass in the freezer for 10 minutes, or fill it with ice and water while you mix.
2. With a small knife, cut a wide strip of peel from a fresh, thick-skinned orange, about 2.5 cm across, leaving a little of the white underneath so it holds its shape.
3. Fill a mixing glass or a jar with ice. Pour in the rum, the vermouth, the CioCiaro and the dash of bitters.
4. Stir for about 30 seconds, until the outside of the glass is very cold.
5. Empty the cocktail glass and strain the drink into it.
6. Hold the peel by its edges, orange side down, high above the glass (about a hand's width), and squeeze it sharply so its oils fall in a fine spray over the drink.
7. Rub the orange side around the rim and drop the peel in.

**closingLine:** *Squeeze the peel from high up, so nobody misses it. If they laugh, keep the words the same size.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*Manhattan has a cocktail. So does the Bronx. Brooklyn is still asking.*

**whoYouAre**
When you love something, people hear about it. A person, a band, a team, the town you grew up in: it gets all of you, out loud, usually before anyone has asked. You're the one who makes the playlist, writes the birthday card that runs onto the back, and says the big words before anyone else dares to.

People assume you don't notice how it looks. You do. Nobody knows the pause after the big words better than you: the half-second before the room decides whether to smile with you or at you. You step into it regardless. The part you hide is the walk home afterwards, going back over the one face that smirked. A no, you'd survive. Being laughed at for meaning it is what stings, because it tempts you to say it a little smaller next time. Still, there's a gift in it: everyone you love has proof. Most people go their whole lives without being loved out loud, in front of others. Whoever you love never has to wonder.

**yours**

1. Manhattan had a cocktail, and so did the Bronx, so for well over a century Brooklyn has wanted one of its own. The earliest on record is from 1883, a rum Manhattan made at the Brooklyn Club, and it apparently never left the club. In 1908 Jack Grohusko, a Manhattan bartender, printed a Brooklyn in the book he wrote. He never lived in Brooklyn. The man who owned his restaurant did.

2. In the summer of 1910, the papers reported, a man living in Brooklyn couldn't sleep, so he got up and mixed a new one: a long glass of cider, absinthe and ginger ale. Asked what excuse he had for calling a pint a cocktail, Maurice Hegeman said there was no law about it, and that he wanted Brooklyn to have a cocktail people would recognise it by. That September, papers from Kansas City to Washington printed another Brooklyn, someone else's, offered with a very flowery tribute, and bartenders had their fun with its recipe. One of the Waldorf-Astoria's bartenders said all it was missing was the ice cream. A bartender far from Broadway said that if he lived in Brooklyn, he'd stick to beer.

3. Brooklyn kept at it. Less than two months after Prohibition ended, a reader wrote to the *Brooklyn Eagle*: there's a Manhattan and a Bronx, so why not a Brooklyn? Recipes came in for weeks. In 1945 the Bronx borough president teased that Brooklyn still had no cocktail, and offered one made with vinegar and DDT. Brooklyn answered so fast that within days the *Eagle*'s columnist heard thirty-three recipes in a single day, and gave up on the subject because more kept coming. Bartenders today are still naming new ones after Brooklyn's neighbourhoods.

4. That, to me, is you. Not the winning, because no Brooklyn has ever really stuck. The not stopping. I like to think Hegeman knew exactly how a pint of cider and ginger ale sounded when he called it a cocktail, and said it plainly because he meant it. When someone laughed, sooner or later somebody else in Brooklyn picked it up, at full size.

5. The cocktail I've made for you is a close relation of the Manhattan, that renowned classic so many other cocktails grew from. It starts from the Brooklyn Club's drink as the *Brooklyn Eagle* printed it in 1945: strong Jamaican rum with Italian vermouth, plus Angostura bitters, just one dash. The club poured equal measures of the two; mine is an aged Jamaican rum, two parts to one of vermouth, so it stays bright. Where Grohusko's had a little French bitter made from orange peel, mine has a spoonful of a dark, bittersweet Italian liqueur that tastes of burnt orange. It's stirred cold, rich and warm from the rum, with a bitter-orange edge, poured into an ice-cold cocktail glass. The last touch is mine too: a wide orange peel, squeezed high over the top, so the orange arrives before anything else. As a nod to you, a drink built on the club's quiet Brooklyn now announces itself.

6. Here's my proposal. Whenever you say the big words and somebody laughs, don't take them back, and don't trim them for next time. Same words, same size. Somebody usually joins in. In Brooklyn, it was thirty-three recipes in a day.

**Conflicts** (fact card)

- **C1 "First".** Oxford GROHUSKO (pdf 952) says *Jack's Manual* "marks the first appearance of the Brooklyn Cocktail"; Oxford BROOKLYN (pdf 360, same author) puts the earliest on record in 1883; *Imbibe!* pdf 211 calls Grohusko's "one of the very first"; and a 1945 reader-facing column recorded the 1883 drink (F17). **Our reading (inference):** Grohusko's is the first *printed recipe* known to Oxford. **Guest text:** never "the first Brooklyn" for Grohusko. Safe: "one of the first", or "in his own book, in 1908".
- **C2 Gage & Tollner's Brooklyn.** 1934: gin, grapefruit, grenadine (Sunshine Special, F12); 1945: two-thirds Jamaica rum, one-third lime, grenadine, "introduced in 1938" by "the late Seth Bradford Dewey" (*Eagle* 10 Dec 1945); also lime, "hot rum" and grenadine "after the last war" (13 Dec 1945). No recipe or year for it.
- **C3 The 1910 lawyer.** "Until yesterday Brooklyn struggled along without a cocktail named in its honor" (Sept 1910), two months after Hegeman's (July 1910) and two years after Grohusko's. The papers didn't know or didn't care: never "the first" for either 1910 drink.
