📜 **Hester:** Wren, the papers turn out to be better than Oxford's one passive sentence, and they give your test names and words (I spent the pour's two web visits on them, through the 1910–1945 press; card `fact-cards/brooklyn-cocktail.md`, anchors and OCR in our folder). **Laughed at, on the page:** in September 1910 a lawyer's Brooklyn, offered with a tribute (it fit the throat "like a velvet flame"), was reprinted from Kansas City to Washington with the bartenders' verdicts: at the Waldorf, "All he forgot was the ice cream"; another said that if he lived in Brooklyn he'd stick to beer (*Washington Post*, 6 Sep 1910, from the *Kansas City Star*). So the jokes were the bartenders', and the papers printed them. Please don't say "the papers laughed". **The person, in his own words:** two months earlier Maurice Hegeman, a Cincinnati man living in Brooklyn, couldn't sleep, thought up a Brooklyn and got up to make it. Asked his excuse for calling a pint a cocktail, he said there's "no law about it. And I wanted Brooklyn to be known by a cocktail" (*Cincinnati Enquirer*, 7 Jul 1910, from the *New York Telegraph*). That's a motive on the record, so "he said" can carry it. It's also your one true thing: he heard how it sounded and said it straight. Paraphrase "known by", because *Before the Room* has "known by his name". **Said again:** after Repeal it was a reader who asked first ("Why not a Brooklyn Cocktail?", *Eagle*, 28 Jan 1934), and recipes came in for weeks. In 1945 the Bronx borough president's vinegar-and-DDT recipe ("good enough for … Dem Bums") got its answer within days: a hotel challenged him to a contest, and the columnist listened to 33 recipes in a day and then gave up on the subject because of "the swelling tide of recipes". The Bronx man then conceded in writing. That's vindication, the Naive Artist's ground, so leave it out unless you want it. **Your two asks.** Baracca: it's true (pdf 952), but only as two plain facts side by side. No page says Grohusko named the drink for Baracca, so either sign that link with "I like to think", or set the facts side by side and let the guest draw it. Don't call it "the first Brooklyn in print": Oxford gives the 1883 drink as earliest on record (pdf 360), and *Imbibe!* says "one of the very first" (C1). Also, Grohusko's own drink was praised, not mocked. **Fences:** a paper crowned a Brooklyn in December 1934 and it still didn't catch on, so never say "nobody chose one". Some of the 1945 answers were politics and publicity, so "nothing to gain" stays out. The lawyer stays unnamed (his name leads to a will case). Tomás: the Brooklyn Club's drink now has a recipe on record, as the *Eagle* printed it in 1945: half 100-proof Jamaica rum, half Italian vermouth, a dash of Angostura. Their bartender said he "doesn't get too many calls" for it. Call it "as served in 1945", never "the 1883 recipe". Tying it to the opening of the Brooklyn Bridge is the club's own story, so label it as legend. Hegeman's was cider, absinthe and ginger ale, in case you want it for the top. Absinthe is *Rosetta*'s base, and the family plan holds cider-with-rum for a sibling.
