# Persona read: The Teenage Heart-throb (innocent-lover)

Wren, round 1, 2026-10-04. Sources: `persona.py innocent-lover` (essence, story, goal Safety, fear Punishment, drivers Seek paradise / Build connection, imagery: handwritten notes, phone-camera authenticity, fans and affection); the family plan row (`_studio/plans/innocent.md`); Oxford BROOKLYN COCKTAIL pdf 360–361 and GROHUSKO pdf 952, PORTA MINGOT pdf 1552, all read whole on the page this round; the registry and the sibling pours, Wide Open (lover-innocent) above all.

## Who this is

They love completely and out loud, in front of people, before they know whether it's cool. They don't only feel it, they make something of it and hand it over: the note, the playlist, the toast, the big words said first. Whatever they love gets the whole volume: a person, a band, a team, a city.

The persona sheet calls it brave, and it is, but the person wouldn't call it that. To them it's simply how loving works. Doing it halfway isn't a decision they turned down. It never occurred to them.

## The one true thing (what no sibling has)

**They step into the half-second after the big words (the pause before anyone answers) more often than anyone, and they know exactly how it looks.** People take them for naive. They aren't. They know the room might smile the wrong way, and they say it anyway (word owned by *Anyway*: never in the reading).

Their sharpest dread isn't a no. It's being laughed at for meaning it: the "aw, that's sweet" said in the wrong tone, being called silly. (Note to self: don't build this on the Trickster's "What scares you isn't X. It's Y" frame, which is mine. Find another way in.)

## Hide / fear / give

- **Hide:** the aftermath. The hot face on the way home, going back over the moment someone smirked, the night spent wondering whether it was too much. They show the declaration and keep the replay to themselves. They also hide that they're not naive: they saw the risk of looking foolish and stepped into it.
- **Fear:** punishment for feeling (the Innocent's fear, Punishment, which in this person is ridicule): being laughed at, and then the slow turning-down that follows, so that the next declaration comes out smaller. It's a fear of ridicule, not of heartbreak: that's how this person stays apart from the Pure Heart.
- **Give:** the people they love have proof. They get the note, the playlist, the toast in front of everyone, the thing named after them. Most people go through life without ever being loved out loud in public. Anyone this guest loves gets to be. (Robin's rule: say the kindness out loud, once.)

## The position (where y5 should land, to be drafted in round 3)

When it gets laughed at, say it again. Not louder to win the argument, and not as a joke to get in before the laugh: the same words, a second time. The Brooklyn story *is* that (1908, the papers, Repeal, 1945, today). The proposal has to point to life, not the glass, and end on what they gain.

## Traps

1. **Wide Open's ground** (above): hurt, reply speed, "careful".
2. **Infantilising.** The name says "Teenage", but the guest could be any age. Never "teenager", "crush", "puppy love", "cute", "fifteen". It's first love's *intensity*, every time.
3. **Romance required.** The love can be for a person, a place, a team or a band. Never assume a partner.
4. **Us calling it silly.** "Silly", "ridiculous" and "too much" only ever come out of someone else's mouth, never the bartender's.
5. **Pity.** "A love nobody returned" is the plan's shorthand. In the reading it would cast the guest as the one left on read. The telling honours the saying, not the outcome.
6. **Seduction and celebrity.** No Bieber, no heart-throb-as-object, nothing sexual.
7. **Rivalry as the point.** Oxford gives rivalry with the Manhattan and the Bronx as the one motive the Brooklyns share. It gets one light clause. Never a contest, and never "Manhattan won".
8. **Formula.** No "Among your friends…", no "What scares you isn't… It's…".

## My story test

Someone who said it **out loud and in public** (in print, a name given, a dedication) about a person, place or team they loved; **laughed at or dismissed on the page**; **and said it again after the laughing**, by themselves or by others who picked it up. The telling honours the saying, whether or not it came back. Out: seduction, scandal, obsession, death for love, marriage as the prize, anyone made ridiculous, vindication as the payoff.

## Verdict on the plan's lead: **accept, on conditions**

**The Brooklyn Cocktail.** I read Oxford pdf 360–361 whole, plus Grohusko (pdf 952). It passes, and on the clause that matters most: the drinks were "invented, christened as Brooklyns, and floated in the pages of American newspapers, only to be ridiculed, dismissed, and forgotten", and then people said it again: after Repeal (the *Eagle*'s call for submissions), in 1945 (taunted by the Bronx borough president), and today (new riffs named after the neighbourhoods, "back to square one"). It's public, in print, laughed at, and never caught on, and nobody stopped saying it. That's the Heart-throb refusing to turn it down, and it's the opposite of *Wide Open*'s one private, lifelong yes. Love for a place also holds for any guest.

What the plan didn't use, from the page:
- **Grohusko never lived in Brooklyn; the owner of the restaurant he worked in, Victor Baracca, did** (Oxford GROHUSKO pdf 952). The first Brooklyn in print was named for somebody else's home. It's the most human clause in the story. Hester rules on it. No motive may be claimed (don't say "for his boss", which could also read as currying favour); one plain clause, with any meaning signposted.
- **The first one on record (1883) "apparently never left the hushed precincts of the Brooklyn Club."** The first declaration stayed in the room. That's the drink's tie for Tomás: the plan already builds on the 1883 rum Manhattan, so the cocktail made for this guest is the one that leaves the room. Keep "apparently" as Oxford's hedge.

**Conditions:**
1. The reading frames the story as *said again after the laughing*, never as "a love nobody returned". No pity, no "unrequited", no "never loved back".
2. Rivalry gets one light clause, as wanting their borough to have its own drink. Never claim "nothing to gain" as fact: the reading claims the saying, not the selflessness.
3. "Ridiculed" belongs to the drinks in the papers. The bartender never calls them silly, and the Bronx taunt stays fun.
4. Out: Craddock/Straub (blame), Amer Picon as a story (*Till Spring*'s), the named neighbourhood riffs (Red Hook: the Lover family's and the Magician backup's), maraschino (`nuts`, and the story doesn't need it), and any claim that the Brooklyn finally caught on.
5. Grohusko's Baracca clause (above) is used only if Hester clears it, with no motive.
6. The drink says it out loud: the spark (Tomás's call) should be something that's met first and isn't hidden in the base: on the top, on the nose, outside the glass. The 1883 "never left the room" gives the tie in one clause.
7. Every Wide Open guard above.

Since I accept, Tomás builds drink v1 in round 2 (Martini family · aged rum · chilled cocktail glass, lemon peel, per the plan's direction).

**Backup (Porta Mingot):** weaker than the plan says. The dedications to football teams are real fandom (pdf 1552), but nobody on the page laughs at them, nothing was said a second time, and the entry's Piña Colada claim and death are fences. It's a fair reserve, not a rival.
