# The Flirt (lover-jester): Wren's read, round 1

## Who this is
The Flirt turns connection into a game: a look, a tease, a joke held a moment too long. The persona sheet says what the game is for: "a way of reaching people without risking too much". It also names the dread: "the room where nobody plays back".

**The one true thing (no sibling has it):** the teasing is how they pay compliments. "You're wonderful" is too exposed to say straight, so they say it sideways ("that jumper is a crime", "you would order that") and watch to see if the other person catches it. A tease that comes back means the compliment was understood. Played back, it turns into a conversation that belongs to the two of them.

- **Hides:** there's real liking under every tease, more than the wink lets on. They keep it light so a no costs nobody anything, and that includes them. The cost is that a lot of people never find out how much they're liked.
- **Fears:** not a no, because a no is still an answer. They fear the flat return: someone who takes the tease at face value and looks hurt, or who smiles politely and leaves it there. The joke is left alone with them in the air. (This is never "being ignored", which is the Trickster's word, and never "nobody gets you back", which is the Prankster's.)
- **Gives (said out loud, Robin's rule):** being teased by them feels like being picked. People walk away feeling a bit more attractive and a bit funnier than when they came in, because someone thought they were worth the game.
- **Second hidden fear, which the story mirrors:** that when they finally say the real thing plainly, they'll get it slightly wrong.

## Keeping it apart from the siblings and neighbours
- **Prankster (*Got You*):** owns jokes as initiation ("the day you got them", "People feel claimed", "if nobody ever gets you back") and "a serious look held a beat too long". The Flirt's play isn't a setup with a punchline. It's a back-and-forth, and what's inside it is liking, not belonging.
- **Sex Symbol / ruler-lover:** owns attention given on purpose, "charm", and "it works because you mean it". Keep "mean it" and "charm" out of the key lines.
- **Muse (*In Your Own Hand*):** owns "put your name on it" (their own name). The Flirt's position must not be naming things.
- **Diva (*Say So*):** owns the closing line "Make the joke yourself, then ask for it anyway". The proposal can't be "joke, then ask".
- **Subverter (*With the Bite In*):** owns "Said straight, they'd stop you". Avoid "straight" as the hinge word.
- **Charismatic Leader:** owns the "It's also…" tagline shape. **Geisha:** prepared and waiting, where the Flirt is live and quick. **Romantic:** says it straight and at length, which is exactly what the Flirt can't do. **Bad Influence:** owns dares. **Charmer:** pleases without needing a reply. The Flirt needs the reply.
- **Wise Fool (*In Good Part*):** its epigraph is "…It was pink." So **pink can't go in our epigraph or a name**. The reading may quote Harrington's "a beautiful pink color" once, in the story.
- **Rule Breaker** has "twenty seconds" in its closing line, so no name built on "Twenty Seconds".

## Traps
Conquest, scoring, crude talk, and anything that sexualises the guest. A partner or romance must never be required: the story is a friendship, and the reading works for a guest who flirts with their barista, their colleagues or their gran. No "you're a tease" as a label. The guest should never come away feeling insincere: the play is honest, it's only coded.

## Story test
A real playful exchange where the play was answered (someone played back), and a sincere thing was slipped inside it: given, a little clumsy, and kept anyway.

## Verdict on the plan's lead (the Jasmine, Proper p. 58): accept, with conditions
I re-read Proper pp. 54–58 in full, and Oxford HARRINGTON (pdf 971) and THE JASMINE (pdf 1099). **The sources conflict.**
- **Proper p. 58:** Matt Jasmin is a friend from architecture school. He said "Make me something you've never made before", and he's the one who said "You just invented grapefruit juice". Year 1990.
- **Oxford JASMINE (Paul Clarke, from emails with Harrington, 2015–16):** "mid-1990s". Jasmin is "a customer" who came in after a shift at Chez Panisse. The grapefruit line came from **Evan Shively, the second person to taste it**: "Congratulations—you've invented grapefruit juice."
- **Oxford HARRINGTON** says 1990. So Oxford disagrees with itself on the year.

Conditions:
1. **Hester settles the conflict and we don't state the contested details** (rulebook). We don't say who teased, and we don't give the year. Both sources agree on something safe: one of the first people to taste it told Harrington he'd just invented grapefruit juice. "A friend" stands on Proper, and Hester decides whether Oxford's "customer" undercuts it. The request "make me something you've never made before" is Proper's alone, and on the record. The plan's "dared" is shorthand: on the page it's a request. Tell it as an invitation, never a dare.
2. **The misspelling carries the whole meaning.** He named the drink after his friend, which is the plain compliment, out loud, and he got one letter wrong for years. "By then, it was too late." The name went everywhere anyway. This is the "I like to think" mirror for the second fear. Keep it light. We never say Jasmin minded, or didn't, because the page doesn't say.
3. **The Pegu lineage gets one owned clause or nothing** (*Making the Calls*). The D. B. Cooper line, the Negroni-as-a-shot trick (p. 55) and the move to architecture all stay out.
4. **Tomás:** no grapefruit in the glass (the Muse's, and the Prankster's). A spark is welcome only if it's something the first taster would have teased back. The classic is defensible here, because twenty seconds' play is the point, but the drink still has to be interesting (Robin 2026-09-30).

## Position I'm aiming for (my words; the closing line is Tomás's)
Once, with one person, let the compliment out of the tease: say the nice thing plainly, even if it comes out a letter wrong. In the guest's terms, what they gain is someone finding out how much they're liked, and probably playing back.

## "This is me" line (draft)
*Every time you tease someone, you're paying them a compliment. Only the ones who play back can read it.*
