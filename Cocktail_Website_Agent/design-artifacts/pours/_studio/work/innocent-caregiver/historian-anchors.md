| ingredient | Sherry barrels for fino aren't filled to the top, and in the space left, a layer of yeast called flor grows on the young wine: a protective barrier that keeps the air off it (F1, F13, C1; Codex p. 245, sherry.wine). | gesture | There's always air at the top of the barrel, and a little more comes through the wood; the flor sits between it and the wine and uses up most of the oxygen itself, so the wine stays protected (F13; Luyten "consuming most of it"; F5 scoped: the Consejo's breathing sentence is about oxygen from moving the wine). | effort, not being used up: the air that would change the young wine reaches the flor first (our reading, signposted) | it gets to you first, and by the time it reaches them it's smaller |
| lineage | Its work stays in the wine: the Codex credits the flor with fino's dry, yeasty character, and much of its dryness comes from its years under the flor, and the flor makes most of the compound behind its almond aroma and its sharp nose (F8, F14, F15; Codex p. 247, *Molecules* 2022, sherry.wine). Never "bread" (G5) or salt (fino's own, Codex p. 245) as the flor's; never apple (G3). | what you gave goes on with them | some of them are calm now and couldn't tell you where they got it |
| watch | It stays over the wine for years: every sherry ages at least two years, and a five-year-old fino was sampled still under an intense white film (F6 all sherry, F7; no fixed fino term, C2). | a watch kept over time, not one rescue: every day, for years | "the dark got smaller" |
| drink | The Bamboo: sherry at the core and "the vermouths surround the sherry" (Codex p. 91, printed with amontillado); *Imbibe!* pdf 210 says "a fino or an amontillado"; on record by 1886 in St. Paul; a staple by 1900. Origin contested (Boothby/Eppinger, Wondrich's hedge, Codex's Japan): none in the reading. Fino "can be lost" among spirits (Codex p. 246). | the young one kept at the centre, with nothing loud around it | (Tomás's build) |
