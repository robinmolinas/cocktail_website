## whoYouAre

Little things add up for you. The friend who lights up whenever baking comes up. The job advert you know would suit someone perfectly. The two people at a party who keep ending up talking to each other. Put enough of them together and you can see how something could turn out, well ahead of everyone else. To you, that isn't wishing. It's where the signs point.

But you learned early to keep it quiet. "Don't get your hopes up" was nearly always said kindly, by people who loved you. So now every hope comes with a little "probably not" in front of it, said first, to beat everyone else to it. The disappointment you can carry. What you dread is the look afterwards, and worse, someone telling you that you got their hopes up.

And still you don't stop. When a person close to you puts a hope down, you quietly pick it up and keep it for them until they're ready to have it back. Most people just tell them to be realistic. What you do is rarer, and it matters more than you know.

## yours

1. Tasmania, the island south of mainland Australia, owes its whisky today to a fishing trip. Bill Lark and his father-in-law were sitting up in the highlands, talking over what the island already had: some of the best water in the world, as they saw it, good barley, and a climate that seemed fine for whisky. And nobody was making any. Then Bill's wife, Lyn, arrived, heard the idea and said, more or less, that they should try it. Neither Bill nor Lyn had ever distilled.

2. What stood in the way was an old rule. Since 1901, Australian law had asked for stills so big that only large industrial firms could distil, and by then even the big whisky makers had stopped: Australians had turned to cheaper imports and away from their own brands. I like to think a whole country had been told not to get its hopes up about its whisky. In 1992 Bill persuaded the government to rewrite the rule, and, as he tells it, the politicians helped at once. Lyn and Bill were the first to get a licence.

3. Within two weeks of the licence, the phone rang at ten at night. It was John Grant, of the Glenfarclas distillery in Scotland, offering to help them make good whisky. His reason, as Bill remembers it: if somebody tastes whisky for the first time at their distillery, it should be a good first time. As others on the island started distilling, Bill decided he should help them too.

4. This is where you come in. The idea that day wasn't a wish. They'd looked at what was in front of them and added it up. The "no", when it came, wasn't a person at all, just a rule that had stood since 1901. And once the door was open, Bill held it for the ones coming after him, the way a distillery in Scotland had held it for him. That's what you do with other people's hopes. You keep them going until they can carry them again, and from where I stand, few kindnesses go further.

5. The cocktail I've made for you is a whisky highball, one of the great simple drinks, made still: a Tasmanian single malt, Lark's own if you can find it, with cold water over ice, for the water and barley they talked about that day. The last thing in is two teaspoons of Glenfarclas, the Scotch from the distillery whose call came at ten at night. It's there for the help that turns up before there's anything to show for it, and for the hopes you keep for other people.

6. So, a suggestion. Whenever you can see how something could go, for yourself or for someone close, drop the "probably not". Say what you've added up, plainly, and then find out what's really in the way. It's often smaller than the hope: an old rule, a form, one person nobody has thought to ask. And sometimes the first answer is a yes.

## Resonance

**This is me line:** *"So now every hope comes with a little 'probably not' in front of it, said first, to beat everyone else to it."* The tagline grows out of it.

**As the guest (the Dreamer).** Seen, not flattered: the hope is recognised as noticing, not optimism; the "probably not" habit is specific and a little funny; the fear (being blamed for someone else's hopes) is named gently; the gift is said out loud (keeping a hope for someone until they want it back). The position is in their interest and ends on what they gain (sometimes the first answer is a yes, the way Lyn's was).

**As the sceptic.** Barnum check: "little things add up" alone could fit anyone, so the three scenes do the work (all three are hopes for *other people*, which is this person, not the Free Spirit's questions). Siblings: no "told off" (Free Spirit), no "one day you stop" (Giggler), no out-loud-at-full-size (Heart-throb: the position is leave out the disclaimer, then find what's in the way, which is the Lark story's own shape), no catch-checking (Underdog). Cross-family: no "dream", "hoping", "hunch", "stopped hoping", "home", "near home". Lover-innocent's "says it back before you've finished" avoided in the ending. History share: y1–y3 are under half of yours (counted below in my turn).
