**method**

### Reading

**epigraph** In honour of a night when the bar could pour only soft drinks.

**whoYouAre**

You walk into a room and it shifts a little. People notice you, and some of them are wary before you've said a word. So you've learned to go slowly and speak softly, and let them find out for themselves.

People assume you were born gentle. You weren't. You decide it, again and again, and you've never told anyone how often. There's a temper in you, and you know just what it could do, most of all when someone is picking on a person who can't answer back. So you don't shout. You go and stand beside them, and the room settles. Here's the gift in it: when you're there, nobody has to fight.

And there's a fear you never mention: the day being strong isn't enough, when it's happening to someone who matters to you, and nothing you've got can stop it.

**yours**

1. In 1949, the California board that issued liquor licences ruled that the Black Cat Café in San Francisco was a "disorderly house". Its reason for that was who drank there: gay men and women met at the Black Cat. The bar's owner, Sol Stoumen, stood to lose his licence. He fought it with a lawyer. Morris Lowenthal argued that the Black Cat's customers had the right to gather there, and in 1951 the state's Supreme Court agreed: people behaving properly in a bar have a right to be served. A right, not a favour.

2. He went on serving them, and the state came back. One San Francisco writer counts the whole fight as fourteen years, and just before the bar's Halloween party in 1963, the licence was taken. The party went ahead without alcohol. One witness remembers hundreds of people still there at six in the morning, drinking only soft drinks and juices.

3. I like to think that night says more about Sol Stoumen than the court case does: the licence was his power, it was gone, and he stayed open for them and poured soft drinks. People rarely see that side of you. Not the strength. What you do once the strength has run out.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a classic, the Horse's Neck. In turn-of-the-century Atlantic City it was simply ginger ale with a long, long twist of lemon, and many liked theirs with a "stick" of rye or gin in it. A leading drinks reference calls cognac with ginger ale a "winter warmer", so cognac it is: a deeper, older one, which still comes through clearly underneath, even with a pale, dry ginger ale leading nearly three to one. Strength, there the whole time, never raised. The whole lemon is peeled in one unbroken spiral and hung down into the glass: with only ginger ale around it, that was once the whole drink. We don't know which soft drinks were poured at the Black Cat that night. The soft drink leads this glass for that night's sake.

5. There will be a day when being strong isn't enough: trouble you can't stop, happening to someone you'd stand in front of. Don't go home. Stay until morning, and give them whatever you've still got, even if all that's left is being there. It was never your strength they came to you for. It was you.

**closingLine** Take your time over the peel. When strength can't help, your time still can.

### Reading

**epigraph** Before anyone made it legally, a king's chamberlain was looking everywhere for whisky from this glen.

**whoYouAre**

Tell you no, and you'll make it anyway. Usually you'd already started. The best things you've made began without anyone's say-so, often on ground that wasn't yours, and they turned out better than what was allowed. A no has never stopped you. If anything, it shows you where to begin.

It's the yes you don't trust. When the people in charge finally want what you made, something in you goes cold, as if being let in were the first step to being owned. And underneath sits a question you've never quite asked anyone: is it good, or only good because it isn't allowed? What I see is this. Much of what everyone takes for granted now, someone like you made first, before anyone agreed it could be done. You show people what better looks like while they're still saying no, and it matters to far more people than ever say so.

**yours**

1. By 1822, the whisky made illegally in Glenlivet already had a name. When the king visited Edinburgh that year, a woman whose family sent some wrote later that his chamberlain was looking everywhere for it. Her father sent word to her at home to empty her own store, and it went to the palace. She remembered it as whisky that had waited a long time in the cask and then in the bottle, "mild as milk", with the real taste of contraband in it. As if being forbidden were part of the flavour.

2. Since at least 1815, one of Glenlivet's illegal distillers had been George Smith, a tenant of the Duke of Gordon. The duke had done much to promote a new law, in 1823, that encouraged distillers like him to "go legal", and he urged Smith to take out a licence. Smith did. In 1824 his became the first licensed distillery in Speyside, and his old colleagues threatened him.

3. I like to think Smith faced the question you carry: was it ever good, or only good because it was forbidden? What's on the record is what he did: he went on making it, in the open. Being let in didn't make it disappear. It made it the one others copied: within a few years, people were trading on its name, and within twenty, London was being sold his as "the Real Glenlivet".

4. The cocktail I've made for you starts from the Old-Fashioned, a renowned classic. The whisky is The Glenlivet 12 Year Old, from the distillery he licensed: a subtle, gentle malt. It's stirred cold over a single big cube with a teaspoon of sugar syrup and orange bitters, then finished with the oil from a lemon peel. The one addition is a tablespoon of elderflower liqueur, to bring up the flowers a well-known cocktail book already tastes in this whisky: "elegant and floral", it says. Nothing smoky, nothing raw: none of it tries to taste forbidden, as a nod to a whisky that never needed to be.

5. So, one proposal. Make something that isn't against anything: no rule in its way, nobody to prove wrong. Then show it to the people whose approval you've never wanted. If they want it, that isn't the moment you lose. It's the moment you find out it was good. And you'll still be the one who made it.

**closingLine** Stir it for twenty seconds, then leave it be. It holds up on its own.

### Reading

**epigraph** There's artichoke in this glass. Say so, and people pull a face. Then they taste it.

**whoYouAre**

The cold lake in March. The rope swing nobody else will try. The plan that sounded mad at dinner and is happening by morning. Your friends groan. Then they come, and it turns into the night they're still telling people about years later.

People think you're chasing the rush. It's plainer than that. In the second you let go, nobody is in charge of you: not the sensible voice, not whoever insists you need a course first, not the life that gets planned for you a little more each year. The drop is the one kind of helpless you choose. The other kind, padded and polite, where everything's decided and nothing's yours, frightens you more than any height. Said aloud, that would come out as a complaint, and you'd rather laugh.

And the laugh is what you give. You take what scares everyone and make it a game they can all be in. Around you, people remember they're allowed to feel alive. That's no small thing to give a person.

**yours**

1. On 1 April 1979, four friends from an Oxford club that called itself the Dangerous Sports Club gathered on the Clifton Suspension Bridge in Bristol, high over the river, and stepped off on elastic rope. Theirs were the first recorded bungee jumps. One of them, David Kirke, in top hat and tails, had covered his face so that his mother wouldn't recognise him in the papers next morning.

2. The rope hadn't been tested. Clever friends had worked out how far it would stretch, and Kirke said later that for a club with that name, testing it first "wouldn't have been particularly dangerous". What went through his mind as he went over, he said, was "Whoooppeeee". The police had at first taken it for an April Fool's joke. By Kirke's account, the police were very good-humoured about it. They arrested all four, and let them go with a small fine, on the understanding they'd never do it again. Six months later, Kirke and others from the club jumped from the Golden Gate Bridge.

3. A journalist who met Kirke wrote that the printed invitations had already gone out, so there was no backing out. Who posted them, we don't know. I like to think someone did it on purpose, closing the way back before anyone else could close it for them. That's you: not the jump, the invitations. Kirke once said the club was against "little, important bourgeois instructors" telling people they had to pass exams before they were allowed to try a sport. Not the danger. Who gets to decide. And years later he said he was glad his stunts had given people some fun.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a sour, one of the great classic shapes: sharp with lemon, sweet just enough. It starts from sloe gin, made by steeping sloes, small wild cousins of the plum, in gin with sugar. That turns it red and leaves it sweet already, so I add no syrup. I shake it hard with London dry gin and fresh lemon, and pour it into a coupe, chilled first. Then the surprise: a small measure of Cynar, an Italian bitter with artichoke among its many flavourings, and a reputation for being extremely bitter. A cookbook of surprising pairings puts cooked artichoke with plum, and here it gives the sweet sloe the bitter edge it needs. I put it in for you: you're the one who turns what makes people flinch into the part of the night everyone remembers.

5. So I'd never tell you to stop jumping. I'd point you at a bigger drop. It isn't off a bridge. It's the plan for your life that somebody sensible drew up, a little at a time, that you've half stopped noticing you never chose. You don't have to tear it up. Take one piece of it back, the way you'd step off an edge: decide, tell people, send the invitations, so there's no backing out. Kirke said life is very long without those brief moments. I'd add one thing. The long part can be yours too.

**closingLine** Strain it fine. Then send the invitations.

### Reading

**epigraph** In 1968, a non-drinker got himself arrested for one of the spirits in this glass.

**whoYouAre**

Someone sighs: it's a pity, but those are the rules. You've already stopped listening. You're working out which rule it is, who it's really for, and what it would take to get round it by Friday. Not for you. Usually it isn't even your fight.

People put it down to nerve, or a taste for trouble. It's neither. It's that you can't watch. Being told to wait, to go through the proper channels, to leave it to the people who run things while it carries on happening: that's the thing that truly frightens you. And there's a part you'd never say out loud: some of it is for you. Doing something, even something that costs you, is how you stop feeling helpless.

So you fix it, quietly, one person at a time: a rule bent just for them, a back way found. It works. What it gives people is real: with you around, "those are the rules" stops being the end of the matter, and that matters more than you let yourself think. But you know the other half. The rule is still there in the morning, and so is the next person it lands on.

**yours**

1. For decades, Nigeria's own spirit was against the law. Ogogoro is distilled from the sap of palm trees. In colonial Nigeria a treaty banned distilling spirits locally, while foreign spirits could still be sold, gin above all. People made it anyway, more and more of them, and it stayed illegal even after independence in 1960.

2. In 1968, a Nigerian educator called Tai Solarin got himself arrested for having some. He did it on purpose, to show everyone how unfair it was to ban a local spirit and allow foreign ones. That September, a Lagos paper's headline read "Solarin Freed of Gin Charge". The ban ended that same year. And Tai Solarin didn't drink.

3. How it went, we don't know. I like to think he simply made the rule do, where everyone could see, the one thing it couldn't defend. It was never his drink. He picked the fight up anyway, and made sure the charge landed on him. I recognise you in that. If putting it right has to cost somebody, you'd rather it cost you.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is built like a Martini, the classic that needs no introduction: stirred, ice-cold and strong, poured into a little stemmed glass, chilled first. It stands on two spirits in equal measure: Pedro's, a Nigerian ogogoro distilled from palm sap in villages and then again in Lagos, and a London dry gin. 5. So let me steer, just once. When something you started takes off, don't try to hold on to all of it. Decide what you'll keep steering yourself, and do it properly. Let the rest run in other people's hands. That isn't it getting away from you. It's how you'll know it was real. And it leaves you free for the next thing nobody has a name for yet.

**closingLine** Mash the quince until no lumps are left. Then pick what you'll steer yourself, and let the rest run.

**method**
1. Fill the glass with fresh ice and leave it to chill.

### Reading

**epigraph** One bartender made the case for this drink by admitting what was wrong with it.

**whoYouAre**

Someone says it's settled, and something in you sits up. It isn't that you think they're wrong. Often you think they're right. But nobody has argued with it yet, and an idea nobody has argued with hasn't been tested. It's only been agreed with. So you take the other side.

You'd hardly ever admit how often you agree, or how glad you'd be to lose to a good answer. "Only playing devil's advocate" keeps your own view safe, where nobody can argue it down. The sigh is what you dread, the "there they go again": once your questions stop counting, you're stuck with decisions nobody was allowed to think about.

Nobody says thank you in the moment. They should. An idea that's survived you can survive almost anything, and when the real objection comes, from a boss, say, the others already have the answer, because you made them find it.

**yours**

1. In the 1980s the Amaretto Sour was a standard. An importer had launched it in 1974 as two parts of the sweet Italian liqueur to one of lemon juice, but by then most bartenders reached for bought sour mix instead, and the drink tasted of little but the liqueur.

2. Then, in February 2012, a Portland bartender, Jeffrey Morgenthaler, posted on his website that he made the best Amaretto Sour in the world. It opens: "No, really. I'm serious." And before he made his case, he gave the other side its due. Other Amaretto Sours were too sweet, he wrote, and amaretto on its own is weak. "It needs help." His help was cask-strength bourbon. Some readers teased him, and one asked whether it was still an Amaretto Sour at all.

3. That's the move I recognise in you. He never pretended the old drink was good. He granted the point, then showed what the drink could be once it was answered. Two years later he tried the move from the other end, with a post called "Hot Toddies suck—long live the Hot Toddy", and a better way to keep one hot. And by 2021, the entry on the Amaretto Sour in the *Oxford Companion to Spirits and Cocktails* was his. I like to think the teasing pleased him: it meant someone was arguing back.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is an Amaretto Sour, a classic again, built on his fix: an Italian amaretto, cask-strength bourbon for backbone, fresh lemon, and lightly whipped egg white, a spoonful of it, shaken and strained over fresh ice, with lemon oil over its thin white foam. Then I argued with him, as you would. He said the others were too sweet, so I took him further than he went: no syrup at all, a little less lemon to match, and the amaretto doing all the sweetening. And there's sherry vinegar in it, half a teaspoon, an ingredient I'd have to defend in most bars, in honour of the side nobody takes. Vinegar is one of the best partners nuts have in the kitchen, and here it keeps the sweetness honest.

5. Here's my side, and I mean it. Keep testing everything: people are better for it, even the ones who sigh. But now and then, argue for something you actually love, and say plainly that it's what you think. No escape hatch, no "just for the sake of argument". Let them push back on you for a change. You may find your own ideas survive you too, and everyone else finally gets to argue with the real you.

**closingLine** Measure the vinegar, and be ready to defend it. Next time, let the view you defend be one you hold.
