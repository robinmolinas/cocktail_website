**epigraph** The vermouth in this glass is there because hardly anyone stopped after the first.

**whoYouAre**

The bill nobody can split, the call nobody wants to make, the mistake that was only half theirs: it's landed on someone near you. You don't rush. You weigh it up, quietly, and then you say it: "Put it on me." And they go home lighter. That's the kindness in you, and it counts for more than you'll ever let anyone say.

The other half is harder. Someone does you a small kindness, a lift, a meal, an evening of their time, and it's paid back within the week, with a little extra. Owing anyone, even briefly, feels like weakness to you, like leaving a door open that someone could walk through. So your books are always in credit. Plenty of people owe you something. You've never asked for any of it back.

**yours**

1. In 1919, months before national Prohibition began, a wartime law had already made selling drink illegal. That year, the Greenwich Village Inn in New York, which a man named Barney Gallant ran, was raided, and he was arrested along with half a dozen of his waiters. Barney agreed to plead guilty on one condition: that his waiters were let go.

2. He was sentenced to thirty days in jail, and he served far less. Yes, his name really was Gallant. You could say it was his place to answer for, and maybe it was simply good business. But I like to think it was more than that: that he looked at his waiters, who'd only been carrying the trays, and decided the cost was his.

3. You'd have made the same decision, and you'd have called it only fair. What I've always wondered is how it felt for those waiters, going home owing someone that much. You've stood where Barney stood more times than you can count. You've never let yourself stand where they did.

4. The cocktail I've made for you comes out of the old saloons, where people at the bar drank in rounds, taking turns to pay, and hardly anyone stopped after the first. A strong drink was too much for that, and one answer was to split the whisky half and half with vermouth, a wine flavoured with herbs: that's where the Manhattan, a renowned classic, and its family come from. So I kept the equal split, the way the Manhattan's early recipes often had it: rye and sweet vermouth, poured to the same line twice, with bitters, then let it run long over ice with soda and a twist of lemon. The rye dry, the vermouth dark and bittersweet, all of it cold and bright. I kept the split for what you won't allow yourself: something else carrying half the weight, so everyone can keep going.

5. So when someone does something for you, don't pay it back. No dinner in return, no bigger present, no "I'll get you back for this". Every time you square it straight away, you take something from them: the feeling you get every time you say "put it on me". Let them keep that. And for once, you get to be the one who's let off.

### Reading

**epigraph** This glass starts from a reader's letter to a New York paper, in 1934.

**whoYouAre**

When something matters to you and the arguing starts, you're rarely the loudest person there. Often you go quiet. The right words turn up later, on the way home, and you can lose sleep over the ones you didn't say. Losing an argument, you get over. Staying quiet when it really mattered, you don't.

Then you make something: a page, a song, a picture, a menu, a message that took all evening. In that, you say all of it, plainly, and you mean every word. Sometimes you wonder whether making things instead of arguing is just a nicer way of hiding. It isn't. An argument changes one mind, if you're lucky. What you make travels. It gives people the words they were missing, and room to join in without anyone being talked down to. That's your way of standing up for something, and it brings people together.

**yours**

1. In the autumn of 1933, with Prohibition about to end, the *Sun*, a New York newspaper, gave a writer named G. Selmer Fougner a daily series to help its readers find their way around legal wine and spirits. Wine writing then tended to be grand and mystifying, and cocktail writing jokey, even crude. Fougner was neither. He didn't talk down to anyone who liked a cocktail or a beer, and he answered readers' questions patiently, however silly. One wanted to know whether all scotch was really made in Australia.

2. When he had a view, he gave it, gently, usually. In 1936, when the grenadine sold in America was mostly imitation, he wrote that the reputable French makers used real pomegranate. And when he didn't know an answer, he put the question to readers all over the country, many of whom had been there when famous drinks began, and they wrote back. The column ran daily until he died in 1941.

3. This was his way of standing up for something, I like to think: not with a speech, but with a daily column, where nobody was laughed at for asking and anyone who knew better could say so. It's your way too. And look at what came back when he asked. More than one reader wrote to him about a single Boston drink, and one of those letters holds the recipe behind the cocktail I've made for you.

4. It's the Ward Eight, Boston's best-known cocktail, built from that reader's 1934 recipe: bourbon, lemon, grenadine and half an orange, squeezed, which he said many people preferred, over a rather large piece of ice in a heavy, round-bowled glass "of the type generally used for beer". Fresh mint goes in with the orange slices, pineapple and cherry he listed, in place of a mint liqueur. "This is an improvement," the reader wrote. He allowed plain water or seltzer; I chose the seltzer. His letter leaves the sugar to taste, so I left out the spoon, because the orange and the grenadine carry the sweetness. The grenadine is made from real pomegranate, for Fougner's column of 1936. Pomegranate's own aromas include mint, so the reader's improvement and the columnist's point share a scent. It's long, bright and tart, a hazy rosy orange, and strong: the reader called his "highly potent".

5. Somewhere there's a thing you made about what you believe, and never put out: the page, the song, the long message sitting in drafts. It's ready. But sending it is the argument, so it waits. Send it, where anyone can see it. Then ask people what they think, and mean the question. Some of the answers will surprise you, and you'll find you were never standing up for it alone.

**closingLine** Mint in last, the way the reader wrote it. Then see who writes back.

### Reading

**epigraph** Sixty cases of the pine in this glass were all one importer could afford.

**whoYouAre**

The medal's still round your neck, or the last exam's handed in, or the record you hunted for two years is finally on the shelf. Everyone's pleased for you. And by that evening you're quietly reading up on the next one.

People call it restless. It isn't, quite. What you dread is finished: a comfortable life that asks nothing of you, a map with nothing left on it. Going after something far away and hard to get is how you find out what you're made of, and once you know, you need something new to find out about. And you never return empty-handed. You turn up with things nobody near you could have got, and you make far away look like somewhere people can actually go. Everyone you know lives a bigger life because you went. No prize on your shelf is worth as much.

**yours**

1. Eric Seed was a student in Austria when he first came across the pine liqueur of the Alps, and he thought it would sell in Colorado's ski towns. One writer calls him perhaps the most mild-mannered person in the cocktail business, soft-spoken among loud characters, and then adds that there was "nothing safe or boring about his business". Seed went back to Austria to find a pine liqueur he could bring over, and returned with Zirbenz, which the Hofer family distillery has made since 1797.

2. He could afford one pallet: sixty cases. None of the big distributors were interested, so a one-man wine business carried it, sold as a drink for after skiing. It struggled, though in some places it did well, and a Denver bartender took it on. Years later Seed said the conifer "is hardly an essential bar ingredient". Back then, with that first bottle behind him, he walked into the best liquor store in Aspen and asked what they'd like to carry. That became his way of working. He asked bartenders on both coasts what he should bring over, and went and found it: one asked for Batavia arrack, a rum-like spirit from Indonesia. For a decade, by one writer's account, a visit from Seed to a cocktail bar was an occasion, and nobody knew what rare bottle he'd pull out of his bag.

3. The pine was never the whole point, I like to think. It took him somewhere, it showed him he could, and then came the question you know well: what now? As I see it, his way out was to stop waiting for something new to find him, and to ask other people what they'd like him to find. That way, the map never runs out. You've had that flat evening more times than you'd admit, and his way out is one you can borrow.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a toddy, the classic warm winter drink, for the end of a cold day. The pine goes in first, Zirbenz or any Alpine stone-pine liqueur, three parts to one of aged rum, a pairing its importer suggests for toddies. Then a teaspoon of allspice dram, the kind of bottle Seed might pull out of that bag. I added it for the find that comes next, so the first prize and the one after share a glass. A little lemon, a touch of brown sugar syrup, hot water, all in a glass mug warmed first, and last, lemon zest twisted above the steam, for its scent. It takes a reddish tint from the pine, and it smells of pine, with spice underneath.

5. So the next evening you arrive and it goes flat, don't go looking for a bigger mountain straight away. Ask a friend what they've stopped hoping to find: the book that's out of print, the song they half remember, the place they always meant to see. Then go and get it. You'll never be short of somewhere to go, and a friend gets the thing they'd given up on. I hope your list stays long.

**method**
1. The day before, make the broth: put about 1 kg of beef shin on the bone, one onion cut in half and one carrot in a large pan with 2 litres of cold water. Bring it slowly to a bare simmer and keep it there for about 3 hours, skimming off the grey foam as it rises. Strain through a fine sieve, add no salt, let it cool and keep it in the fridge (it keeps 3 days, or freeze it). Lift off the fat once it's cold.
2. If the broth has set to a soft jelly in the fridge, that's a good broth. Warm it just until it pours again, then let it cool to room temperature.
3. Fill a tall glass to the top with ice.

You rarely admit that the rules hold you up too, or that some nights you've agreed with every person who told you it was a terrible idea. What you guard against is wobbling in front of them. But look at what your rules give people: in their worst week, someone still expects something of them, so they still count. That's respect, and most people never think to offer it.

**yours**

1. After Hurricane Katrina, Neal Bodenheimer, then tending bar in New York, felt he should be back in New Orleans. Many people from the city who lived elsewhere felt that if they didn't go home, the city might not survive. So he came home. His idea was to be one of the first there with a cocktail bar standing on its own, not inside a hotel or a restaurant. He bought a building in Freret, a lonely neighbourhood far from the French Quarter, and his partner, Matt Kohnke, loved it. At least ten to twenty people tried to talk him out of it: a terrible idea, they said. Years later he admitted that at first they'd been a little nervous too, and that they couldn't afford to be anywhere else.

2. Cure opened in 2009. He handed the drinks over to his bartenders. What he did give the bar was a dress code: no shorts, no flip-flops, no baseball caps. It gave the place a reputation it never quite lived down. For the first nine months to a year, he said, they spent their time persuading people that they should drink cocktails, and that the bar wasn't just pretentious.

3. Then comes my favourite part. Years later, talking about Cure's first days, he didn't say the critics had been wrong. He said craft cocktails had partly earned that label, and that they really had to work on their service. So they did. Cure did well from the start, and later he and his partners opened more bars. I like to think the dress code was never really about shorts. In what he called, in a way, a decaying city, where people started over, it said: this place still expects something of you. That's you. And the rest is you at your best: he granted the part of the criticism that was fair, worked on it, and kept going.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Brandy Crusta, a New Orleans classic from the mid-1800s. It's famous for how it's dressed: its name means the crust the sugar makes around the rim, and a long lemon peel lines the glass like a collar. Inside go cognac, a teaspoon apiece of dry curaçao (orange liqueur) and syrup, Angostura, and lemon, just half a teaspoon, all stirred until icy and strained into a little wineglass from the fridge, no ice in it: deep amber and rich. My main change is in the cognac. It sits for twenty minutes with ordinary black tea, so the firm grip at the end comes from the tea, not from more lemon. I did that as a nod to you: firm, without ever raising your voice. By 1908, one New York newspaperman was already sneering at sugar rims as old-fashioned. I kept the crust anyway.

5. Next time you're keeping a standard and someone calls it pretentious, or too much, don't hear it as all or nothing. Find the part they're right about, admit it plainly, and fix that part. You'll worry it looks like wobbling. It doesn't. That's how the rest of what you stand for holds, and people will trust your rules more for it.

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 300 ml | white rum, light and dry (Spanish-style, unaged or lightly aged, 37.5-45%; not spiced, not sweetened) | the kind of rum that was there when the gin wasn't |
| 100 ml | fresh lime juice (about 3 to 4 limes), measured | |
| 50 g | caster sugar (granulated works, it takes longer) | dissolved in the lime, no syrup: rum, lime and sugar, nothing else |
| 4 | makrut lime leaves, fresh or frozen | lime's scent, without more sour; they come out before you pour |
| about 500 g | crushed ice (a bag of cubes in a tea towel, hit with a rolling pin) | |

**method**
1. Put six small tumblers in the freezer for 10 minutes.
2. Pour the lime juice into the pitcher, add the sugar, and stir until no grains are left on the bottom: about a minute.
3. Tear each makrut leaf in half and drop the halves into the lime. Press them against the bottom of the pitcher with a muddler or the end of a rolling pin, five times each, until they smell strongly of lime.
4. Add the rum.
5. Fill the pitcher about two-thirds with the crushed ice. Swizzle for 30 seconds by the clock: push a long spoon down to the bottom and work it up and down through the ice, turning it as you go.
6. Lift the leaves out with the spoon.
7. Pour straight away into the six tumblers, ice and all, along the row and back, so every glass gets the same.

**yours**

1. In the 1890s, Jennings Cox ran an iron-mining company near Daiquirí, a port town in the east of Cuba. The family story goes that he had some American visitors to serve, and found he'd run out of gin. He did have rum, from a distillery in Santiago, under thirty miles away. But he worried, the family says, that it wouldn't suit the Americans' taste.

2. So, in the family's telling, he picked limes from a tree in his own garden, added their juice and some sugar to soften the rum, and swizzled it all in a pitcher with shaved ice. It went down well at once, the accounts say. One of his engineers later remembered it as more of a group effort among Cox and his crew, and maybe it was. People in the Caribbean had been mixing rum, limes and sugar for two centuries by then, but the Daiquiri, a renowned classic today, goes back to him: he made the first one, or at least oversaw its making. And for years afterwards, the old newspaper accounts say, he simply called it his rum sour, and kept taking it to the bars of Santiago.

3. I like to think none of his visitors saw the worry the family remembered. They saw a man with a pitcher and a handful of limes. I recognise you in that: the answer arrives with a level face, and the doubt stays behind it. And then there's the name. He'd solved a problem with visitors to serve, and for years he went on handing the answer round as just his rum sour. Whether he enjoyed that day isn't known, but I like to think he did. You'd do the same: you hand the answer over plainly, and nobody hears how much you loved finding it.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is built like that pitcher, for six: a Daiquiri in the shape the family story gives it. White rum and fresh lime, with sugar stirred into the lime until it's gone, swizzled on crushed ice for thirty seconds and poured straight away into six small chilled tumblers, so nobody waits longer than anyone else. A Daiquiri always asks for some quick thinking, because limes vary in sourness from one fruit to the next. I've measured everything a measure can fix, and the limes are yours to judge. My only addition is four makrut lime leaves, pressed into the lime and lifted out before you pour. Makrut is a different lime, with harsh juice but fragrant leaves, so the answer comes from the part of the tree you'd never think to squeeze. The leaves bring lime's scent without making the drink any sharper. They're there as one more answer to his worry, and as a nod to the question you always ask first: what else is there? It's icy, bright and fresh, and the lime carries right through it.

5. When every face next turns to you and nothing has come yet, tell them the truth, smiling: this is the bit you love most. Watch what that does.
