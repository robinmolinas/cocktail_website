**method**
1. Chill the glass in the freezer, or fill it with ice water while you mix.
2. Put the cognac, sweet vermouth, mandarin liqueur and bitters in a mixing glass or jar. Fill it with ordinary ice cubes (not crushed).
3. Stir for about 15 seconds, until the outside of the glass feels cold.
4. If someone's still talking, stop stirring and leave the spoon where it is. The drink can sit on its ice for a minute and a half or so without getting watery.
5. When they've finished, empty the serving glass and strain the drink into it.
6. Squeeze the mandarin peel over the top so its oils fall on the drink, then drop it in.

### Reading

**epigraph** This drink can sit on its ice while someone finishes talking. Someone once timed it.

**whoYouAre**

You hear it before anyone else does. A joke at dinner that lands a little too hard. A voice going up half a note in a meeting. A group chat going quiet in the wrong way. By the time the others notice, you've already pulled up a chair, asked the one who's upset what they meant, and let them say all of it.

People take you for easygoing. You aren't. You're listening the whole time, because when people are in your care, you'd rather hear the hard thing tonight than watch it break something later. You could settle most of it with one word, and you rarely use it. You'd rather they finish. It matters more than you think: people say the real thing around you, because they're sure you'll hear all of it. You never mention how hard you work at it, or the fear underneath: that the one time you miss it, it all comes apart, and someone you were looking after pays for it.

**yours**

1. In 1924, a barman called Jimmie Charters started at the Dingo, a bar in Montparnasse, Paris, as the assistant. Years later he told his story to a writer, Morrill Cody, and the job he described went a long way past the drinks. He was there to settle fights, to listen when people confided in him, to stop jealousies before they started, and to crack jokes. He'd never worked so hard in his life, he said.

2. Fights, he said, were to be avoided at all costs. He could usually smell one coming, and then he'd do anything to stop it. He'd try a drink on the house, and usually, he said, the free drink worked. And this is what gets me: the two who'd been about to fight would drink to each other, or both drink to him, or one way or another forget to be angry. It didn't always work. Once a fight broke out anyway, and he had to carry one side out, one at a time.

3. I like to think the drink was never the point, and that he wasn't deciding who was right. He was turning two people back towards each other before anyone got hurt, and that's what you do. He also admitted what it cost him. When a regular ran up a big tab, he couldn't stop the credit, for fear they'd get angry, walk out, and everything would be gone. I think you know that fear. It's the no you swallow to keep the room together.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Manhattan, one of the most famous classics, the one so many others were built on. I've made it on cognac instead of whiskey, with a small spoon of mandarin liqueur and the oil from a mandarin peel, as a nod to the cognac and the Mandarin in the cocktail Jimmie invented at the Dingo. It's rich and warm, and the first thing you'll smell is mandarin. And it's stirred on purpose. Dave Arnold, who wrote a whole book on the science of cocktails, once left Manhattans sitting on their ice for a minute and a half before straining them, and could find almost no difference. So this one can wait a minute or two on its ice while someone finishes what they're saying.

5. You're right to hear people out, and they're right to trust you for it. But one day the kind thing will be the no: the tab stopped, the plan called off, the decision made before everyone agrees. Say it plainly, once they've finished. The people you listened to won't walk out. They stayed because you listened, and that's exactly why your no will hold.

### Reading

**epigraph** Two teaspoons of vermouth went in, for a taste one bartender was told to start liking.

**whoYouAre**

You notice the thing that's slightly off. The line on the page that sits a millimetre low. The dish that should have come out hot. The word on a menu that doesn't mean what it says. Most people let it pass. You can't, and you'd rather seem cold than let it go out like that.

People take it for pride, or for your taste. It isn't, mostly. At the end of every piece of work there's someone who'll never know what you turned down for them, and they're who you're thinking of. You hardly ever mention it, because it sounds like asking to be thanked. So your no arrives alone, without its reason. What you fear is something careless going out under a name that was meant to stand for something. Around you, good becomes normal. People raise their own bar near you, often without noticing they have, and whoever's at the end gets it as it should be.

**yours**

1. In 1990s London, the hottest bars were pouring fruit "Martinis". People loved those drinks, and plenty still do. A bartender from Camden called Wayne Collins hated them, and wrote an article against them in a magazine called *Class*. His quarrel was with the name. As he told Robert Simonson in 2014, he didn't like that it was called a Martini.

2. Here's the part I love. The way Collins remembers it, he'd ask why anyone would drink gin and vermouth, and his teacher, Lee Chappell, would tell him he'd better start liking it, because it was for the guests, not for him. Chappell, by the way, ran a place in Covent Garden called the Long Island Iced Tea Shop. Nobody in this story is a snob.

3. I can't know what was in Collins's head when he wrote that article, but I like to think it was Chappell's lesson: not his own taste, but a promise made to someone else, about what a name should mean. He didn't stop at no, either. In 2000, as bar manager at High Holborn, he put on a menu of drinks he'd found in old books, ones that weren't on other London menus. That bar, as one London bartender put it, influenced a lot of people, and the training programme he built, Mixxit, is credited with driving up service standards in the trade. That's the shape of what you do: the no comes with a better yes behind it, and people around you start holding the same line.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a whiskey Daisy. Daisies were on his High Holborn menu, and I've made this one the old way: whiskey (bourbon, here), fresh lemon, some sugar, just a touch of orange curaçao, then only a splash of soda. It's an old classic you'll still rarely find on a list. I serve it very cold, in a fine stemmed glass. Like his Sazeracs, it comes in good glassware with no ice in it. And I've added two teaspoons of dry vermouth, as a nod to the gin and vermouth Collins was told to start liking, for the guests. You won't taste it as vermouth. You'll taste a drier, rounder drink because of it.

5. I wouldn't ask you to lower the bar. Please don't, for anyone. But from now on, when you turn something down, add the why, the way Chappell did. A no that comes with its reason stops sounding cold. It sounds like care, because that's what it is. And the reason travels where you can't: people start keeping your standard in rooms you'll never walk into, because at last they understand why.

| for the rim | fine (caster) sugar | on the outside of the rim only |

**method**
1. Put the coupe in the freezer for ten minutes.
2. Pour the half teaspoon of apricot eau-de-vie into a saucer, and spread a little fine sugar on a second saucer.
3. Turn the cold glass upside down and roll the outside edge of its rim through the apricot spirit, then through the sugar, until a thin band of sugar runs all the way round the outside.
4. Pour what's left of the apricot spirit into the glass and turn the glass slowly until the inside is coated. Leave it there. Nothing gets tipped out.
5. Put the vodka, lemon juice and simple syrup in a shaker, fill it with ice and shake hard for 10 to 15 seconds.
6. Strain it into the glass.

### Reading

**epigraph** A Chicago bar guide of 1900 says to coat the glass. The drink came decades later.

**whoYouAre**

Every summer there's the same street party, and every summer a few more people find a reason to miss it. You're the one who suggests the Saturday instead of the Sunday, and the neighbours nobody ever thought to knock for. You keep the food everyone brings, the old songs, the raffle with the terrible prizes. Someone who's been coming for thirty years tells you it won't be the same. It isn't. The next summer, the street is full.

Here's the part nobody guesses: you love the old version more than the people defending it. You argue with dates and numbers, because "I'd hate to lose this" sounds soft, so the ones who love it too can hear someone wrecking it. The row doesn't scare you. What does is the change you pushed for turning out to be what finishes it off, and everyone remembering it was you. And look what you leave instead: the things people love are still there, with more people in them, because someone cared enough to change them. Nobody says so at the time. Someone should.

**yours**

1. Before he opened Henry Africa's in San Francisco in 1969, Norman Hobday found the place, which he remembered as a bar with blackened windows. There wasn't enough money to remodel, he said later, so he brought in plants. As he put it, they did "a hell of a job for what they cost". It was bright and cheery, he said, and people started swarming in.

2. Hobday loved the place as much as his customers did. A few years before it closed, he legally changed his own name to the bar's, Henry Africa, and kept it until he died. Many bars like his were done up with brass, Tiffany lamps and ferns, like the lounges of the 1890s, which had often turned away women who came on their own. Now women and men came in on equal terms. Fern bars are credited with bringing the American saloon back from the dead.

3. He gave the cheap reason, and I believe him. But I like to think it was never the whole reason: nobody takes a bar's name for the rest of their life over a cheap fix. You'd know that move. You give the sensible reason out loud, and the love shows in how long you stay. And what you change tends to outlive the place. Henry Africa's closed in 1986. The change didn't. When the bar world went back to the old craft in the early 2000s, it chose to keep, among other things, the bars where women drank on equal terms, and the women working behind them.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is Hobday's most enduring drink, the Lemon Drop, made the elegant way it was first served, before it shrank into a shot: up, in a sugar-rimmed glass. Mine is a chilled coupe, and I take the sugar all the way round. The lemon is squeezed fresh, the way his bar made its juices, to order. It tastes of the lemon candy it's named after, sweet and bright. The change is in the glass. In a Chicago bar guide from 1900, James Maloney gave a set of drinks that went into a glass coated inside with apricot liqueur, a sweet red one. I do the same with a clear, dry apricot brandy. Half a teaspoon is enough to coat the glass and hold the rim's sugar in place, and I tip none of it out. It's there as a nod to what you do: the old thing kept on purpose, holding the new one. The apricot reaches you first, then the lemon.

5. One thing I'd like you to try. You keep the love behind the sensible reasons, and that's why the people defending the old version hear an enemy. Let them hear the love first, before the dates and the numbers. They'll find someone on their side, because you always were. And once the place is full again, don't stand by the wall checking that it held. Go in and enjoy it. It was always as much yours as theirs.
