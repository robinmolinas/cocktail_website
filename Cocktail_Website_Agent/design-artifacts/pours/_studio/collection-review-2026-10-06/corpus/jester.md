| 1 thin wheel | fresh orange, about ½ cm thick, cut into eight pieces, peel and all | the peel is where most of an orange's flavour is; the thin wheel gives only a splash of juice |

| 2 dashes | Peychaud's bitters, recommended (or another bright-red New Orleans-style aromatic bitters) | the Sazerac's bitters, for the guests who'd rather have had a Sazerac |

**method**
1. Put the wine glass in the freezer for ten minutes.
2. Cut a thin wheel from an orange, about half a centimetre, and cut it into eight pieces. Leave the peel on.
3. Put the orange pieces and the coriander seeds in a shaker. Press them firmly with a muddler (or the end of a rolling pin) until the peel smells strongly of orange and the juice runs.
4. Add the gin, both vermouths and the bitters. Fill the shaker with ice and shake hard for about ten seconds.
5. Pour it through the shaker's strainer and a small tea strainer held over the glass, so no seeds or pulp get in.
6. Nothing on the rim. The orange is already in it.

**closingLine:** *Muddle the whole wheel, peel and all. Then tell the story of the one that didn't take.*


### Reading

**epigraph** People tried everything to fix the orange in the Bronx cocktail. This is one more try.

**whoYouAre**

It's a grey week, or a long wait, or a birthday nobody's quite in the mood for, and you've decided it's going to be good anyway. So you think of something: a silly game with proper rules, a theme, a time to turn up. Half the fun is that you took it seriously. People who would never start anything themselves find they've joined in, and even the one who swore they wouldn't ends up laughing.

There's a list, though, and only you have it: the ones that didn't take. The quiz nobody got into. The evening that stayed flat however hard you pushed it. Everyone else forgot them by the next morning. You didn't, because under all the fun is the thing you dread most: a gloom nobody could lift, and you the one who couldn't lift it. Here's what those nights gave people, even the flat ones. They could tell that someone wanted the evening to be good for them. That's what your fun was for all along, and it matters more than whether the game worked.

**yours**

1. Early in 1917, on a Sunday afternoon in St. Louis, Clara Bell Walsh had about fifty guests over for something new: an hour of cocktails, from noon until one. A newspaper at the time gave her the credit for the idea. It's the first party on record that was planned as a cocktail party and called one.

2. It wasn't her first idea. She'd tried other party themes before it, and in one of them the guests dressed up as babies and drank from bottles. That one didn't catch on. The cocktail party did, and more than a century later it's still one of the ways people get together. I'd have gone to both.

3. It's the cocktail party people remember. But it might never have come without the ones before it, and I like to think she knew it. It's the same with the list you keep. The flat evenings on it aren't the opposite of the good ones. They're how you learned what people will join in with, and what they won't. And among the drinks that Sunday there were a few Sazeracs, served to the older guests who were set in their ways. I like to think that was someone making sure even the hardest ones to win over had something there for them. That's you, too.

4. The cocktail I've made for you starts from the sensation of her day, the Bronx: gin, two kinds of vermouth and orange. It was on her menu, and it was famously hard to get right. Put in enough orange juice to taste it, and the drink goes weak, so people kept trying new answers. The version I start from doesn't pour juice at all: one thin slice of orange is crushed in the shaker, peel and all, because the peel is where most of an orange's flavour is. You can taste the orange without drowning the gin. Then I added mine. A pinch of cracked coriander seeds goes in with the orange: coriander is one of the usual flavourings in London dry gin, with a lemony note of its own. And in place of the Angostura there are two dashes of Peychaud's, the Sazerac's bitters, for the guests who'd rather have had a Sazerac.

5. So keep the list. Just stop reading it as a record of what went wrong. Every idea on it that didn't take is part of the one that will. When something you've planned falls flat, laugh about it with them, and start planning another. Throw it anyway. I'd come to both.

**closingLine** Muddle the whole wheel, peel and all. Then tell the story of the one that didn't take.

### Reading

**epigraph** Three coffee beans float on top. They're meant to be flies. It's a long story.

**whoYouAre**

Somebody once said something daft in passing, and you didn't let it go. You took it one step further, then another. Now there's a word people use without knowing it started with you, a thing you always wear, a little ritual nobody remembers agreeing to. It started as a joke, and it's still a joke. You just never saw a reason to stop.

At every age, someone has expected you to grow out of it. What you don't say is that you could. Normal isn't a mystery to you: you could do it tomorrow, and nobody would stop you. That's the part that frightens you, more than any look across a room: how quietly it could happen. Meanwhile, next to you, other people get to be a bit ridiculous too, and they are. They feel freer around you than they'd ever think to tell you. That's a bigger gift than being taken seriously.

**yours**

1. The way a Paris bar's own book tells it, it started in 1924 with a gloomy piece by a New York columnist, who called the regulars of the Paris bars barflies: the ones who never seem to leave. His name, and this is true, was Oscar Odd McIntyre. Then a nightclub owner joked about it in an advert: his Christmas Eve takings would go to the "needy barflies".

2. Harry McElhone, a Scot who ran that bar, Harry's New York Bar, answered with an advert of his own, calling a meeting, and around Christmas the International Bar Flies was founded there. The book lists McIntyre as president, "Big Blue Bottlefly", and Harry as "Little Blue Bottlefly". By 1927 it had over five thousand members, and branches, called traps, from Shanghai to Buenos Aires. My guess is they joined for the reason people join in with you: someone had gone first, and was clearly fine.

3. The joke wasn't his. He just wouldn't let it end where most people would have, which is exactly what you do with a joke. And he kept it going. In 1940, as the war closed in, he locked his papers in a safe, buried it and left. After the war he returned to the bar and picked up where he left off, and a later booklet lists him as the society's president: "Exalted Blue Bottle Fly". He'd been promoted. I like to think the silliness was one of the things he kept safe.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is my take on Cameron's Kick, a forgotten classic, a whisky sour that first appeared in Harry's 1922 book. It's half Scotch whisky and half Irish whiskey, shaken with lemon and orgeat, a sweet almond syrup. Two countries' whiskies in one glass sounds like it shouldn't work, and it plainly does. I've given it a little more lemon than the old printed recipe, and a touch more almond, so it tastes brighter. Then I added the flies: three coffee beans floated on top, the way sambuca, an anise liqueur, is often served "with the fly", because with a little imagination the beans look like houseflies. Here they're for the Bar Flies. They add a little roasted-coffee scent as the glass comes up, and if you bite one, a bitter crunch against the almond.

5. Some stretches of your life will need you to be serious, and when one comes, you'll manage it. It doesn't get to take the silliness with it. When it's over, pick up where you left off: the word, the ritual, the thing you always wear, all of it. You won't be the only one who missed it.

**closingLine** Three flies, every time. If you ever stop, start again at three.

| *for the ice* | | |
| 1 | plastic cup of water (about 200–250 ml) | a cup that fits inside your rocks glass; ordinary tap water, cloudy ice is fine |

**method**
1. Fill the plastic cup almost to the top with water and put it in the freezer. Leave it for three to four hours, until the sides have frozen hard, about a finger's width thick, with a skin of ice across the top and water still liquid in the middle. Freezers vary, so check from the third hour: press the top with a spoon.
2. While it freezes, mix the drink in a small jug or jar: rye, rum, demerara syrup, bitters and the cold water. Put it in the freezer beside the cup. It's too strong to freeze at home, so it'll just get very cold.
3. When the shell is ready, break a hole in the top with the handle of a spoon, about the width of a thumb, and pour out the water that hasn't frozen. Squeeze the plastic cup gently and slide the shell out.
4. Stand the shell in the rocks glass, hole up, and pour the cold drink in through the hole, right before you take it to the table. If a little doesn't fit, keep it and pour it in after the crack.
5. At the table, in front of the person it's for, tap the shell firmly with the back of a spoon. It cracks, and the drink runs out into the glass over the broken ice.
6. Squeeze the orange peel over the top so its oils fall on the drink, and drop it in.

**closingLine:** *Crack it in front of them. Then don't leave the table.*


### Reading

**epigraph** The most familiar cocktail there is, sealed inside ice. Nobody tastes it until the ice breaks.

**whoYouAre**

It's a birthday, and you've been asked to say something short. You do: one sentence, read slowly from a very long sheet of paper. There's a second where nobody knows whether they're allowed to laugh. You let it run a little longer than is comfortable. Then it goes, and it's a laugh nobody planned, not even you. The evening where everyone laughs on cue, at the punchline they could see coming from the door, bores you to tears.

Here's the part that doesn't show: you're watching every face. You'd never explain one of your jokes, because explaining feels like taking it apart while everyone watches. So when one doesn't land, you keep a straight face and take it home with you. What you'd least like to hear is that it came across as cold, that someone thought it was on them, or felt left outside it. It almost never is. And it's a rare thing you give people: a real reaction instead of a polite one. For a second nobody has to know how to feel, and the night everyone's still describing weeks later is usually one you were at.

**yours**

1. In the spring of 2011, a bar opened in Chicago with no bar in it. There was no counter to lean on: the drinks came to the table, from stations at the back that looked more like a laboratory. The chef behind it, Grant Achatz, said they wanted to mix the newest techniques with tradition in a way they thought hadn't been done. One writer picks out an Old-Fashioned as perhaps the finest: the most expected cocktail there is, sealed inside a ball of ice, set in a rocks glass. At the table the ice was cracked, and the drink ran out over it.

2. Chicago's cocktail crowd didn't warm to it at first. A critic that first spring watched the server crack the ice and counted it among the drinks played, quietly, for laughs. The same review found the drinks could feel a little cold, because nobody had time to talk about them. Funny and cold, in the same week. In the summer of 2012 Charles Joly took over the drinks, and he said later it hadn't felt like a bar: it lacked a sense of hospitality. He didn't make the drinks ordinary. They stayed every bit as strange, but now a bartender came with them, to joke and to talk. It had to be, in his words, about "hospitality, the booze and a good time."

3. I like to think that critic put a finger on exactly the thing you worry about. The strange part was never the problem. The joke was there from the first week, and somebody laughed. What was missing was someone staying in the room with it. You know that second better than anyone, the one where people don't yet know whether they're allowed to laugh. It's a better second with someone beside them in it.

4. The cocktail I've made for you brings that idea home. Inside is the renowned classic, a rye Old-Fashioned: rye, demerara sugar, Angostura bitters and orange peel. It's a drink with as many recipes as bartenders, and the whole craft of it is restraint, so it's made exactly. It waits, cold and already diluted, in a shell of ordinary cloudy ice from your own freezer. A home freezer can't freeze a drink that strong, so plain ice holds it like a cup. Whoever made it cracks the shell at the table with one tap of a spoon, while whoever it's for looks on, and the drink runs out over its own broken ice. Nothing in the bottle is strange. The joke is all in how it arrives, which, if you ask me, is you exactly.

5. So keep every strange thing you had planned. Don't explain it beforehand, and don't make it sensible. Just don't disappear straight after. Stay for the second that follows, and for the conversation after that. Nobody needed the joke taken apart. They needed someone in it with them. The ones who weren't sure the first time are the ones who'll come back for whatever you try next.

**closingLine** Crack it in front of them. Then don't leave the table.

**method**
1. The night before, make the pear syrup. Stir the chopped pear and the sugar together in a jar, close it and leave it in the fridge overnight. In the morning, press it through a sieve and keep only the syrup. Keep it in a closed jar in the fridge and use it within a week. If it comes out thin, use 10 ml in the drink.
2. Put the cocktail glass in the freezer.
3. Pour the bourbon, the dry vermouth, the pear syrup and the bitters into a mixing glass or a jar.
4. Fill it with ice and stir with a long spoon for about 30 seconds, until the outside is very cold.
5. Strain into the cold glass.
6. Hold the strip of lemon peel over the drink, shiny side down, and squeeze it so its oils fall on the surface. Then take it away.

### Reading

**epigraph** Bourbon, stirred dry. A four-line poem about San Francisco's 1906 fire ends on whiskey.

**whoYouAre**

The letter says the changes are "for your convenience". You have a line about it before anyone's finished reading it, and the table groans, then nods, because it's true. People assume you expect the worst of everything.

Here's what they miss. You only notice the gap because you still know how it should be. Under every joke is a picture of something better, and you've never quite given it up. You keep that part quiet. A joke is hard to laugh at; a hope is easy. What wears you out is the pretending: a dinner where everyone agrees to call it fine, and it goes on and on. Further down sits a fear you rarely say: that one day it won't be funny any more.

This matters more than people know. When something's absurd, you make it possible to laugh at it without giving up on it. After your line, everyone breathes out.

**yours**

1. After the earthquake and fire of 1906 in San Francisco, several clergymen said the destruction was divine retribution for the city's sins. A city that had just lost so much was told it had deserved it. A whiskey warehouse, A. P. Hotaling's, came through the disaster. People had saved it: its own workers among them, with the fire department and the Navy.

2. A journalist and poet called Charles K. Field is credited with four lines about it:
*If, as they say, God spanked the town*
*For being over frisky,*
*Why did He burn the churches down*
*And save Hotaling's whiskey?*
I love that it never argues. It asks one question and lets the whiskey answer. It isn't laughing at anyone's faith. It's laughing at the idea that a city in that state had it coming. People were still quoting it a hundred and more years after the fire.

3. Then his story turns. Two decades after the fire, from 1927 to 1940, Field was on NBC radio with a programme of poetry and inspirational talks, under a made-up name: Cheerio. No press photographs were allowed, and his fee went to charity. The joke is remembered as his. The hope went out under another name. I like to think he wanted it that way.

4. I think you'd understand him. The jibe and the hope have one root: caring how it turns out. Making fun of the claim stood up for the city. The poetry was the same care, without the joke. Usually only one of the two gets seen.

5. The cocktail I've made for you starts from a classic that was already on record in the 1800s: a Manhattan made with dry vermouth. Mine is stirred with ice till the mixing glass frosts, then poured, with no ice, into a chilled stemmed glass. Two-thirds of what I pour in is bourbon, because the whiskey is the punchline. Dry vermouth and Angostura, just two dashes, keep it lean. Its lemon is just the oil from the peel: the peel itself comes out. Stirred into all that dryness are one and a half teaspoons of syrup made from a ripe pear, left overnight in sugar. It's in every sip, and nothing on the glass gives it away: a nod to the hope Field sent out under another name, with no face on it. It's a sharp, clean drink with something soft running all through it.

6. Keep the jokes. Plenty of people get through the week because of them. But once, say the hope out loud, as yourself, before a joke can get there first. Tell someone how you think things could be. It might get laughed at. I'd bet it gets listened to, and I'd love you to find out.
