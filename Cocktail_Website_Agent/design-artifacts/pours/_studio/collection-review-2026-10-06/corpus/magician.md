### Reading

**epigraph** Reportedly, a 1911 surprise party got its own drink. The name probably went elsewhere.

**whoYouAre**

You notice what someone needs before they've said it, and you act before anyone asks. By the time their big day comes, it's done: the call made, the table booked, the right people quietly in the room. And you're at the back, or not there at all, because it was never meant to be about you.

What you'd never admit is the question that comes after: did I have the right? Help nobody asked for can land wrong, and you know it, so part of you is always listening for the moment a kindness turns out to be meddling. Most of the time it doesn't, because you only do it for people you've watched closely enough to know. Help nobody has to thank you for is help nobody has to repay. You hand someone a moment that is completely their own.

**yours**

1. In 1911 a musical called *The Pink Lady* opened at New York's New Amsterdam theatre, starring a young actress named Hazel Dawn. A drink was named after the show, and as she told it, it was first made for a surprise party in her honour at Murray's Roman Gardens on 42nd Street. The 1911 Pink Lady was a measure of ojen, a sweet Spanish anise liqueur, shaken with Peychaud's and Angostura bitters.

2. Who arranged that party, or who made the drink, isn't on record. And the name probably didn't stay with their drink: in 1913 a drinks book printed a Pink Lady made with gin and applejack, and that's the one that went into the recipe books. In other hands it got simpler and cruder, until by the end of Prohibition most bars made it with gin, grenadine and egg white.

3. That's the worry you carry: something kind you set up, taken out of your hands and put on something you'd never have made. But look at what lasted. The drink from that night didn't disappear. In New Orleans it carried on under another name, the Pink Shimmy. And I like to think it matters that, when Hazel Dawn told the story, she told it as a party in her honour: the name wandered off, and the night stayed hers. That's exactly what your kind of help is for.

4. The cocktail I've made for you starts from the Pink Lady of that night, not the one that went into the books: Spanish anise shaken with Peychaud's and Angostura bitters. I've changed one thing, as a nod to whoever made it first. Three quarters of the sweet anise is swapped for a dry one, with far less sugar. You won't see it. It just takes the edge off the sweetness, so what was a sweet liqueur with bitters becomes a proper cocktail, and nobody will thank it for any of that. It reaches the glass hazy, because anise clouds when it meets cold water, and pink from the bitters.

5. Keep doing it without asking. The people you do it for are the ones you know well enough. Only, when you've arranged someone's moment, don't leave before it arrives. A surprise is the one kind of help you can see land: the door opens, the room shouts, and for a second they can't speak. Be there for that second. That's how you'll know you had the right.

**method**
1. Make the syrup ahead. Warm 100 g sugar in 100 ml water, stirring, until it dissolves. Take it off the heat, add two sprigs of fresh rosemary, and leave it to steep for 20 minutes. Strain, and let it cool. It keeps a week in the fridge.
2. In a small glass, stir the orange liqueur and the Angostura together. That's your stand-in for orange bitters.
3. Chill a tall glass and fill it with ice.
4. Pour the gin, vodka, lemon juice, rosemary syrup and the stand-in into a shaker with ice. Shake for about five seconds, just long enough to chill them.
5. Strain into the glass, top with the soda, and give it one gentle stir from the bottom.
6. Clap the rosemary sprig once between your hands to wake its scent, and stand it in the glass.

**closingLine:** *Mix the stand-in first: that's the ask. Clap the rosemary last: that one's for you.*


### Reading

**epigraph** There are no orange bitters in this glass. Two other bottles stand in for them.

**whoYouAre**

Someone asks you for a few words at their party, and you come back with a toast that has the room crying and then laughing. Someone calls with a broken heart, and by the end of the night there's a plan, a playlist and a joke they'll repeat for years. You don't do small, and it isn't showing off. You listen for what the person meant under what they said, and you give them that.

What worries you is getting it wrong in the most literal way: doing exactly what was asked and watching it land flat, because the words were never really the wish. So you listen hard, and you give more, just in case. And with all those ideas flying, nobody thinks to turn round and ask what you'd wish for. You don't tell them either. In your head, wishes are something you grant. It gives people something that lasts, though. When you take a small request seriously enough to make it wonderful, you tell someone their wish mattered, and they remember that longer than whatever you made.

**yours**

1. In 1999 a bar opened inside New York's Grand Central Terminal, in what had once been a railroad executive's private office: the Campbell Apartment. The management picked ten drinks from an old hotel bar book, because the names sounded good, and handed them over with no recipes. The bartenders taught themselves the drinks from the ingredients alone, although most customers wanted a light beer anyway. One of them, Eben Klemm, remembered thinking: this is kind of cool.

2. Half the drinks called for orange bitters, and nobody knew where to buy them, so Cointreau and Angostura got mixed together to stand in for them. Another called for maraschino. Klemm found out it was a real liqueur, clear and made from cherries, not the "maraschino juice" bartenders were pouring, which I take to be the syrup from a jar of cocktail cherries. So he went to Astor Wines and bought the one bottle the shop had. Taken at its word, I'd say, that recipe would have made a drink nobody meant.

3. At Pico, a Tribeca restaurant that opened in 2001, the bar was lagging, and Klemm offered a deal: he'd improve the bar if he could create original cocktails. The owners agreed. "They were all original," he said later. "I wanted them to relate to the kitchen." I like to think that's the whole secret: when he finally asked for something, it was to do it his own way, and his own way still had their kitchen in mind. You'll build something out of ten names and no recipe, and you'll go and find the real thing rather than hand someone the jar. What you almost never do is what he did at Pico: offer your help and, in the same offer, say what you want.

4. The cocktail I've made for you starts from one of those Pico drinks, the Collins d'Electra. Gin, vodka and a fresh rosemary syrup he created for it are all that's known of it, so everything else is my own, built like a Tom Collins, one of the great tall classics: lemon, a glass full of ice, soda on top, a sprig of rosemary for the scent. And I've added one small thing from the Campbell Apartment, as a nod to the stand-in mixed there when nobody knew where to buy orange bitters: a teaspoon of Cointreau, mixed first with a couple of dashes of Angostura. It's tall, bright and herbal, with a warm orange edge underneath, and I like to think the rosemary is where you can taste the kitchen.

5. So this is my wish for you. Whoever asks for your help next, say yes the way you always do, and before you start, name the one part you'd like to make entirely on your own terms. It isn't taking. It's usually where the best thing you make for them comes from, and for once, one of the wishes granted will be yours.

**closingLine** Mix the stand-in first: that's the ask. Clap the rosemary last: that one's for you.

### Reading

**epigraph** The still behind this whisky was first built of wood and iron.

**whoYouAre**

Right now, something you've made is nearly there. A recipe, a piece of writing, a shelf, a plan. Everyone who's seen it says it's done. Only you can tell what's still off, so you change it, bit by bit, and you note each change so you can always find your way back.

You'll do a thing forty ways without complaint, because every one of them can still be undone. It's the last step you put off: the moment it's served, sent, signed or said, and can't be taken back. So it waits, sometimes long after it's ready. What you hide even better is the hope. People see someone careful, a little sceptical, who wants to see proof. Underneath, you believe almost anything can become something better, and you want that to be true more than you'd ever say before it works. Give you something everyone else has written off, and in time you'll hand back a better one. The people close to you start to believe things can get better, because they've seen you make it so.

**yours**

1. Aeneas Coffey spent twenty-four years in Ireland's excise, the service that taxed spirits and chased the illegal stills. He started at the lowest grade and rose to inspector-general of excise in Ireland. Then, in 1824, at forty-four, he left and bought a distillery in Dublin.

2. His first still was made of wood and iron, a single column. It was soon replaced by two columns of copper. The new still was simpler and sturdier than the design it improved on, cheaper to heat, and it made a stronger, purer spirit from grain, in one continuous run. Ireland's biggest distillers scorned it; as late as 1878 they were printing that these things made whiskey no more than they made wine or beer. Scotland took it up, from 1834 on, and it went on to change the whole British spirits industry.

3. Why he began in wood and iron, we don't know, but I like to think it was where he could still change his mind, and the copper was where he stopped. And notice the order: he'd left the excise first, years before any patent. That's the step you find hardest. Not the forty tries. The copper.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Martini built on single grain Scotch whisky, the spirit stills like his still make in Scotland. It's lighter than malt whisky, and in a mixed drink it can easily vanish, so I built this one for it to lead. It's most of what you pour, stirred very cold with a blanc vermouth, pale and gently sweet, and a bar spoon each of syrup and calvados, the apple brandy of Normandy, with lemon oil on top. Apple isn't what anyone expects beside grain, and I put it there as a nod to what you do with ordinary things. Clean, bright and quietly surprising.

5. When something you've made is nearly there again and your hand goes back for one more change, ask who the change is for. If it's for the thing, make it. If it's for you, put it in copper: serve it, send it, say it. A result you can't take back is also one nobody can take away from you. And then, at last, you get to enjoy it finished.

### Reading

**epigraph** Cognac was replanted on a rootstock from Texas. The grape that couldn't follow is here too.

**whoYouAre**

Something is going under: a friend's business losing money every month, a plan for a big day that has come apart, a team about to lose the thing it built. Everyone else is afraid of making it worse, so nobody touches it. You're the one who says, quietly, "this is what has to change." Then you see it through, step by step, until it stands again.

Afterwards, while the others are relieved, a part of you isn't, quite. Something had to give so the rest could hold: the old name over the door, the bigger room, the side of the project someone loved most. It worked, and you still think about that part. What you fear is what it might do that you didn't mean. It's why people trust you with the change: you weigh what it will take before anyone else thinks to ask, then you make the call anyway, and carry it yourself. Because of you, things that would have been lost altogether are still here.

**yours**

1. In the 1870s, the vineyards around Cognac were the biggest vineyard region in the world. Then a louse called phylloxera arrived, and by 1893 six hectares in every seven were gone.

2. The way out took over a decade to find: a rootstock, found in Texas, that could grow in Cognac's deep chalk, with the French vines grafted on top. The vineyard was saved by being changed. But one grape couldn't make the change. Folle Blanche, the region's floral grape, didn't suit grafting: its bunches grew so tight that they rotted. So Cognac planted Ugni Blanc, with far less scent, and made fine cognac from it all the same. The save was real, and much bigger than what gave way.

3. I like to think whoever chose the new roots knew what they were giving up, and chose it anyway, because the alternative was nothing at all. That's the call you make. Better still, Folle Blanche never disappeared. It made way in Cognac, but further south, in Armagnac, it's still grown, and its brandy comes out floral and fruity, at its best while it's young.

4. The cocktail I've made for you starts with cognac, from the grape that came through, and gives the other one its place back, as a nod to the part you had to let go: a smaller pour of young Armagnac made from Folle Blanche, a quarter of the brandy. In Gascony one of its names means lip-stinger, which hints at how sharp it is. Its brandy keeps the flowers but very little of the sharpness, because spirits carry almost no acid. So I've put some back with a syrup of green gooseberries, a fruit that can stand in for grapes in cooking. It's built like a great Old-Fashioned, and it comes out bright and fragrant: two brandies, with a green, sharp edge.

5. Once you've pulled something through and it's safe again, look for whatever had to make way. More often than you'd think, it hasn't gone anywhere, and now there's room. Bring a little of it back in, and let yourself enjoy the whole of what you saved.

**closingLine** Keep the quarter of Folle Blanche, however small it looks. Once it's safe, find a place again for what you had to give up.

**method**
1. Steep the fig leaf in the blanc vermouth at room temperature. Taste it after half an hour, and lift the pieces out by about an hour. The vermouth keeps in the fridge; this makes enough for ten drinks.
2. Put the tulip glass in the freezer to chill.
3. Pour the grappa, the fig-leaf vermouth and the syrup into a mixing glass or a jar. Fill it with ice and stir for about fifteen seconds.
4. Strain into the chilled glass. No garnish: the grape is what you should smell first.

### Reading

**epigraph** The grappa here comes from one grape variety. Grappa made that way began in Friuli.

**whoYouAre**

There's someone in your life that everyone has quietly made up their mind about. You haven't. You noticed something once: the evening they stayed behind to fix a thing nobody would ever have checked. Since then you've talked about them as if their turn is coming. Usually it comes. And when people ask how you knew, you can't really say.

That's the part nobody hears. Your reasons are so small that, said out loud, they'd sound like nothing, so you hand over the conclusion and keep the reasons. It's also why you can seem a little mysterious. Underneath sits a worry you seldom admit: that what you saw in someone was only what you hoped to see, and they went ahead because you said so.

And here's your gift to whoever is near you. In your eyes, nobody is ever finished. People grow into the place you kept for them, and plenty never find out who kept it. Most people only get that kind of faith from someone who loves them. You hand it out on far less.

**yours**

1. Grappa is the spirit Italy distils from the wine press's leftovers, once the juice has run off. Italians have made it out of thrift since the 1700s or earlier. With spirits like it, the custom was to bury the pressed grapes until the wine was made, and only then put them in the still. Outside Italy, people laughed at it for years as rough "moonshine" made from the "dregs".

2. In 1973, Giannola and Benito Nonino, of Friuli in Italy's north-east, did something small and new. They distilled the leftovers of one grape, the picolit, on their own, and used them only fresh and lightly pressed: the first grappa from a single grape. In Giannola's words, she and Benito "selected the best Friulian winemakers" to get it. One early doubter, she says, came round to be one of their strongest supporters. And six years before, the family says, the first of their test bottlings had their working printed on its label: the year, how much they'd distilled, how many litres came out, the bottle's number.

3. Nothing was rescued. Grappa was already being made. They only looked at it differently, one grape at a time, and I like to think Giannola could already smell what was in the picolit that nobody had kept apart long enough to find. Abroad, the laughing went on for nearly twenty more years. And not everything went the way the new grappa promised: most grappa is still drunk in Italy, and the traditional style has come back. I recognise you in that. Not right about everything, but right about the part that mattered, years before most people could see it.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Martini, that renowned stirred classic, with Nonino's grappa from a single grape in place of the gin. It's stirred with a pale, sweet French vermouth that has spent an hour with a fresh fig leaf. A fig tree is grown mainly for its fruit, and yet its leaves are highly fragrant. I chose it as a nod to the Noninos looking twice at something everyone already used. There's nothing on top: the grape reaches you first.

5. When you next see where someone's heading, show them what you saw, not just where you think they'll end up, the way the Noninos, as the family tells it, printed their working on the label: the evening they stayed behind, the small thing they fixed. Then they aren't carrying your hope. They're holding your reasons, and they can grow into them at their own pace. When they get there, they'll know they did it themselves. And you won't be the only one who can see it anymore.
