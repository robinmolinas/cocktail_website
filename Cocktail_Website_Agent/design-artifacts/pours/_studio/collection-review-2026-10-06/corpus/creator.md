**method**
1. Put a coupe in the freezer.
2. Pour the gin and the vermouth into a jar or mixing glass, then add one barspoon of Fernet-Branca (about 5 ml).
3. Fill it with ice and stir for about thirty seconds, until the outside of the glass is very cold.
4. Taste a drop off the spoon. Then look up at the person it's for. Are they worn out tonight, and do they need the bitter? If they do, add another half spoon and stir a few more turns. Never more than two spoons in all.
5. Strain it into the cold glass. Squeeze the orange peel over the top so its oils fall on the drink, and drop it in.
6. Hand it to them yourself.

### Reading

**epigraph** The smallest thing in this glass is what made it someone's.

**whoYouAre**

When someone you love is having a hard time, you rarely know what to say. You know what to make: a meal from scratch, a card you started three times, a present you were still finishing at two in the morning because one small thing wasn't right yet. People call it perfectionism. It isn't. To you, a shortcut says "you weren't worth the time". So you never take one.

What they're really holding is proof that someone looked closely at them. What you rarely let anyone see is what it costs you: the evenings you vanish into it, sometimes away from the very person it's for. And the quiet hope, each time you hand it over, that they'll understand what it was saying.

**yours**

1. In the 1920s, the most famous woman bartender in Britain was Ada Coleman, known as Coley, at the Savoy in London. She didn't start behind a bar. She began at another London hotel, in its flower shop, making buttonholes for gentlemen to wear. Small things, made by hand, for someone else.

2. One of her regulars was the actor Charles Hawtrey. He came in once worn out from overwork and asked her to make him "quite alive" again. By her own account, she spent hours experimenting until she had something new for him: gin, sweet vermouth and a little Fernet-Branca, a dark, bitter Italian digestif, the kind once sold in pharmacies. It became the Hanky Panky.

3. Coley made drinks at the Savoy for more than twenty years. Of everything she made, the only drink of hers known to survive is the one she made for one tired man. That's a coincidence, and I won't pretend it's more. But I like to think she understood what you understand: you can't really tell someone "I can see how tired you are". You can make them something, take the time it takes, and let it say that for you.

4. So the cocktail I've made for you is her drink, made to measure: gin, sweet vermouth and a barspoon of Fernet, stirred with ice until it's very cold. In a small dose, the Fernet gives it depth without making it plainly bitter. Then you taste a drop off the spoon, and look up at the person it's for before you decide whether they need a little more.

5. One thing, though. You do your best work alone, out of sight, and hand it over finished. Next time you make something for someone, this or anything else, don't vanish to do it. Make it where they can see you. Let them watch you taste, look up, and add a little more because of the day they've had. They've always had what you made. Let them have you, making it.

**closingLine** Look up before you pour. That's the recipe.

**Lemongrass syrup** (makes about 250 ml; keeps two weeks in the fridge): slice 2 lemongrass stalks thinly and bruise them with the back of a knife. Warm them in a pan with 200 g sugar and 200 ml water, stirring until the sugar dissolves. Take it off the heat, leave it for 30 minutes, then strain it into a clean bottle.

**method**
1. Put a coupe in the freezer.
2. Pour the Angostura, the lemon juice and the lemongrass syrup into a shaker. Yes, all 45 ml of the bitters.
3. Now pick the accent: any unsweetened spirit on your shelf, such as rye, gin, mezcal, rum, brandy or whisky. Not a liqueur; it would make the drink too sweet. Try one you haven't used here before. Add 15 ml.
4. Fill the shaker with ice and shake hard for about ten seconds, until the outside is too cold to hold.
5. Strain it into the cold glass.

**closingLine:** *It'll balance whichever spirit you pour. Whether you like it is the experiment.*


### Reading

**epigraph** Bitters usually come by the dash. This drink is built on them.

**whoYouAre**

Hand you instructions and you'll follow them, right up to the moment you don't. You start things before you know what they'll be. That isn't a hole in the plan. It's the part you like best: finding out.

Some of what you try doesn't work, and you honestly don't mind. You rarely mention those ones, and there are more of them than anyone guesses. What gets under your skin is the opposite: something works, and everyone wants it exactly like last time. Being asked to repeat yourself feels like a door closing. Stand next to you long enough and people start saying what you say: "let me try something." You've shown them that something that didn't work isn't a disaster. It's just the next thing to change.

**yours**

1. You've probably seen the bottle behind a bar. Almost nobody pours more than a few dashes of it. It's Angostura, and it began in the 1820s as a medicine: a surgeon in Venezuela made it for soldiers' stomachs. It was never meant for drinks. Bartenders borrowed it anyway, and in their hands it stayed a seasoning, hardly ever the main thing.

2. Then a bartender in Brooklyn, Giuseppe Gonzalez, made a sour (spirit, citrus and sugar, shaken) with an ounce and a half of it. The bitters became the base. The whiskey, just half an ounce, became the accent. The way he tells it, the drink didn't win the competition he made it for, and he was told nobody would drink it. It wasn't an immediate hit. But it spread, and the *Oxford Companion to Spirits and Cocktails* now uses it as its example of a recipe that looks absurd on paper and works in the glass.

3. He wasn't trying to be clever, I like to think. He just wanted to know what would happen. That's the question you ask about everything, and like him, you'd rather find out than be told. And it didn't stop at one recipe. Bartenders went on to make their own versions of it, which is exactly how you'd want something of yours to be treated.

4. So the cocktail I've made for you keeps his ounce and a half of bitters and his lemon, and changes two things. His almond syrup is gone. In its place is a syrup of lemongrass, which sits beautifully beside warm spice and citrus. And his half ounce of whiskey is now yours to choose: any unsweetened spirit you have open. Gin, mezcal, rum, a smoky whisky. Every one balances, and every one makes a different drink. It's shaken hard and served in a wide, shallow stemmed glass. The bitters stay. Only the accent moves.

5. It didn't win, Gonzalez says, and he kept making it anyway. Somewhere you've got something you left half-tried: a recipe, a plan, an idea you walked away from just as it started working. Go back to it. Not to make it the way it was. To find out what it turns into when you stay.

**closingLine** It'll balance whichever spirit you pour. Whether you like it is the experiment.

**method**

2. Pour the gin, both juices, the syrup, the cream and the orange flower water into a shaker. Add the egg white last.
3. Close the shaker and shake it hard with no ice for about fifteen seconds, until it froths.

5. Strain it into the cold glass through a small sieve. No ice in the glass.
6. Pour in the soda slowly, a little at a time, tapping the base of the glass on the table now and then, until a firm white cap of foam stands on top.
7. No garnish. Serve it while the foam stands.

**closingLine:** *Pass the shaker when your arms give out. Just don't let it stop.*


### Reading

**epigraph** Anyone can have the recipe. The foam has to be earned.

**whoYouAre**

You see the finished thing first. Long before anyone else can, you can describe it: its size, its shape, what it will change. And you describe it so clearly that people want to help build it.

What they don't see is the arithmetic you do alone: how far away the end is, what it will cost, whether you're the one slowing it down. Some nights you doubt the whole thing. You never do it out loud, because other people are building on your belief. What frightens you isn't that it won't get finished. It's that you'll make it smaller so it can be.

**yours**

1. In New Orleans, more than a hundred years ago, a saloonkeeper named Henry Ramos served a gin fizz unlike anyone else's: gin, lemon and lime, sugar, cream and egg white, shaken until the ice was gone and the drink had turned into a soft white cloud. It was slow to make. Then it became famous, which meant more of them, every one just as slow.

2. The obvious thing would have been to shorten the shake. He didn't. He hired more hands instead. Beside each bartender stood one or two young Black men hired only to shake. By Mardi Gras in 1915 there were thirty-five men doing nothing but the shaking, each one working until his arms gave out, then passing the shaker down the line. The drink was never one man's work, and it couldn't have existed without theirs. And Ramos had always given the recipe away to anyone who asked. When Prohibition closed his bar, he went on giving it, and in 1925 he dictated it to a newspaper. He died three years later.

3. I don't know why, but I like to think he knew what you know: the recipe was never the hard part. The hard part was refusing to make it smaller, and keeping it whole even when it needed more hands than he had. And when he couldn't carry it any further, he left it clear enough for other people to carry on. They still are. A century later, bartenders are still inventing ways to reach his standard, and his formula still works.

4. So this is his drink, made to his standard: gin, lemon and lime, a little sugar, cream, egg white, three drops of orange flower water as written in the recipe he gave away, and soda to finish. Bartenders today shake it once without ice, to build the foam, and then hard with ice for five minutes, far longer than anyone expects. The soda goes in slowly, until a white cap forms on top. If someone is with you, hand them the shaker halfway. It can't be made halfway. It can be handed on.

5. So, a proposal. Think of the thing you've been quietly trimming so that it fits: your time, your budget, other people's patience. Put it back to its real size, and write it down that way, not the version that sounds reasonable: clear enough that someone else could pick it up and carry on. Then give it to them. Things this size are never finished by one person. But someone has to hold out for the whole of it.

**closingLine** Pass the shaker when your arms give out. Just don't let it stop.

**Thinned agave syrup:** stir equal parts blue agave syrup and water until they're one. Keeps two weeks in the fridge. (He thins his so none of it stays behind in the measure.)

**Clear ice** (a day or two ahead): fill a small picnic cooler with hot tap water, leave it on the counter to cool, then put it in the freezer with the lid off. It freezes from the top down, pushing the air and the cloudiness into the last water to freeze. Take it out before it's frozen solid, tip the block out and cut away the cloudy part. Let the block sit until it looks wet and clear, not frosty. Then score it with a bread knife and tap the back of the blade with a rolling pin until one large cube splits off. Keep the spares in a bag in the freezer.

**method**
1. A day or two ahead, make the clear ice (above).
2. Rub the cut side of a squeezed lime half around the outside of the rim (the outside only), halfway round the glass only. Roll that wet half in a saucer of salt. The other half stays bare.
3. Put your clear cube in the glass.
4. Pour the tequila, the lime juice and the thinned agave syrup into a shaker.
5. Fill the shaker with ice and shake hard for about ten seconds, until the outside is too cold to hold.
6. Strain it over the clear cube. Nothing on top.

**closingLine:** *Cut the cloudy part off the ice. Then stop cutting.*


### Reading

**epigraph** Its maker called it the house Margarita. Other people named it after the restaurant.

**whoYouAre**

You rewrite a message three times, and every time it gets shorter. The last version looks like it took a minute. Most of what you make is what you said no to.

You don't want less. You want to feel each thing properly, and too much of anything drowns it out. Some people mistake the quiet for coldness, or for plain. The ones who know you know it's room: what you make leaves space, and people find their own meaning in it.

**yours**

1. In the mid-1980s, Julio Bermejo was back behind the bar of his family's restaurant in San Francisco, an ordinary neighbourhood place, pouring Margaritas the usual way: fresh lime, but sour mix too, cheap tequila, orange liqueur. Then he tasted a tequila made only from agave, and decided the bar would pour nothing but tequila made that way, at about four times the cost. He made that call, he says, "as a young person with no authority".

2. Over the years the sour mix went, and then the orange liqueur, with agave syrup in its place. What was left was tequila, lime, a little agave, and nothing to hide behind. He called it the house Margarita. Other people gave it the restaurant's name.

3. I like to think you'd recognise those years: all that work, to end up with less. Nobody thanks you for what isn't there.

4. So this one keeps his three ingredients and his habit of thinning the syrup with water, so none of it clings to the spoon. The measures are mine. The syrup is blue agave, the same plant the tequila comes from.

5. Sometimes you cut one thing too many, and it's usually the one that was only there because you loved it. The salt on this glass isn't needed. It's there on one side, in case you want it. Leave one thing like that in whatever you make next. Not because it's needed. Because you like it.

**closingLine** Cut the cloudy part off the ice. Then stop cutting.
