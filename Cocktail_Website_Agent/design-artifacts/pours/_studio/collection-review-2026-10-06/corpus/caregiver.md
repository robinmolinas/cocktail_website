**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 ml | London dry gin (40% or stronger) | |
| 45 ml | lime and caraway cordial, homemade (below) | the part nobody sees, and the whole of the balance |
| | *for the cordial:* 150 ml fresh lime juice, 90 g sugar, 90 ml water, the zest of 4 limes, 1 tsp caraway seeds | some London dry gin makers used caraway the same way: a little, to accent the flavour, not change it |

**method**
1. Make the cordial first, a day or an hour ahead. Peel the zest from 4 limes, just the green part. Squeeze the limes, and more if you need them, to get 150 ml of juice.
2. Put the sugar, the water, the zest and the caraway seeds in a small pan. Warm it gently, stirring, until the sugar has disappeared. Don't let it boil.
3. Take it off the heat, stir in the lime juice and leave it for 20 minutes.
4. Strain it through a fine sieve into a clean bottle and keep it in the fridge. It's best within a week.
5. When it's time, put a coupe in the freezer, or fill it with ice and water while you mix.
6. Pour the gin and the cordial into a shaker, fill it with ice and shake hard for about 10 seconds, whatever way your arms like to shake.
7. Empty the glass if you filled it, and strain the drink in. No garnish.

**closingLine:** *When someone says it's good, say caraway.*


### Reading

**epigraph** Two things in this glass. The second was made before anyone asked for it.

**whoYouAre**

You have a way of doing things. How a shelf goes up, how a sauce is started, how the spare bed gets made for someone coming to stay. There's an order to it, and you keep to it when you're the only one there. Especially then. Whoever sleeps in that bed will never know about the corner you tucked twice. They'll just sleep well, that's how you share that you care, and that's enough for you.

What you rarely say out loud is how proud you are of it. Pride feels like making it about you, so you call it "just doing it properly" and give the credit to the thing. And once or twice, explaining your way, you've watched someone hear "my way is better". You never meant that. You meant: this is the way I know how to look after you.

**yours**

1. Kazuo Uyeda began working behind a bar in Tokyo in 1966, and in 1997 opened his own, Tender, in Ginza, a district where a drink can take a quarter of an hour to arrive. He spent years on a way of shaking that sends the ice round the shaker in a pattern instead of back and forth. It's known as the hard shake: hard to master, not hard on the arm. He wrote that in the West people look at results, while in Japan, at heart, they "respect the process".

2. When he showed it in New York, he spoke next to no English, and a translator was used. What reached the room was "Nobody can do the hard shake but me", and people heard a boast. The man who brought him over says he meant something humbler: "I can't do your shake. I can do my shake." Asked about it once, he said it was about fine bubbles that make a drink "mellow and easy to drink". And he added, "I have my way, but it is not only the way." When bartenders tested it, they found that a good, vigorous shake of any style makes much the same drink.

3. I don't read that as the end of his story. I like to think it's the heart of it. The care was never only in the drink. It was in holding himself to his way for whoever sat down in front of him, and leaving room for everyone else's. That's you. Proud of how you do it, and glad when someone else does it their own way.

4. One of his signatures was a Gimlet, a renowned classic, "velvety" and jade-green. "Not Only the Way" isn't exactly his: it's gin and a lime cordial you make yourself. Lime cordial was first made to keep lime juice good on long sea voyages. In the cocktail I've made for you, the cordial is sugar, water, lime zest and a teaspoon of caraway seeds, the kind in some rye breads, warmed until the sugar disappears. Then fresh lime juice goes in off the heat, and it all steeps for twenty minutes. Nobody will name the caraway. It's just why the lime tastes rounder. Made once, it waits in the fridge for the next six people. Then you shake it however you shake.

5. When someone thanks you for something you made, I think I know what you say: "oh, it was nothing." I'd like you to stop saying it. Give them the order you did it in, the corner you tucked twice. That isn't boasting. People love to hear that you've cared for them. Your method provides comfort and support to them, value it. It's the only part of the gift they'd never know to ask about.

**closingLine** When someone says it's good, say caraway.

### Reading

**epigraph** The fizz in this glass was once meant to cure sailors. It never did.

**whoYouAre**

When someone you care about is told there's nothing more to be done, you don't quite accept it. That same evening you're reading, comparing, writing down questions for the next appointment, finding the one person who might know. Not because you think you're cleverer than the experts. Because "that's just how it is" has never once been enough for you when someone is hurting. Walking away feels like breaking a promise to others, but most importantly to yourself.

Some of what you try goes nowhere. Those tries never make it into the story you tell, but you keep them, every one. And here's something I'd guess you've never said out loud: you love it a little. The not-knowing, the chase. Some days that feels almost wrong, as if it made it about you. It doesn't. It's why you're still looking after everyone else has stopped, and why your care so often reaches people you'll never meet.

**yours**

1. Joseph Priestley was an English clergyman who ran experiments on the side. He found that a bowl of water hung over a vat of fermenting beer came back sparkling. What he had in mind was sailors. In his day scurvy, a disease that struck them at sea, was thought to be a kind of rot, and that gas seemed able to stop rot. So he hoped his fizzy water might cure it. He wrote that it could be "particularly serviceable" to them.

2. He didn't take it to a shop. He took it to the Navy. The doctors they sent him to approved it, and a trial was ordered on the King's ships. He published a way anyone could copy, with chalk and acid, and never went into the business himself. And it was no cure. His water was only water and gas.

3. Others took it up after him, and half a century later soda water found its way into famous mixed drinks: the highball, the Collins, the fizz. Every one of them owes something to a man who was trying to help sailors. It failed at what he'd hoped for them, and turned out useful in a way he never planned. I like to think that's how it often goes for you. You look hard for one answer, for someone else's sake, and what you find turns up years later, somewhere you'll never see, helping someone who will never know your name. (If you've never read Mitch Albom's "the five people you meet in Heaven", you'd love it).

4. The cocktail I've made for you is the plainest but crispest drink those bubbles made possible: whisky and soda, tall, over ice. Whisky starts life as a kind of beer, so I steep it with dried hops, the flower that gives beer its bitterness. That's my own nod to the vat he started with; the record doesn't say what was in his. Then very cold soda, poured slowly and stirred once, and a long strip of lemon peel, twisted once as it goes in, and left in the glass. More than two thirds of the glass is the kind of fizzy water he hoped would be a cure. It's also what makes it a highball.

5. Don't hide the tries that went nowhere. Someone else is about to set off down the same road, and what you found there could save them the trip. One wrong answer ended up in drinks all over the world. Post-it Notes were born from a failed experiment to create a super-strong adhesive. And stop treating the love of the looking as the selfish part. It's what carries the help out of the room.

**closingLine** Pour the soda slowly, down the side. And when something doesn't work, say so: someone out there needs to know.

**method**
1. Fill the teacup with hot water to warm it, then pour that water away.
2. Put the honey and the thyme sprigs in the cup. Press the thyme into the honey with the back of a spoon a few times, just enough to bruise it.
3. Pour in 110 ml of water that has just come off the boil and stir until the honey has disappeared.
4. Leave it for two minutes. Then lift the thyme out.
5. Stir in the whiskey.
6. Hand it over while it's hot.

**closingLine:** *Two minutes for the thyme. Spend them sitting down.*


### Reading

**epigraph** Nobody makes this one for themselves. And it won't wait.

**whoYouAre**

Something goes wrong: a fall on the stairs, a phone call at two in the morning, a friend's voice that isn't quite right. By the time anyone else has decided what to do, you're already moving. You don't feel brave. You feel clear. You're the one saying "you call, you hold this, stay with them", and it's the surest you ever feel.

Nobody sees the part that comes later. When everyone's safe and home, your hands start to shake, or you lie awake until four. And the quiet weeks can be harder than the bad nights, because with nobody to go out for, you're not sure what you're for. Standing back has always felt to you like the worst kind of selfishness. So you've never let anyone come out for you. You'd rather be the one who comes.

**yours**

1. The hospice on the Great St Bernard Pass has been run by canons since the eleventh century, and they and their servants went out into the snow to find travellers who were lost. Over time they began taking dogs with them. The picture everyone knows is a dog with a little barrel of brandy on its collar. It's a lovely picture, and almost certainly a legend. The dogs' real work was plainer: finding the way back to the hospice through a blizzard.

2. The most famous of them was Barry. He is said to have helped rescue forty people. There's a story that he was killed by a French soldier who mistook him for a wolf, and the museum in Bern that keeps him calls that a legend too. What's on record is quieter. Barry retired for his last two years, was brought to Bern at the prior's request, and died there in 1814.

3. We don't know who looked after him in those two years, but I like to think someone did: that after all the nights of going out, someone else finally did the going, and he let them. The rescues get told. That part doesn't. You know the first part by heart. Finding the way back through the worst of it is the one thing you never doubt. It's the second part you've never allowed.

4. So this isn't a drink you carry out to anyone. It's a hot one: Irish whiskey, and a little honey stirred into hot water, in a wide cup. Two sprigs of thyme sit in the honey and water for two minutes and come out before the whiskey goes in, and whoever's making it waits with you. There's no brandy in it, and that's on purpose. And it's hot for one reason: it doesn't last. Put it aside for a better moment and the moment's gone. It has to be made for you, and you have to be there when it's ready.

5. You won't ask for help in a crisis. I know that, and I'm not going to argue with it. So turn it round. On some ordinary evening, when nothing's wrong and nobody needs you, let someone make you this, and sit down while they do. Let them do the going, just once.

**closingLine** Two minutes for the thyme. Spend them sitting down.

**method**
1. Put 8–10 fresh basil leaves in a small jug. Add the crème de cassis.
2. Press the leaves gently with a muddler or the back of a spoon for about 20 seconds — you want the oils, not pulp.
3. Fine-strain the basil cassis through a tea strainer into the chilled flute or wide coupe. Discard the leaves.
4. Pour the ice-cold Crémant slowly down the side of the glass. Do not stir.
5. Serve immediately.

**closingLine:** *Let the Crémant carry the basil up. Then say one thing you'd usually leave outside.*


### Reading

**epigraph** Everyone expects the cassis. The basil gets there first.

**whoYouAre**

People think calm just comes to you. It doesn't. You bring it in with you, the way you'd bring a coat for someone who didn't think to. A rowdy kitchen goes quieter when you walk into it. Someone who was about to cry starts talking instead. Two people halfway through an argument remember that they like each other. Nobody thanks you, because nobody saw you do anything. You just came in.

Hardly anyone has seen you in a mood. Cross over nothing. Wanting the window seat, the last slice, an evening that's only yours. You keep all of that on the other side of the door. Somewhere you learned that being good is what keeps your place, and that wanting too much might cost you it. So the people who love you know half of you. It's the easy half, for them.

**yours**

1. Félix Kir became mayor of Dijon in 1945. He hadn't invented the drink he's remembered for: Blanc Cassis, white wine and a measure of crème de cassis, the dark liqueur made from Burgundy's black currants, had been drunk in Burgundy for years before him. He served it at the city's receptions, set it out, and welcomed people with it. It came to be known by his name, and he died just as the rest of the world was taking it up.

2. He was also a respected veteran of the Resistance. We don't know what that meant for him, or what he brought into those receptions with him, but I like to think he understood what a glass does. You hand one to someone and the moment fills up: they thank you, they start talking, and nobody thinks to ask how you are. They knew the glass he handed them, and very little of what he carried in.

3. I think your calm works the same way. It's real, and it's generous, and it's also the safest place in the room to stand. If you're the one steadying everyone, nobody's looking at you, and nobody's cross with you, because you're never cross first. And as long as you only ever bring the calm, you never have to find out whether they'd love you with the rest. That's what it costs. Not that nobody sees the rest of you. That you never get to hear the answer.

4. The cocktail I've made for you starts from the one he poured, with two changes. Crémant de Bourgogne, Burgundy's sparkling wine, takes the place of the still white wine, built in the glass, not shaken. And before the Crémant goes in, a few basil leaves are pressed into the cassis and strained away. A Kir is supposed to taste of cassis. The bubbles carry the basil up, so the first thing you meet is the part nobody expected. I wanted that part first.

5. So start with whoever you're surest of. When they ask where you'd like to sit, don't say you don't mind. Say the window. And when something small makes you cross, let it reach your face before you smooth it over. It's a small thing to ask, and it will feel enormous. I don't think anyone will leave. I think they'll finally meet the rest of you.

**closingLine** Let the Crémant carry the basil up. Then say one thing you'd usually leave outside.
