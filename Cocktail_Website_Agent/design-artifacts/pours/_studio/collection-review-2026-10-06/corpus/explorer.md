**method**

2. Pour the tequila, the vermouth and the spoon of peach liqueur into a mixing glass or a jar.
3. Fill it with ice and stir with a long spoon for about 20 seconds, until the outside feels very cold. The melting ice is part of the drink: it adds about two-fifths to what you poured.

### Reading

**epigraph** The plant behind this glass flowers once in its life. Most are harvested first.

**whoYouAre**

Every time you return to a place you love, you're bracing before you arrive. The café that might have closed. The field that might be houses now. The woods at the end of the lane, with a car park where the gate was. Everyone else sees the view. You're counting what's left.

You start missing things while they're still here. So you touch them as little as you can. You don't pick the flower. The stone stays where it was. The path looks the way you found it. People sometimes take that for strictness, and you'd rather they did. It's easier than saying how much it hurts to watch something thin out while everyone around you is still enjoying it. And easier than admitting the fear under that one: that some day you'll be part of the reason it went. But here's what your care does, whether anyone thanks you or not. When other people arrive, there's still something there for them to love.

**yours**

1. The agave, the plant tequila is made from, grows for years before it flowers, and it flowers only once. A stalk shoots up from its heart, sometimes twenty centimetres in a day. It makes its nectar at night, and bats come for it and carry the pollen. Then the plant dies. As tequila grew, producers stopped letting their agaves flower. A plant spends the sugar in its heart on that one flower, and once it has flowered it's no use to the still. Stopping the flowers took away a main food of the nectar bats, and their migrations shifted.

2. David Suro-Piñera produces tequila. With a bat scientist, Rodrigo Medellín, he helped start a programme that asks growers to let some of their agaves flower, at least five in every hundred, and to show that bats have fed from them. Letting them bloom, Medellín said, is "considered a waste": a plant that flowers can't be harvested for tequila. One of Suro-Piñera's own tequilas was on the programme's first list. And his reason, in his own words, wasn't only the bats. It was "the necessity of genetic strength": plants left to flower make the crop stronger for later. The lesser long-nosed bat, one of the agave's bats, came off the US endangered list in 2018, the first bat ever taken off because it had recovered. Its recovery had many causes.

3. The bats, I like to think, came back one year to the fields they'd always fed from and found the flowers gone. That's your fear, exactly. What Suro-Piñera asked for is what you already do everywhere you go: leave some of it standing, even when taking it would make perfect sense, so there's something there for whoever comes next. His reason is yours too. You don't hold back only because you're soft about wild things. You hold back because you feel responsible for what a place will be long after you've left it.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Martini, the drink with more poems and jokes to its name than almost any other, made with blanco tequila: the clear kind that never sees a barrel, made from agave alone. I'd pour Suro-Piñera's Siembra Azul, though it isn't the bottle on that first list. A delicate, herbal blanc vermouth goes with it. No peel, no garnish, so nothing is laid over the plant's own smell. Then one teaspoon of peach liqueur. When an agave flowers, its sugar goes to the nectar instead of the still, and I like to think that spoon is the sweetness the still never got. I chose fruit rather than a flower. You'd leave the flower where it grows, and a peach is there to be picked. It's served small and very cold, without ice, so it's at its best in the first few minutes. It asks you to pay attention while it's there.

5. You don't have to be the only one watching. The next place you're afraid of losing, don't go alone. Take someone and show them what you see: the thing that's thinning out, the one nobody else has noticed yet. Then there are two of you who'd notice if it went. And I think you'll find that watching it with someone feels less like grieving and more like what it really is: loving it while it's here.

**method**
1. A day ahead, stir the tequila, Cointreau, syrup, water and vanilla together in a jug.
2. Pour half into each of two freezer bags or wide lidded containers, about 450 ml each. Each one makes two drinks. Seal them and lay them flat in the freezer for a day. The alcohol and sugar keep the mix from freezing hard: it sets thick and icy.
3. If it's still liquid after a day, your freezer isn't quite cold enough. Add the lime (75 ml to each bag) three to six hours before you serve, and it will set.
4. To serve two, put two goblets in the freezer for ten minutes. Squeeze 75 ml of lime juice, unless it's already in the bag from step 3. Break up one bag with your hands through the plastic, tip it into a blender with the lime, and blend just until smooth and thick, a few seconds. Blending longer only warms it.
5. Pour into the chilled goblets. No salt and no garnish. The second bag makes the next two, and they'll come out the same.

**closingLine:** *Make it a day ahead. Then the evening is for your guests, not the blender.*


### Reading

**epigraph** One idea behind this glass came from a convenience store.

**whoYouAre**

Somewhere in your life there's the proof of how you work: a thing that used to jam, drip or stick, until one afternoon you'd had enough of it. You took it apart there and then, and it hasn't jammed since. And you regret none of it.

What you keep quiet is how it starts. It sounds like moaning: the queue that's slow for no reason, the form that asks for the same thing twice, the way something has always been done that has never quite worked. You've tried sighing and getting on with it. It doesn't take. It stays with you until you've found a way round, and the way round usually comes from somewhere nobody thought to look. The sentence you can't bear is "it's always been like that". The one that frightens you is hearing yourself say it, and meaning it. And the things you fix stay fixed. People stop having the problem, forget it was ever there, and get their afternoons back. That's what your restlessness gives them, and it's worth far more than the grumbling it started with.

**yours**

1. By the early 1960s, some people were already making the Margarita in a blender. About ten years later, in Dallas, Mariano Martinez was making frozen ones at his own restaurant, and they weren't working. They kept coming out different, and not even cold, his customers complained. His bartenders were threatening to quit. The restaurant was burning out blenders, he said later, as fast as it could buy them.

2. The answer wasn't in a bar. It was at a 7-Eleven, in the Slurpee machine. He took a soft-serve ice cream machine and bent it to the job: through trial and error, as he tells it, they made it spin faster and gave it a bigger compressor, so the drinks came out colder. Before long, frozen Margarita machines were a fixture, and many versions came onto the market. The original is in the Smithsonian now. He never meant to invent anything, he said; he was just trying to make his customers happy.

3. The complaints bothered him long before any machine did, I like to think. You'd know that feeling. You hear the grumble everyone hears, and you're the one who can't let it go. Then you find the fix somewhere nobody thought to look: a restaurant's answer, sitting in a convenience store. And once it works, you're happy for it to disappear. I doubt many people ordering a frozen Margarita today think about Dallas, or burned-out blenders. They just get a cold drink. That's how you look after people: they never have to find out there was a problem.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Margarita, one of the most famous cocktails there is, made frozen, with blanco tequila and Cointreau, the orange liqueur Martinez's father always insisted on. It's built the way you'd build it: most of the work happens once. A day ahead, you mix everything but the lime, water and all, and freeze it in two bags. By coincidence, the book I took the method from describes it as making a 7-Eleven-style frozen treat. After that, every two glasses are one bag from the freezer and the juice of two or three limes, then a few seconds in the blender. There's no ice added and nothing to guess at, so it comes out the same, and properly cold, every time. And there's a little vanilla in the batch, just half a teaspoon. Orange and vanilla always makes me think of the ice-cream counter, so I borrowed it from there, the way he borrowed a machine. It's served thick in a big chilled stemmed glass, with no salt.

5. So trust the annoyance. It isn't moaning, and it isn't ingratitude. It's usually the first sign that you've found something worth fixing. The next time someone tells you it's always been like that, take it as directions: they've just shown you where to start. And when it works and nobody says a word, enjoy the quiet. You made it.

**method**
1. Make the caramel syrup ahead. Put 1 tablespoon of water in a small heavy pan and pour 200 g of white sugar on top. Heat it on medium without stirring, just swirling the pan now and then, and watch it the whole time. It goes clear, then pale gold, then deep amber. Take it off the heat at deep amber, as soon as it smells toasted and before any smoke rises: past that point it turns bitter in seconds. Stand back and slowly pour in 200 ml of hot water. It will spit and boil hard, and the caramel will set into lumps. Put the pan back on a low heat and stir until everything has dissolved, then let it bubble gently for a minute or two more, until it's about as thick as runny honey. Let it cool and keep it in a closed jar in the fridge. It makes enough for about 25 drinks.
2. Put a small tumbler in the freezer for at least 10 minutes.
3. Read the strength on your bottle's label. Up to 47.5% (95 proof), use 60 ml of bourbon and no water. If it's stronger, the bourbon and cold water together make 60 ml: up to 51.5%, 55 ml of bourbon and 5 ml of water; up to 57%, 50 ml and 10 ml; up to 63%, 45 ml and 15 ml; up to 70%, 40 ml and 20 ml. If you're between two, take the one with more water.
4. In a mixing glass or jar, pour 1½ teaspoons of caramel syrup, 2 dashes of Angostura, then the bourbon (and the water, if you need it). Fill it with ice and stir steadily for about 30 seconds, until the outside of the glass feels very cold.
5. Strain into the cold tumbler. Don't add ice.
6. Squeeze the orange peel over the top, skin side down, so its oils fall on the drink. Rub it once around the rim, then set it aside.

### Reading

**epigraph** One barrel, bottled alone, used to stay inside the trade. Then someone put it on sale.

**whoYouAre**

The top of the high board, the whole pool gone quiet, everyone looking up. Years later it's a microphone, or a first day in a place where you don't know a soul. You've gone through it so many times in your head that you know every step of it. From the outside, it looks as if you were made for this.

You're scared every time. Nobody's ever been able to tell. What gets you up there isn't a lack of fear. It's a bigger one: the day you could have gone and didn't, and knowing it for the rest of your life. So you go. And afterwards, when people ask, you make it sound like very little. That isn't modesty. You've seen what happens when something looks impossible: nobody tries. So you make it look possible, and somebody behind you decides they could do it too. Hardly anyone knows you do that on purpose. It's one of the most generous things a person can do for whoever comes after them, and you do it every time.

**yours**

1. Most whiskey is poured together from many barrels before it's bottled, sometimes a great many, so every bottle tastes the same. A single barrel doesn't get that. Whatever that one barrel became is what goes in the bottle. In Kentucky, Albert Blanton, whose name the distillery in Frankfort once carried, used to bottle one particularly good barrel on its own, for himself or as a gift to people in the trade. Other distillery bosses of his time did it too. It stayed with him and the people he gave it to.

2. Elmer T. Lee had joined the same distillery in 1949, and worked under Blanton. When new owners asked Lee for a premium bourbon, the idea, he said, was what he'd learned from Colonel Blanton. In 1984, Lee and his staff did it in front of everyone: barrels bottled one at a time, and sold. It was the first single-barrel bourbon brand. Other fine bourbons had been launched before and taken off the shelves for lack of interest. This one's audience gathered slowly, and then, as Lee told it, the other distillers followed, three or four years later. "I had no idea," he said, "it would become as worldwide as it is." When the owners asked if a bourbon could carry his own name, he said yes, provided he could pick the bourbon that went in it. At ninety, he was still picking.

3. I don't know if Lee was nervous. I like to think he was, a little, and that nobody could tell. This is the part of the story that's about you. Years of learning something where it was safe. Then one day doing it on a shelf, where anyone could judge it. And twenty-five years later, still no speech: "I had no idea." You know that shrug, because it's the one you use. And it did what it always does for you: it made the thing look possible, and the others followed.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is an Old-Fashioned, the oldest shape a cocktail has and one of the best loved, made with one single-barrel bourbon and no other whiskey. I'd pour Blanton's, or any single-barrel bourbon. The sugar is my change. Every bourbon barrel is charred inside, and the charring turns some of the wood's own sugars to caramel. So instead of the usual sugar, I do the same to it: it's cooked in a pan to deep amber, taken off the heat before it can turn bitter, and loosened with water, to bring out what the barrel already has, not to cover it. I did that for you, because you know exactly how far to go, and when to stop. Two dashes of Angostura, a long stir over ice, and it's poured, small and cold, into a tumbler from the freezer, with no ice. Last, the oil from an orange peel, for its scent, and the peel set aside.

5. After the next thing everyone watched you do, don't make it sound like nothing. Find the person who's thinking of going next, and tell them you were terrified, and that you went. Your steadiness showed them it could be done. Your fear will show them they don't have to be fearless to do it. And you get back something you've been giving away every time you shrugged: how much it took.

**closingLine** Take the sugar off at amber. And when they ask, let them hear how close it came.

**method**
1. **The falernum (10 minutes, then a day; makes about 375 ml, enough for about 20 drinks).** With a vegetable peeler, take the green zest off 4 limes, leaving the white pith behind. Put the zest and 12 whole cloves in a clean lidded container with 150 ml of the gold Jamaican rum, and leave it at room temperature for 24 hours. Strain it. In a small pan, warm 200 g of sugar with 100 ml of water, stirring, just until the sugar dissolves, then let it cool. Stir the syrup into the strained rum and pour it into a clean bottle. Write everything that went in on the bottle. It keeps in the fridge for a few months.
2. **The cinnamon syrup (5 minutes, then 30 minutes off the heat).** Warm 200 g of sugar in 200 ml of water with 3 cinnamon sticks, stirring until the sugar dissolves, then let it simmer for 2 minutes. Take it off the heat and leave it for 30 minutes, then strain. It keeps in the fridge for about two weeks.
3. Fill the tall glass with crushed ice so it chills while you mix.
4. Put everything except the mint in a shaker. The grapefruit juice and the cinnamon syrup go in separately: together they're "Don's Mix", two parts grapefruit to one of cinnamon.
5. Fill the shaker with ice cubes, close it and shake hard for about 10 seconds.
6. Strain over the crushed ice, and top the glass up with more crushed ice.
7. Slap the mint sprig once between your palms to wake its scent, then stand it in the ice.

### Reading

**epigraph** Once, if you asked what was in drinks like this, you'd hear: rum and fruit juice.

**whoYouAre**

Years ago, someone gave you an answer that was perfectly true and told you nothing. You nodded, and kept the question. It has come along ever since, at the back of an ordinary week, and now and then another piece falls into place. Nobody would guess that one of your questions is older than some of your friendships.

You've stopped mentioning it, because people expect a question to be settled or dropped by now. Some take you for a know-it-all. It's nearly the opposite: you can't rest inside something you aren't allowed to understand. What frightens you is being stuck for good with an answer you can't get behind. And once you do understand, you lay all of it out, plainly, so nobody else has to take it on trust. People get the whole answer from you, free, and every year it cost you was worth it.

**yours**

1. In 1979, at twenty-one, Jeff Berry started going to tiki bars on his own. At the Tiki-Ti on Sunset Boulevard he asked the old barman, Ray Buhen: "What's in this drink?" Whatever the drink, Ray gave the same reply: "Rum and fruit juice." Berry heard it in bar after bar. Ray wasn't being difficult: he had once worked for Donn Beach, who created the Zombie at his Hollywood bar, and Beach had told him never to give away the bar's trade secrets, in case rival bars copied them. So by the time Berry started asking, many of the old tiki recipes, the Zombie's among them, had become a mystery.

2. Berry had a day job in films. His off-hours went on the question. He read microfilm in libraries, tracked down retired barmen, and their children when the barmen were gone, and learned to read recipes written in code. In 1998 he put it in print that the Zombie's recipe was still unknown. Getting to the bottom of the Zombie took ten years and three books, he said. He found an early recipe in a self-published cookbook from 1950, and in a former headwaiter's notebook, what he calls the original. And he printed a Zombie with every part named.

3. The answer that kept Berry going, I like to think, wasn't a no. He could have lived with a no. It was "rum and fruit juice": polite, true, and empty. You've been handed that answer too, more than once. A shrug doesn't end a question for you. It starts one. And his narrowed the way yours do, from every drink on the menu down to one: the Zombie, and then he stayed with it for years, beside an ordinary job, until he could write the whole thing down.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Zombie, the first famous tiki drink and still one of the great ones. Every part Berry printed is in it, and your recipe names each one. Two of them, grapefruit and cinnamon, travel under one old name, Don's Mix, a classic tiki syrup. Here they go in one at a time. The balance is mine: more of the lime, the grapefruit, the cinnamon and the falernum, so it's brighter and fresher. And I make the falernum myself (a sweet syrup of lime, cloves and rum that goes into many tiki drinks), as a nod to all the years he spent asking what was in things: with a bought bottle, you'd have to take the maker's word for it. It's shaken, then poured into a tall Zombie glass full of crushed ice, and topped with mint. It's a big drink and a strong one, made to be taken slowly.

5. One more thing from Berry's story. On his own, he got "rum and fruit juice". When he went in with Ted Haigh, a loud, sociable fellow historian whom one writer called Berry's opposite, he left with a recipe. "The first Don the Beachcomber recipes I got were because of Ted," Berry said. So take the question you've carried longest, and ask it out loud to the person you know who's least like you, the one who'd ask a stranger anything. Ask before you're sure it's a good question. You may get there years sooner, and on the day it all falls into place, you won't be the only one who's glad.

**closingLine** Write everything that's in the falernum on its bottle. Then ask your oldest question out loud.
