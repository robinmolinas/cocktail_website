**method**
1. Make the syrup ahead. Pour the just-boiled water over the chamomile and leave it for 5 minutes, then take the tea out and squeeze it. Stir the honey into the warm tea until it's smooth, and let it cool. Keep it in a closed jar in the fridge and use it within about two weeks.
2. Put the glass in the freezer, or fill it with ice and water while you mix.
3. Pour the fino, both vermouths, the syrup and the bitters into a mixing glass or a jar, and give it a stir so the syrup dissolves.
4. Fill it with ice and stir with a long spoon for about 30 seconds, until the outside is very cold. The melting ice is part of the drink: it adds about a third to what you poured.
5. Empty the serving glass if you filled it, and strain the drink in. No ice in the glass.
6. Cut a strip of lemon peel, just the yellow part. Twist it over the glass so its oil falls on the drink, then drop it in.
7. Cork the fino and put it back in the fridge. Once it's open it fades quickly, so use it within a week or so.

### Reading

**epigraph** Part of the taste in this glass comes from something that stayed behind in the barrel.

**whoYouAre**

You were small too. People forget that part. You were the one holding the smaller hand at the crossing, saving the last biscuit, staying awake because someone littler was frightened of the dark. No one had asked. It just seemed obvious that anyone you loved should be safe, and if nobody else was watching, you would. You were never less scared of the dark. You just had someone to be calm for.

You still do it. When something goes wrong, the calm voice comes out before you've decided anything. And underneath it, whenever someone you love gets hurt, part of you is already asking what you should have seen, as if it had been your watch. Here's what that gives them: the ones who grew up near you learned from you that the dark can be got through. Some of them are calm in it now, and they couldn't tell you where they got it.

**yours**

1. Fino sits at the driest end of sherry, and it's made in a way I still find surprising. Its barrels aren't filled to the top. Once the young wine is in the barrel, a pale layer of yeast grows across its surface. It forms by itself: the people who make sherry say it comes from yeasts already in the air of that part of Spain. It's called flor, and it stays over the wine for years.

2. What it does is keep the air off. There's always air in the space left at the top, and a little more comes through the wood, and air would turn a fino into a different wine, darker and heavier. The flor sits between that air and the wine and breathes up most of the oxygen itself, so the wine underneath stays protected. The air that would change the wine reaches the flor first. I like to think that's the whole job. Not a rescue. Just being the one it reaches first, every day, for years.

3. That's what you've done since before you were tall. The fright, the bad news, the thing that would have spoiled their night: it gets to you first, and by the time it reaches them, it's smaller. Most of the time they never knew it was there. That's how you've always cared. Everyone close to you has felt safe without ever having to think about it, and that was you.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is built on fino. It's delicate, and beside anything loud it can get lost, so no spirit goes in with it, just a little dry and a little sweet white vermouth around it, the way the Bamboo is built, a famous classic already on record by 1886. The flor itself is left in the barrel, but you can still taste its work: the dry, yeasty edge and the hint of almond in the smell are mostly the flor's. I sweetened it with a teaspoon of honey stirred into chamomile, the tea people make at bedtime, for all the nights you stayed awake so someone smaller wasn't alone in the dark.

5. Pick one of the people you watched over, one who's grown now, and tell them about one time back then when you were scared too, while you were the one being calm. They can carry it now. I think they'll be glad to know what reached you first, and prouder of you than you'd guess.

**closingLine** The fino needs only quiet company. Let one of them know you were scared too.

**method**
1. **The cubes (at least 6 hours ahead).** Stir 150 ml red wine and 150 ml cola together in a jug until the fizz settles, so the tray doesn't foam over. Pour into an ice-cube tray (a silicone one is easiest: the cubes come out soft) and freeze on the coldest setting. They freeze softer than water ice, a bit like an ice lolly.
2. Put the wine and a fresh bottle of cola in the fridge, so both are cold.
3. Fill a tall glass to the top with the cubes, straight from the freezer.
4. Pour in the wine, then the cola, slowly: it foams up on the cubes.
5. Stir once, gently, and serve it straight away.

### Reading

**epigraph** Half red wine, half cola. There's a right bottle to mix, apparently.

**whoYouAre**

What you've made, you hand over straight away: a cake iced the colour the day felt, a card you drew instead of buying one, a birthday song with one verse too many. It's obviously lovely. It hasn't crossed your mind that there's a proper way to do it.

Then someone explains, kindly, how it's usually done. You nod. You still don't see what was wrong with it. The day you'd dread is the one when the explanation starts to make sense. Because what you make rarely looks like the thing. It looks like how the thing felt, and the people it's for can always tell. That's rarer than getting it right, and it's worth more.

**yours**

1. In the Basque Country, people pour red wine and cola into a tall glass of ice, half and half, and call it a Kalimotxo. Spaniards had probably been mixing the two since at least the 1920s. The name, the story goes, came at a festival in Algorta in 1972, after a friend's nickname, and within about ten years it had caught on through the Basque Country and Navarre. In much of the former Yugoslavia it's bambus; in Hungary, vadász.

2. People who know wine have their doubts. A writer in the *Guardian* once remembered a tapas bar in Granada where his partner started pouring cola into a glass of the good local red. The barman saw it and came down the bar, pointing: the house wine from the barrel is the one you mix, and this bottle, you don't.

3. In Bilbao, they take it seriously. The most trusted account of its history was put together by an artists' collective there called Funky Project. The *Oxford Companion to Spirits and Cocktails* admits that doesn't sound much like an authority, then takes them at their word anyway, for that very reason.

4. I like to think you'd have done what the Basques did: looked at the wine and the cola, and not seen the problem. So the cocktail I've made for you is a Kalimotxo, the way they pour it: red wine and Coca-Cola, half and half, stirred. My red is a young Spanish one, the kind poured by the glass. It's a Basque classic, dark, cold and fizzy, a little like sangria. I haven't corrected a thing. The one change is the ice. It's the same drink, frozen into cubes first, so as it melts it turns into more of itself, never into water, so nothing waters it down. The fizz comes from the fresh pour. That's for you: something you make should still be what you made, right to the last of it.

5. So when someone next explains the proper way, listen. Some of it will be useful. Then make the next thing the way it looked to you, and hand it straight to whoever you made it for, before anyone else sees it. They'll see what you meant. And you'll get to enjoy making it again, the way you did before anyone explained.

**closingLine** Stir once, over its own ice. How it's usually done can wait until they've tasted it.

**method**

### Reading

**epigraph** From around Cognac, where something growing on the walls was misnamed for over a century.

**whoYouAre**

When someone says "actually, nobody's really sure", most people sigh. You light up. You ask why: why the form needs that box, why a thing is called what it's called, why everyone does it this way. You're not making a point. You'd honestly like to know, and you're secretly hoping the answer will be stranger than you expected. It usually is.

Not everyone hears it that way. Over the years a few of your questions got you told off: "because I said so", the sigh, the look across the table. So now you ask lightly, with a smile, as if it hardly matters. It matters. The worst answer, to you, is a label: "oh, it's just a…", handed over to close the subject, so that nobody looks again. And yet around you, people do look again. With you, "I don't know" stops being embarrassing and becomes the start of the fun, and people need that more than they'll ever tell you.

**yours**

1. In 1872, a pharmacist in Cognac named Antonin Baudoin drew two botanists' attention to a black growth on the walls and roof tiles near the distilleries. It looked like soot. A few years later he printed his own short study of it. His answer was that it was a kind of blue-green algae. He was wrong.

2. Others filed it under a catch-all name that more than three hundred kinds of fungus have been put under over the years, nearly all of them moved out since. Then for decades the science went almost silent, and the studies that did look kept calling it the ordinary sort of fungus found everywhere. It wasn't. More than a century after Baudoin's first look, a team of scientists showed it didn't fit the group it had been put in, or any similar one, and gave it a group of its own, named after him, as the man who first brought it to scientists' attention. Its nearest known relatives live on rock in Antarctica. It feeds on the spirit that escapes ageing barrels, yet it's millions of years older than any barrel, so it's thought to live on something else too. Nobody is quite sure what.

3. I like to think Baudoin felt what you feel the moment someone says "it's just…": that it wasn't, quite. He wrote fifteen pages on it, and guessed wrong in print. Then the people who finally got it right put his name on it anyway. That's what your questions do. Even the ones that land wrong get other people looking, and what they find is often stranger than anyone guessed.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is stirred over ice until it's icy, then poured without it, the way a Martini is, from two bottles from around Cognac. One is a cognac aged for years in oak. The other is Pineau des Charentes, the region's aperitif: sweet grape juice stopped with young cognac from the same farm before it could become wine, then rested in oak too. Into part of the cognac I've steeped a few Sichuan peppercorns. You'll feel them as a faint tingle on your lips before the warmth comes through, a small question in the glass, the kind you've never been able to leave alone. I've poured it into a tulip-shaped glass, narrow at the top, to keep the scent with you. I like to think of it as a little of what escapes the barrels, kept for you this time.

5. So when a question next comes to you, don't make it small. Ask it properly, and offer your guess, even if you suspect it's wrong. Being wrong didn't keep Baudoin's name off it. It got the whole thing looked at. And I'd bet the real answer turns out stranger than your guess. That's the part you'll enjoy most.

**closingLine** Ask what's in it before anyone tells you what it is.

### Reading

**epigraph** Mango and juniper, from far apart, have a note in common. It was always there.

**whoYouAre**

You never set out to win anything. You wanted to get a little better at the thing in front of you, then a little better again. So you rarely turn round to see how far it has taken you. Other people do, and when they mention it, you're honestly surprised.

And when something good comes your way, there's a moment before the happiness. You replay how it happened, checking it was all fair, as if someone might ask. Somewhere along the road you learned that good things can be taken back, and you couldn't bear anyone thinking you hadn't got there honestly.

What you don't notice is that you never suspect anyone else. When someone you know gets the job or the prize, you're simply glad, and sure they got there fairly too. Not many people manage that, and it's a gift: people watch you take the straight road and start to believe it goes somewhere.

**yours**

1. It starts with a cocktail shaker. In 1994, the day after he saw the Tom Cruise film *Cocktail*, a Slovak named Stanislav Vadrna asked the boss of the village pub where he worked to buy him one. And you never mention the fear. Miss one day, and it feels as if all of it could slip. So you shrug and call it your little thing, because the seriousness gets smiled at. But look at what that care does for everyone near you. Whatever you're looking after is safe, and anyone close to you learns that a small thing taken seriously is worth more than a big thing taken lightly. With you, nothing small is ever only small.

**yours**

1. Boadas opened in 1933, a few steps from the Ramblas, Barcelona's most crowded street. It was barely more than a corridor with a bar in it. After the civil war it gained a few metres and became a little triangle, and it has stayed that size ever since. Maria Dolors Boadas, the owner's daughter, grew up in it: she learned to walk there, and did her homework to the sound of the customers. By 1956 she was looking after the bar alongside her father, and from 1967 she ran it herself, for nearly fifty years.

2. Boadas threw its drinks: poured them from a tin full of ice into an empty tin and back, in a long stream, until they were cold and watered down just enough. The technique had come from Cuba, and elsewhere it had fallen out of use; for a time, it was said, Barcelona was the last place still doing it. The family, and the bartenders after them, kept it going, and paved the way for its return. A Barcelona bar connoisseur once said watching her was like a show. A writer who knew her put it more exactly: no spectacle, just a highly professional person. Her best teachers, she told him, were always the guests, and she read it in their faces after the second sip.

3. I like to think the throw was never about the stream, but about the second sip: the drink being right, for this person, tonight. That's how you measure things too. Not by size, or numbers, or who's noticed, but by whether it's all right today. And there's something in her story for the part of you that's scared. A gesture that had fallen out of use almost everywhere was kept alive in that small room, and as of 2021 it could be seen in many of the city's bars, and an apprentice who started there in 1970 owned the place. In the end, what she looked after grew sturdier than any one keeper, and all the days she gave it are part of why.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a dry gin Martini, the great classic, made with the throw Boadas kept going. Three tablespoons of gin and one of dry vermouth, measured with a spoon as a nod to a bar whose founder shunned the jigger, as its bartenders still did in 2021, then thrown between two tins four or five times, till it's cold, and poured through the strainer into a little wine glass, with a thin curl of lemon peel dropped in. We don't know which drinks Boadas throws, so this one is mine. I kept it to two bottles because, for you, the care should be the whole of it: nothing in it is there to impress. It's bright, cold and clean, and it tastes that way because someone paid attention.

5. You look after it so closely that you mostly look for what might be going wrong. Next time, before you check what needs doing, stop and look at it the way someone new would, as if you'd never seen it. It's been all right for years now. That isn't luck. It's every one of those days, and you're allowed to take pleasure in what they built.

**closingLine** Four or five throws, then stop while it's cold. Make it for someone, and watch their face at the second sip.

**method**
1. Put the sour glass in the freezer for 10 minutes.
2. Cut a lemon in half. Squeeze one half and measure 20 ml of juice into the shaker.
3. Cut the squeezed half into four pieces and drop them in with the sugar. Press them with a muddler (or the end of a rolling pin) until the sugar is wet and smells of lemon. Press, don't grind: shredded white pith turns bitter.
4. Add the bourbon, fill the shaker with ice and shake well for about 12 seconds.
5. Strain into the cold glass through the shaker's strainer and a small kitchen sieve, so no peel or sugar grains get through.
6. Hold it by the stem, so your hand doesn't warm it.

### Reading

**epigraph** A Whiskey Sour, as it was ordered. For a while, one bartender made it with Scotch.

**whoYouAre**

There's a crumb on someone's chin at dinner, or a typo on the first slide that everyone has already read, or a colleague whose name someone has been saying wrong for a year. Most people saw it. Maybe they thought it wasn't their place, or that it was kinder to leave it. You go over, quietly, and say so, to that person and nobody else. It never felt brave to you. It just seemed like what you'd want, if it were you.

There's a second after that you never mention. You've said it, and for a second you wait to see whether you're in trouble. At some point someone told you the truth was rude, and part of you still listens for it. And what hurts more is the reverse: finding out that people you trust watched you get something wrong and said nothing, to be nice.

Your way hands people the chance to fix it while it's still small, and it counts for a lot. Being told by you never feels like being caught out. It's being trusted to take it.

**yours**

1. Gary Regan was a bartender from England who later wrote a book on making drinks. He turns up more than once in my stories; this one I keep for you. In the book he tells newcomers to expect mistakes, and gives one of his own. Once he had a job behind a bar, for a while, whenever someone ordered a Whiskey Sour, he made a Scotch Sour. In England, ask for whisky and you get Scotch. The Whiskey Sour is an American classic, and in his book it's made with bourbon or rye.

2. No customer put him right. Maybe none of them noticed, he writes, "Or if they did, they certainly didn't tell me." A waitress caught it. That's all he says about her: no name, no words. Only that until she did, he hadn't learned what he was doing wrong.

3. Whoever else noticed, if anyone did, let him carry on. I like to think she did him the kind of favour you do, and treated him as someone who could take it. "Caught" is his word, but he doesn't use it as a complaint: he set the story down in print, against himself, for anyone starting out. That's what you rarely get to see after you've said it: the mistake fixed, then handed on to whoever comes next.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is the drink that was ordered, a Whiskey Sour: bourbon, sugar and fresh lemon, a renowned classic, served chilled, in a small stemmed glass. It borrows one tip from the same book: granulated sugar pressed into the lemon. The sugar works at the peel, he wrote, and the drink tastes fresher for it. I did it as a nod to what follows a catch like hers. He was put right, and then he passed on something better. Telling someone doesn't end with them being fixed. Sometimes it's how they get good.

5. So when you spot the next one, say it, and don't stand there waiting for the verdict. I'd bet most people are glad, even if it takes them years to get round to saying so. And you've done this for plenty of people without ever asking for it back. Pick someone close and ask them to do the same for you. It's your turn to be told.
