**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 ml | London dry gin | ideally 43% or stronger; 40% works, just a touch lighter |
| 30 ml | fresh lemon juice | lemon, as Oxford's Southside has it |
| 22.5 ml | simple syrup | equal weights of sugar and water, stirred until clear |
| 6 | fresh mint leaves | shaken in and strained out |
| 8 | fresh tarragon leaves | about the tip of one sprig; shaken in beside the mint, strained out |
| 60 ml | soda water, cold | added last |
| 1 + 1 | sprig of mint and sprig of tarragon | to finish, set in together |

**method**
1. Put the highball glass in the freezer for ten minutes, then fill it with ice.
2. Put the gin, lemon juice, simple syrup, mint leaves and tarragon leaves in a shaker. Fill it with ice and shake hard but briefly, about five seconds, so there's room left for the soda.
3. Pour it through the shaker's strainer and a small tea strainer into the glass, so no bits of leaf get through.
4. Add the cold soda water and give it one quick stir.
5. Clap the mint sprig and the tarragon sprig once between your hands to wake their scent, then set them in the glass together.

**closingLine:** *Put the two sprigs in together. Then find out what one of them knows now that you don't.*


### Reading

**epigraph** The 21 Club's house drink for many years. The second herb in it is mine.

**whoYouAre**

Someone rings with a decision they can't make and asks what you'd do. You could tell them. Usually you don't, at least not at first. You ask what they've noticed so far, you say what you'd keep an eye on, and you let them get to the end of the sentence themselves. It takes longer. The one thing you couldn't live with is someone following you into a wrong answer. What they work out for themselves is theirs, to keep or to change.

Then one day they stop ringing. They've got it. Years later they explain something to you, faster and better than you'd have managed, and you feel two things at once: very proud, and slightly left behind. You only ever mention the first. That's better than any answer you could have handed them: they leave able to manage on their own, and sure they always could.

**yours**

1. There was a time when New York's 21 Club was arguably the best-known bar anywhere. Brian Rea was hired there, and Jerry Berns schooled him in running a bar and a restaurant. Rea was an enthusiastic student. He became head bartender. Then he left.

2. Another bar followed, then the West Coast. In 1969 he dreamed up a new job, the beverage director, who would run the bar and the drinks separately from the food. He talked to six big hospitality companies about it, and one said yes. I like to think the lessons took precisely because he carried them somewhere else: taught the bar and the restaurant as one business, he made a job out of setting them apart.

3. Berns lived until 2006, so that new job came well within his lifetime. We don't know if Berns followed it. I like to think he did, and that he was glad the idea wasn't his. That's what I recognise in you. You've never wanted anyone to turn into a copy of you. The day someone you helped goes past you is the day you know it worked.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is the Southside, a classic and, for many years, the 21 Club's house drink: gin, fresh lemon, sugar and mint, shaken, poured over ice in a chilled highball glass and lifted with soda. It's crisp and bright, the way a long drink should be. And I added one thing of my own, a few leaves of tarragon shaken in beside the mint, as a nod to the student who took what he was taught somewhere new. Tarragon shares some of what makes lemon smell of lemon, so it belongs with the rest, and still it brings something of its own. To finish, a sprig of each goes in together, both standing tall, neither one hidden behind the other.

5. Think of someone you once helped who has gone further than you, in the very thing you helped them with. Don't wait for them to ring. Get them to explain it to you, and this time let them get all the way to the end of the sentence. It isn't a debt they're paying back. It's the proof you were right about them.

**closingLine** Put the two sprigs in together. Then find out what one of them knows now that you don't.

### Reading

**epigraph** Bourbon's grain is at least half corn. This glass hides a little more in the syrup.

**whoYouAre**

By the time you say "that'll work", you've already watched it fall down a dozen ways. The trip for twelve, the move, the kitchen that has to feed everyone at once: you've walked through it in your mind, found where it gives, and fixed it while everybody else was still choosing dates. So your calm yes has a whole night of no behind it, and nobody ever hears that part.

You don't much mind how something looks. Someone else can pick the colours and the name. What you can't trust is a thing that only looks sound: the confident arrangement nobody has tested, the promise with nothing underneath. You'll say "not yet" to something everyone loves before you'll watch people stand on it.

They rarely know it, but this is what you give them. Nothing goes wrong, so nobody asks why. They get to enjoy the evening without once thinking about the floor, and that is care, even if it never gets called that.

**yours**

1. In Kentucky, where most bourbon is made, barrels age in tall warehouses called rickhouses. The name comes from "rick", the word for the wooden structure the barrels rest in. In the simplest rickhouses, the racks aren't just inside the building. They are the building: joined into one frame, with the floors fixed onto it and the walls only a covering wrapped round the outside. It can be brick, wood or tin, and none of them takes the weight.

2. A great deal of air moves through a rickhouse, and that air helps the whisky age evenly. Each barrel lies side-on to the aisle, so it can be rolled out and moved if it ever needs to be. There's a newer, tidier way: barrels stood upright on pallets and packed tight. It saves plenty of space, at the cost of the air, and it's a fair trade for the distillers who choose it. But I like to think the open racks hold a lesson: their gaps were built in from the start.

3. I see you in that building. People notice the walls: the easy evening, the trip where nothing went wrong. Nobody sees the frame you set up underneath it in the nights before, with every weak spot already found. And you're happy to leave it unseen. Admiring the racks was never the point. They just have to take the weight.

4. So the cocktail I've made for you is a bourbon Old-Fashioned, the renowned classic, with what carries it hidden inside. By law, at least half of bourbon's grain is corn, and none of it shows in the glass. As a nod to the frame nobody sees, I steeped the sugar syrup with popcorn, so a toasted corn note sits quietly under the whiskey. It's mixed the night before and kept in the freezer, and its water is measured out rather than left to the ice, so what you pour is exactly what you meant. The only thing on show is a strip of peel, orange or lemon, whichever you have. Like the walls, it's the part that could be anything.

5. One thing I'd push you on, though. When it's for people who matter to you, you fill every gap, because a gap is one more thing that could go wrong. Next time you lay out a weekend for them, check every part as usual, and then put the gaps in yourself, as carefully as anything else: a chair nobody's been given, an afternoon with no name on it. You'll simply have given them some air, and in my experience that's where the best of a weekend usually happens.

**closingLine** Add the water and the peel, and that's your last job tonight. Then be a guest at your own evening.

### Reading

**epigraph** "Gin" began as a short form of the name of the spirit in this glass.

**whoYouAre**

Over dinner, a friend explains a lovely theory about how something famous really began. It's neat, it has a name and a date in it, and you love it at once. Hours later you're still in your chair, turning it over. People take that for doubt. It's closer to the reverse: you fall for ideas faster than anyone you know, which is exactly why you check the ones you love.

What worries you is your own delight: a lovely idea, passed on with confidence, that turns out to be wrong. And when a favourite doesn't survive a closer look, you let it go without a fuss. Nobody sees what that costs you.

You take other people's ideas as seriously as your own. Someone floats a half-thought, and you follow it further than they dared, and they leave feeling cleverer than they came. It's respect, and it means more to them than you know.

**yours**

1. There's a famous answer to who invented gin, repeated so widely that drinks historians despair of ever shifting it: a chemist in Holland called Sylvius. It took hold in 1824, when Samuel Morewood wrote that a German traveller, Engelbert Kämpfer, had credited the spirit's discovery to "Professor Sylvius of Leiden". Morewood was nobody's fool. He's been called the founder of the field, and his history of distilling was the first full one. He was probably right, too, that the spirit Kämpfer meant was genever, the Dutch spirit gin grew out of.

2. Maybe Sylvius did make a fine one. Kämpfer seems to have meant just that: Sylvius's own version of a spirit the Dutch were already making. Morewood read it as the invention of the spirit itself, and most of the writers who came after him took his word without question. Historians have only recently begun to correct it. Genever goes back at least to the late 1400s, more than a century before Sylvius was born, and the author of its earliest known recipe is unknown.

3. I like to think the answer lasted because it was such a good one: a name, a title, a city. It's exactly the kind of answer you'd love. And you're also the one who'd go back to the traveller's words late at night, and find the claim smaller than it looked, and the spirit under it older and bigger. I see both of those people in you: the one who loves the neat answer, and the one who goes back anyway.

4. So the cocktail I've made for you is built on the older spirit. It's that classic of classics, the Old-Fashioned, made after a New York bartender's recipe of 1895, which came in a Holland gin version too: a little sugar, Angostura, lemon peel, ice. The spirit is oude genever, the old style (the word names the style, not years in a barrel): rich, malty, closer to a whisky than to the gin you know, which took its short name from it. Under it, poured in before the genever, is a little French grape brandy, distilled from wine, as a nod to the wine Dutch distillers made their spirit from before they turned to grain. Once it's stirred you can't find the layers. The older one is in every sip.

5. So keep loving the neat answers. Don't change that. Just, next time one delights you, don't save the going back for midnight and your own chair. Go back with the friend who brought it: "I love that. Want to see what's underneath?" Most of the time, what the two of you find is older and bigger than the story you started with, and it's theirs to keep too. Going back stops being the lonely part, and becomes what the evening was for.

**closingLine** The brandy goes in before the genever. Love the next neat idea, then learn what it started as.

### Cocktail block

- **name:** Note to Self
- **tagline:** Impressive is easy. Exact is what you're after.
- **glassware:** tall highball glass (about 300 ml), chilled in the freezer, 1-inch ice cubes added one at a time
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
|---|---|---|
| 50 ml | unpeated Highland single malt Scotch whisky | ideally one whose label says it was aged in bourbon casks, with no sherry or wine finish |
| ⅛ teaspoon | pure vanilla extract | the real thing, not "vanilla flavouring"; oak casks give whisky some of the same flavour |
| 100 ml | soda water or seltzer, very cold | straight from the fridge; two parts soda to one of whisky |

**method**
1. Put the glass in the freezer for at least 15 minutes, and the soda in the fridge.
2. Pour the whisky and the vanilla into the cold glass.
3. Lower one ice cube in with a bar spoon so it doesn't crack, and wait about 10 seconds.
4. Stir slowly until drops form on the outside of the glass, about 10 seconds.
5. Add one or two more cubes, until the ice is two-thirds of the way up, and stir briefly.
6. Pour in just enough soda that the ice doesn't float. Push the spoon to the bottom, raise the lowest cube about an inch, and lower it again: that mixes it without knocking out the bubbles.
7. Add a last cube and the rest of the soda. Stir once, a single turn, and serve straight away.

### Reading

**epigraph** A careful 1887 book on whisky hardly mentions casks. The vanilla here is for them.

**whoYouAre**

You know when the boiler was last serviced, what the meter read in March, and how long bread really takes in your oven, not what the packet says. When an argument starts about how things used to be, you don't join in. You fetch the notes.

Some people mistake that for coolness. It isn't. Writing it down is how you keep a messy world still enough to think in. And, though you rarely admit it, it's one of the things you enjoy most. You're better company than your notebook suggests.

What haunts you is the thing you never thought to write down, the one that mattered after all, in a column you didn't make. But when someone needs to know what really changed, nobody has to guess. They count from your numbers, and the count holds.

**yours**

1. In 1885 a London trade paper sent a journalist called Alfred Barnard to visit every whisky distillery in Britain and Ireland. Two years later his reports came out as one book, *The Whisky Distilleries of the United Kingdom*. He's regarded as the first whisky writer. He set down the small details of much of the equipment at each distillery, and a modern reference book on spirits calls his book the earliest and most accurate account of the industry in its day.

2. Writers still count from his numbers. In 1887 he found eighteen distilleries working in what is now the Republic of Ireland. By the 1960s there were three, and that fall is still measured from his eighteen. Old pictures of still houses are captioned as he found them. And yet his book hardly mentions the casks. We don't know why. For all his fussing over detail, he was sociable and funny, and he dotted his reports with scenes from the journey, so the book reads as a travel story too. A hundred years on, it was reprinted.

3. I like to think two things kept it on the shelf: everything he did write down can be checked, and you can tell he was enjoying himself. I see you in both. Your notes will have a gap somewhere. Everyone's do. His gap hasn't stopped people counting from his book.

4. So the cocktail I've made for you is a true classic, the whisky highball: whisky and soda, which took over from brandy and soda in Britain in the second half of the nineteenth century. It's an unpeated Highland single malt, ideally one whose label says it was aged in bourbon casks, lengthened with two parts very cold soda. It's built step by step, and every step has a reason. A frozen glass and cold soda keep the bubbles. The first cube is lowered in slowly, so it doesn't crack. The bottom cube is lifted once, to mix it without knocking out the fizz. I've added one touch: a little real vanilla, as a nod to the casks his book hardly mentions, since oak casks give whisky some of that same flavour. It's there in the recipe, like everything else.

5. A last thought, and it isn't about the numbers. Keep the readings, the dates and the bread times exactly as you do. But put yourself in the notes too: the joke, the hunch, the part you loved. The numbers are what people will check. You are what they'll want to read.
