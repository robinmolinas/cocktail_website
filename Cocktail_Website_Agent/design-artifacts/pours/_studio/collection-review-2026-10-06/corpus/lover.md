**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| about 60 g | roasted apple (roughly half a medium apple, peeled and cored) | wrapped in wet brown paper and roasted until soft, as Willard's recipe has it. Then left to cool. |
| 60 ml | brandy (grape brandy: a VSOP cognac, or any aged grape brandy of about 40%) | the captain's page says only "brandy"; Wondrich reads it as the grape brandy he preferred |
| 60 ml | cold water | |
| 1½ tsp (about 6 g) | sugar | Willard used as much sugar as brandy; I cut it to a teaspoon and a half, or it would be more than four times too sweet |
| 1 | lump of ice | ordinary ice, one piece |

**method**
1. An hour or more before anyone comes, peel and core the apples, half an apple for each drink. Wet a sheet of brown paper (a brown paper bag soaked under the tap works), wrap each apple in it, and roast at 180°C (350°F) for 30 to 45 minutes, until the apples are completely soft. Willard buried his in the embers of a fire. Let them cool.
2. Put half a roasted apple and the sugar in the glass, and mash them together with a spoon.
3. Add the brandy and the cold water, and stir until the sugar has gone and the apple is loose in the drink. Do this before anyone arrives, in the glass or in a jug for several. It will wait.
4. When they come, give it a stir, add one lump of ice, stir a few more turns, and serve with the spoon in the glass.

**closingLine:** *Roast the apples. While they cool, call the one you'd most like to see.*


### Reading

**epigraph** A captain wrote this toddy down from a barman who, people said, never forgot a face.

**whoYouAre**

Someone's coming over. Long before they get there, you've been at it: the chair moved nearer the heater for the one who always feels the cold, the music down to where people can talk, the light just so. By the time they walk in, it looks as if it all happened by itself. That's the idea. Nobody should feel they put you to any trouble. They should just feel expected.

What you'd never tell them is: I was hoping you'd come. All that getting ready is how you hope without asking. A room made ready can't be turned down, and a plain question can. The thought you don't let yourself stay with is the ready room with nobody in it. Here's what people take home from your evenings, even when they couldn't say why: the feeling that someone had them in mind before they arrived.

**yours**

1. In New York, back when hotel guests checked in at the bar, the City Hotel's desk clerk and its bartender were the same man, Orsamus Willard. People told a story about him. A guest sailed to England, stayed ten years, and walked back into the bar. Willard greeted him by name, and remembered his old room number.

2. They said he almost never left the hotel, and that he'd do anything he could to make a guest comfortable. He left no book and no account of his own life, and even the plain facts of it are hard to find. When he went home to Massachusetts, his house had a number on every room door. I like to think he was still getting rooms ready, for guests who might or might not come.

3. That's where I see you in him. Not the famous memory. The rooms made ready anyway.

4. One of his guests did write his toddy down. A British army captain who drank at his bar printed a few recipes in his travel book, and credited one of them to Willard by name: the Apple Toddy, one of the renowned drinks that made the American bar famous abroad. After Prohibition, it all but disappeared. The cocktail I've made for you is made after Willard's: the best apples rolled in wet paper and roasted until soft, then equal measures of apple, brandy and water, as he had them, and one lump of ice. His called for as much sugar as brandy, far too sweet by today's taste, so I kept it to a teaspoon and a half. For the brandy, I took the grape brandy the historian David Wondrich believes Willard preferred. Nearly all of it happens before anyone arrives: the apples roast, cool and wait. I kept it that way as a nod to you, because that's where your care always starts.

5. So next time you're getting a room ready, add the one thing you always leave out. Ask. Not "come by sometime": the plain sentence, to the person you're hoping will come. Getting ready is how you hope. Asking is how they find out they were hoped for. And I wish you a full house.

**closingLine** Roast the apples. While they cool, call the one you'd most like to see.

**Mint syrup (makes about 400 ml, keeps a few days in the fridge):** 250 g white sugar, 250 ml water and a big handful of fresh mint leaves (about 20 g, stalks off). Blend until the sugar has dissolved, then pour through a fine sieve.

**method**
1. Make the mint syrup: blend the sugar, water and mint leaves until no grains are left, then pour it through a fine sieve and chill it.
2. Put two small stemmed glasses in the freezer.
3. Cut a lemon in half. Wet the outside edge of each glass with the cut side, then dip that edge into a saucer of coarse sugar. Keep the sugar on the outside, so none falls into the drink.
4. Squeeze the orange, grapefruit, lime and lemon fresh, and measure them into a shaker with the gin and the mint syrup.
5. Fill the shaker right to the top with ice and shake hard for about ten seconds.
6. Pour it through a fine strainer, half into each glass.

**closingLine:** *Pour both glasses. Hand one over with something you've made, and put your name on it.*


### Reading

**epigraph** 'Juice of A Few Flowers' sounds like a joke. The handwritten card says otherwise.

**whoYouAre**

Friends show you things first: the song before it has words, the first chapter, the dish they've never cooked for anyone else. Something in the way you pay attention makes them want to finish it, and often they do. People are braver with their own work when you're around. Sometimes you even turn up in it: a line in the song, a character who talks the way you talk.

You make things too, and there's usually something of yours, half done, that nobody has seen. It's lovely to be someone's reason, and it feels safer than being judged on your own work. But the you in their songs is their idea of you, and an idea is easy to love from a distance. People have made beautiful things out of you. Very few have asked what you're making.

**yours**

1. In the 1920s, an American couple, Gerald and Sara Murphy, had a favourite little beach near Antibes, and it became a gathering place for their friends: Picasso, Hemingway, the Fitzgeralds. When F. Scott Fitzgerald wrote *Tender Is the Night*, he drew its main couple partly from the Murphys. And Gerald had a line about his cocktails: they were "just the juice of a few flowers". It sounds like a charming man's joke. Maybe that's all it was.

2. But among the Murphys' papers at Yale there's a card in Gerald's handwriting, headed "Juice of A Few Flowers". An ounce of orange, an ounce of grapefruit, half an ounce each of lime and lemon, an ounce of gin, shaken, in a glass with its rim wet with lemon and dipped in coarse sugar. He was a painter in his own right, too. He showed in Paris from 1923, and seven of his fourteen paintings survive. In one of them, *Cocktail*, the lid of a cigar box alone took him four months.

3. The charming version is the one that travelled: the man in the novel, the lovely line. The exact part, the half-ounces and the four months, was his, and you have to go looking for it. I like to think that card is what he'd most have liked someone to ask him about. I suspect you have a card like that too.

4. The cocktail I've made for you keeps every one of his measures, and his sugar on the rim. But I keep that sugar on the outside of the glass, where it sweetens your lips and not the drink, so it needed a little more, inside. I made that a mint syrup, and the mint comes from a cocktail he called the Bailey. In a letter, he wrote that he'd invented it, "as were a great many other good things". So this drink comes from two of his recipes, and joining them is my idea: I put the mint in as a nod to that sentence, where he simply says he made it. It's light, bright and properly sharp, four fresh juices and a little gin, and it fills two small glasses.

5. So when someone shows you what they're working on, show them something of yours back. Not the polished version, and not so they'll praise it: the unfinished one, in your own hand. Let one person see the maker, not the reason. That's how you get known up close. And since hardly anyone asks, I will: what are you making?

**closingLine** Pour both glasses. Hand one over with something you've made, and put your name on it.

### Reading

**epigraph** The Bloody Mary once had a politer name, and no ice at all.

**whoYouAre**

You say yes first, every time. Yes to the cold sea in May, to the dish nobody else at the table dares to order (and then everyone wants a forkful), to walking on somewhere after dinner because the night's too good to end there. You enjoy things properly, out loud, and it's catching. With you around, people stop feeling guilty about the long lunch on a weekday, or the second pudding.

It's never the bill you mind. It's the lights coming up. You can feel the end of a good night coming long before anyone reaches for a coat, sometimes right in the middle of the best part, and that's partly why you enjoy things so hard. Nobody's there for the ride home, when everyone goes back to their own lives and the noise stops all at once. The fear under it is a plain one: that when the music stops, you'll be the one left in the quiet. Still, you make enjoying things feel safe, and people remember who did that for them.

**yours**

1. Crosby Gaige was the son of the postmaster in a place called Skunk Hollow, New York, and he became one of Broadway's leading producers, in a career that took in some 150 plays. But his true passions lay outside the theatre: fine wine and cooking.

2. After the crash of 1929, he started making his living from those two loves too. He wrote a monthly column called Cellar and Pantry, and in 1937 NBC started broadcasting his daily radio programme, *Kitchen Cavalcade*. There was a printing press in the barn on his farm, and among the things it printed were dinner menus.

3. I like to think that after some 150 plays, he knew what every good show knows: it ends while people still want more, and that's why they come back. You'd have understood each other. The big party of the Twenties stopped in 1929, the moment you'd dread most, and what he did was lay a longer table, one that people he'd never meet could join every day on the radio. His last book is called *Dining with My Friends*. His own dinners come first, and then his friends' follow.

4. The cocktail I've made for you starts from a recipe in his cocktail guide of 1941, a witty book with recipes sent in by famous names, and one of the ways the Bloody Mary spread. Gaston Lauryssen of New York's St. Regis hotel sent him his hotel's version, under the politer name it went by there: the Red Snapper. It had as much vodka as tomato juice, with Worcestershire sauce and salt among its seasonings, and it was served straight up, as a cocktail, not over ice. The equal parts and the glass without ice both stay. I took out the Worcestershire and put in a splash of manzanilla, a bone-dry Spanish sherry, so his cellar and his kitchen share the glass. With fresh lemon and a pinch of celery salt, it's savoury, bright and properly cold. And because it's served up, it's short, and it has a real ending. I wanted that for you.

5. One night soon, when it's at its very best, the table loud and nobody watching the time, be the one who ends it. Not because you're tired, and not to be sensible. Say it out loud: this was perfect. Then name the next one, and the date. You'll leave on the best note instead of the last one, and you'll find out what happens when the music stops. Nobody vanishes. They ask you when. And may the next one be even better.

**closingLine** There's no ice in it, so it ends. Choose the night's ending yourself, before the coats come out.

### Reading

**epigraph** This cocktail is made at room temperature. Some things shouldn't arrive cold.

**whoYouAre**

Someone you love says it's a shame you can't make it. It's three hundred miles away, in the middle of the week. You don't argue. On the night, you're there, on time, in your good coat, and the whole room turns round. That's how you love. Not a card, not a call. You said you'd come, so you come, the whole way, with everyone watching. Anyone you love never has to wonder whether you'll be there, and everyone around them can see how loved they are. Nobody forgets being loved like that.

It was never the distance. It's the second before you see their face, when you don't know yet whether they're glad you came, or whether they'd have been fine without you. You'd never admit it, but somewhere along the way, being needed started to feel like the proof that you're loved. It isn't the only proof. Most of the time, it isn't even the best one.

**yours**

1. In November 1889, a reporter called Nellie Bly set out to go round the world faster than the hero of Jules Verne's novel, who took eighty days. She had promised seventy-five. ### Cocktail block

- **name:** In Kind

- **glassware:** julep cup (pewter or stainless steel; any metal tumbler of about 300 ml at home)
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 ml (2 oz) | bourbon, 45-50% (90-100 proof) | any straight bourbon at that strength; the story names no bottle |
| 7.5 ml (¼ oz) | rich sugar syrup (below) | the ode's sweetener: sugar mixed into cold water until it's thick as oil |
| a few leaves | fresh mint (spearmint) | pressed round the inside of the cup, then taken out, as the ode asks |
| about 2 cups | ice cubes, pounded in a canvas bag | the way Chris McMillian pounded the ice for his juleps; a clean tea towel works too |
| 1 bouquet (4-5 sprigs) | fresh mint (spearmint) | set in the ice beside the straw |

**Rich sugar syrup:** 100 g sugar and 50 g cold water in a jar. Close it and shake until the sugar has gone and the syrup runs thick and clear (a few minutes; fine sugar goes faster). Fridge, about a month.

**method**
1. Put the cup in the freezer for ten minutes.
2. Put the ice cubes in a clean canvas bag (or fold them into a tea towel) and pound them with a wooden mallet or a rolling pin until they're fine and snowy. The canvas soaks up the meltwater, so the ice goes in dry.
3. Take the cup out, holding it by the rim. Press a few mint leaves round the inside with the back of a spoon, every side, then take the leaves out.
4. Fill the cup halfway with pounded ice. Pour the bourbon over the ice, then pour the syrup over the top.
5. Still holding the cup by the rim, churn the ice with a long spoon for about ten seconds. Add ice to two-thirds full and keep churning until the outside of the cup is frosted all over. The ice gives the drink about 30 ml of water as you go: that's part of it.
6. Heap more ice on top in a mound above the rim. Push a short straw in at one side, and set the mint bouquet in the ice right beside it, so the mint is under your nose at every sip.

### Reading

**epigraph** This drink has its own ode. A bartender used to say it while pounding the ice.

**whoYouAre**

At dinner, a friend mentions the book that got them through a bad winter. The talk moves on. You don't. You ask what it was about that book, then about the one page they still think of, and half an hour later the two of you are somewhere the rest of the table never went. You take what people love seriously, and they remember who did.

You do the easy talk well enough: the trains, the weather. But you feel the evening sliding past on the surface, and later you write three pages to someone who wasn't there. At some point you started keeping the long version for the one person you're sure will follow it. Here's the thing you'd never tell the table: you've decided most rooms aren't the place for it. Sometimes you're right. But it means you can sit at a full table and still feel unmet. Not an empty room: a full one.

**yours**

1. Chris McMillian came to bartending in his thirties, in New Orleans, and was intrigued by the city's old drinking life. He researched its long-gone bars and early cocktails, piecing his trade's history together drink by drink, and he shared what he found freely, with other bartenders and with the people at his bar, before that kind of history was fashionable. Before Hurricane Katrina, he was one of the few in the city trying anything adventurous behind a bar.

2. Around 2000, he recalls, at the Ritz-Carlton's small library bar, he took to making Mint Juleps the old way, pounding the ice in a canvas bag with a big mallet, in rhythm. And as he pounded, he'd recite an ode to the julep: a long, lush piece of prose, usually credited to a writer called Soule Smith, and printed in a newspaper by 1907. It goes on far longer than any recipe needs to. It even calls bourbon and mint a pair made for each other, grown from the same land and the same stream.

3. It had been reprinted and quoted for decades before him. But I like to think that's what being answered looks like: someone, generations later, taking a long piece of writing seriously enough to give it a voice, in a hotel bar, where most talk is small. It's the reply you hope for whenever you send the long version. And it's the risk you rarely take, because I doubt he waited to find out who at the bar deserved it.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Mint Julep, the renowned classic, put together in the order the ode sets out. The sugar is mixed into cold water till it's as thick as oil, as the ode asks. Mint is pressed all round the inside of the cup, then taken out. The bourbon goes over the ice first, the syrup over that, and the ice is pounded in a canvas bag, McMillian's way, so it goes in dry. Churned, the cup frosts white all over. I broke one of the ode's rules: it says no stirring, and I churn it, so the sweetness doesn't sink to the bottom. Otherwise it follows the ode's steps, as a nod to you: you're the one who reads all of a thing. A bouquet of fresh mint stands beside the straw, so the scent and the taste arrive together, the way the ode wanted. It's ice-cold, fragrant and quietly strong, and it's built to last.

5. Next time the talk at a full table stays on the weather, don't save the long version for later, or for the one person you're sure of. Give it to the room. Say what a book did to you, or why one song still gets you every time. Not everyone will follow. Someone will, and it may be the person you'd least have picked. That's how a crowded evening stops being lonely: you go deep first, and let them come after you.
