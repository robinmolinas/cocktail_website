**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 ml | brandy | an aged grape brandy, such as a VS cognac or a Spanish brandy |
| 30 ml | espresso | one shot, pulled only when you're about to shake; a stovetop moka pot works too, though the foam will be thinner |
| 15 ml | roasted parsnip syrup | made ahead; the jar keeps about a week in the fridge |
| 2 drops | salt water | or a small pinch of salt |

**method**
1. Ahead of time: peel and slice 250 g of parsnips and roast them at 200°C for about 35 minutes, until the edges are brown. Simmer them for 10 minutes with 250 g sugar and 250 ml water, let it cool, then strain, pressing on the parsnips. You'll have about 400 ml, enough for twenty-odd drinks. Keep it in the fridge.
2. Chill the glass.
3. When someone's actually there, pull one espresso. Never before: it loses its texture fast.
4. Put the brandy, the syrup and the salt in a shaker, then the espresso. Fill it with ice and shake hard for about 10 seconds.
5. Strain into the cold glass and drink it at once: the texture doesn't last.

**closingLine:** *Make the syrup before you know who it's for.*


### Reading

**epigraph** Part of this drink is made days ahead. The rest waits until someone's there.

**whoYouAre**

Most people pick up a knack from living among strangers: looking through someone. The person sitting by the cash machine with a paper cup, the one talking to nobody on the bus, the tourist turning the map round for the third time. Everyone else's eyes slide past. Your eyes stop. You say hello. You ask where they're trying to get to. For a minute, someone everyone had stopped seeing is just a person in the queue again.

What people miss is the cost. The ones you walk past, because you were late or tired, follow you home for longer than all the times you stopped. You'd hate to be called good. You're just more afraid of a street where everyone has stopped looking, where anyone could end up being stepped round, you included. The people you stop for get to be someone, not a problem. And everyone watching learns that stopping is normal.

**yours**

1. Naples has an old custom called the *caffè sospeso*, the suspended coffee. Someone having a coffee at the bar pays for a second one before they leave, for someone who can't afford one. Nobody knows how old it is, or how it began; the stories don't agree. It doesn't belong to anyone in particular. It's just something people there do, one coffee at a time.

2. Later, somebody comes in and asks at the bar: is there a suspended coffee? If there is, they're served like any other customer. One Italian scholar reads that question as the gentle part. Nobody has to say "would you pay for a coffee for me?", so the difference between the two of them never has to be said out loud. The coffee arrives, as the scholar puts it, "from the clouds", not from somebody's hand.

3. I like to think the people who keep it going see whoever walks in next the way you see a stranger: as someone, not a problem. And whoever pays never chooses who gets it. It's for whoever comes in. That's how your kindness works too: it starts with stopping, before you know a thing about them.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is built the way the custom works. Part of it is made days ahead, as a nod to the coffee paid for someone who hasn't come in yet: a syrup of roasted parsnips, sweet and a little earthy, with enough in the jar for twenty-odd drinks, waiting in the fridge for whoever's next. The rest is only made when someone's actually there, because espresso loses its texture fast. One shot, pulled fresh, shaken hard with brandy, the syrup and two drops of salt water, poured into a cold coupe and drunk at once. It starts from the Italian *shakerato*, espresso shaken with ice and lightly sweetened; I've added the brandy. Coffee is listed among parsnip's best pairings in the flavour book I trust most: think of carrot cake with a strong coffee.

5. You can't stop for every one. Nobody can, and the ones you miss will come back to you later. So leave something waiting for them: a coffee paid ahead at the place you go every morning, an hour given somewhere they'll come in after you've gone. Then, some morning when you're late and you hurry past, you'll know something's already there for them, and they won't follow you home.

**closingLine** Make the syrup before you know who it's for.

**method**
1. Make the syrup: put the sugar, water and liquorice root in a small pan and bring it just to a simmer, stirring until the sugar is gone. Let it simmer gently for 5 minutes, then take it off the heat and leave it for 30 minutes. Strain out the root and let the syrup cool.
2. Put the syrup and the bitters in a plain tumbler first, then pour in the gin.
3. Add three or four ordinary ice cubes and stir for about 20 seconds, until the outside of the glass feels cold. Then stop: the cubes keep melting as you drink.
4. Nothing goes on top.

### Reading

**epigraph** Four plants flavour this gin, they say. The last you taste comes first here.

**whoYouAre**

There's something you're good at that doesn't match the picture people have of you. A cake nobody believed you'd made. A song you only sing in the car. A drawing on the back of a work envelope. When someone finds out and says "you never said", you laugh and change the subject. Part of you has always believed you had to pick: be good at it, or stay one of them.

The truth is, you want both. And you've got one thing wrong: when it shows, nobody you love thinks you've got above yourself. They're proud it's one of theirs who did it. That's your gift to them, more than the cake or the song: it shows them what someone like them can do.

**yours**

1. Tom Nichol grew up in Tullibody, in Scotland, near the distillery where his father worked. At seventeen he was dragged into it, screaming and shouting, he says: his father had set up the interview. For over thirty years he worked in whisky and in jobs nobody outside a distillery hears about, including liquid carbon dioxide and plain grain spirit. It gets little thanks, he said, because it tastes of nothing and smells of nothing.

2. In 2005 he moved to the gin team at the same distillery, where Tanqueray was made. Within a year he was Tanqueray's master distiller, in charge of the gin itself. He's credited with new Tanqueray gins of his own, and with keeping the everyday one as it should be. When he retired in 2015, the gin trade's guild gave him a lifetime award. He called it completely unexpected, and said his father would have been very proud of him.

3. He'd lived forty years by then a couple of miles from where he was born, and he argued one idea hard, sometimes short-temperedly: a big distillery doesn't have to make worse spirit than a small one. I like to think you'd recognise that: the talent turned up right where life had put him, and once it was noticed, he didn't leave the place behind; he stood up for it.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a gin Old-Fashioned, the classic of spirit, sugar and bitters, built in an ordinary tumbler over ice from the freezer tray. The gin is Tanqueray London Dry, with nothing to cover it. It's said to be made with only four plants: juniper, coriander, angelica and liquorice, the hint that comes at the finish. So the sugar comes as a liquorice root syrup, in honour of a talent that was in that distillery long before anyone named it. The liquorice is the gin's own, they say: it only brings forward what was already there.

5. So show it to the people who knew you first, before strangers. Make the cake for your own table. Sing with the window down on the way to your family's. Nobody will think you've changed. You'll find out you never had to choose, and they'll get to be proud of something that's theirs too.

**method**
1. Put the cocktail glass in the freezer for ten minutes, or fill it with ice and water while you work.
2. Measure the tequila, triple sec, lime juice and syrup into a shaker. Measure them exactly: this is the tight part.
3. Fill the shaker with ice and shake hard for about twelve seconds, until the outside is too cold to hold comfortably.
4. Empty the glass and strain the drink into it. No salt.
5. Now the loose part. Cut a wide strip of grapefruit peel with as little white as you can, hold it skin-side down over the glass and squeeze it so its oils fall on the drink, then rest it on the rim. That's my top. If someone else is making it, the top is theirs: anything that goes on the drink rather than into it.

### Reading

**epigraph** One restaurant owner chose the brands in this drink. Whoever's pouring chooses the top.

**whoYouAre**

You started something of your own. Maybe a Sunday football team, a stall at the weekend market, a supper that turned into a monthly thing, or a whole business. When people ask why, they expect a big reason. The real one is smaller, and you rarely say it: you wanted to live your way, and pay your own way doing it.

There's something else you keep quiet. Somewhere along the line, other people started treating it as theirs. They turn up early, bring friends and save each other seats. And part of you worries about ending up in the corner, keeping the whole thing running for everyone else to belong to. The freedom you went out for always turns out to have room for other people in it.

**yours**

1. Tomas Estes taught English in Los Angeles and wanted to live in Amsterdam, a place where, he said, he could learn a lot about himself. He couldn't see how a teacher could manage it. A Mexican restaurant could. "My main goal was to support myself," he said, though one writer saw it differently: the restaurant was his way to freedom.

2. He opened Café Pacifico there in 1976, with little money, on a street people told him was too dangerous to visit. A second Pacifico opened in London, around a long bar. Young bartenders with few places to go for a proper cocktail found it, and one of its barmen spent ten years making Margaritas for the ones just off their shifts. At another of his London places, one bartender said he felt at home.

3. His partner Phil Bayly remembered his rule for the staff: "Tight on bottom, loose on top." Get the work right, then do it your way. I like to think that's the whole of him: he left to be free, then gave the same freedom away. In 2017 he said he was handing his shares in the London restaurant to the partners who worked there, keeping a minority share because he wanted to stay "part of the community". He added that he should probably have got out of the way five years sooner.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Margarita, a renowned classic. I made it with the two brands one of his London barmen said Estes chose, Sauza for the tequila and Giffard for the triple sec, shaken hard with fresh lime and served ice-cold, with no salt. The bottom is measured to the drop, as a nod to the tight half of his rule. The top is the loose half: I squeeze the oils of a grapefruit peel over it, and when someone else makes it, the top is theirs to choose. It only ever goes on the drink, never into it, so whatever they pick, the drink underneath still holds.

5. Don't leave it as late as he did. Whatever you've started, the team, the stall, the thing with your name on the door, hand someone the piece you've kept hold of and let them do it their way. It won't be how you'd do it. It'll be theirs, and that means it's working. Then turn up the way everyone else does: early, with a friend, for no reason except that you like it there. You went out on your own to be free. You don't have to stay on your own to keep it.

**closingLine** Measure the bottom exactly. Then hand the top to someone else, and join the people you made room for.

### Reading

**epigraph** The rye this drink calls for was first made in a barn, by amateurs.

**whoYouAre**

Someone says it in the pub, on the doorstep or in the group chat: somebody really ought to do something about it. The broken bench, the club with no treasurer, the school play nobody will run. Everyone nods. Before you can stop yourself, you've said "I'll do it", and only later, doing the washing-up, do you realise you've no idea how.

Nobody hears about the evenings: the videos, the forums, the cousin who might know. You're out of your depth most of the way through, and you know it. The words you dread are "who asked you?", and worse, the doubters turning out to be right. You go anyway, partly because if you thought about it any longer, you wouldn't. The thing everyone was waiting for somebody to fix gets fixed. And because you so plainly need a hand, people who'd never have volunteered end up holding the ladder. It counts for more than you know: what was everyone's complaint becomes something they did together.

**yours**

1. For about twenty-five years, Ralph Erenzo had camped on climbing trips to New York's Shawangunk Ridge with no toilets and no running water. So in 2001 he bought an old mill near the ridge to give climbers a proper campground. The neighbours objected. One of them, as Erenzo remembers it, told him the aim was to stall until his money ran out.

2. When the campground stalled, Erenzo asked the town's zoning officer what he could do with the land, and heard that a winery counts as farming. So Erenzo and Brian Lee, a broadcast engineer, set out to make spirits instead. Neither had any idea how. Lee joked that the only fermenting he'd ever done was cinnamon buns with his children. By Erenzo's count it took about two and a half years, from knowing nothing, to switching the still on and making alcohol. He said later that he worked from instructions he found online. And right at the start, Erenzo had asked his son, Gable, too: "I'm building a distillery – I need your help!"

3. Their first bottles held a vodka made from New York apples. Later came their whiskeys, among them a rye from New York grain. By 2015 the state counted more than a hundred craft distilleries, and its agriculture commissioner called Tuthilltown a model for producers across the state. I like to think the best thing Erenzo made there wasn't in a bottle. It was that call for help: what he was about to try, and that he couldn't do it alone, in a single sentence, long before he knew how. You always say the first half. It's the second half you swallow.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Manhattan, one of the great classics: rye stirred with sweet vermouth (a red, wine-based aperitif with herbs) and bitters, then poured over a single large ice cube, and an orange peel pinched over it so its oils land on the drink. It's built for Hudson's rye, sold today as Do The Rye Thing, the line those amateurs began, if you can find it. Beside the rye there's a little apple brandy, as a nod to the apples they started with. It isn't their vodka. It's their first go, kept next to the thing they got good at, and one of my favourite cocktail books calls rye with apple brandy magical. It comes out deep and warm, a little sweet, and brighter for the apple.

| | *the rule:* the sloe gin and the gin always add up to 60 ml (2 oz) | most sloe gins: 30 ml of each. Sweeter than most: 22.5 ml sloe gin, 37.5 ml gin. Sweet as a syrup: 15 ml sloe gin, 45 ml gin |

**method**
1. Put the glass in the freezer for 10 minutes, or fill it with ice and water while you make the drink.
2. Pour a little of the sloe gin and taste it on its own. Every bottle is sweetened by its maker, and a home-made one is sweetened to its maker's taste, so this sets your measures: the sweeter it is, the less sloe gin and the more plain gin, choosing the line from the rule. The two always add up to 60 ml.
3. Pour both gins, the dry vermouth and the bitters into a mixing glass (or any tall, sturdy glass) and fill it with ice.
4. Stir for about 30 seconds, until the outside of the mixing glass feels very cold.
5. Empty the serving glass if it's holding ice, then strain the drink into it. No ice in the glass.
6. Hold the lemon peel skin side down over the drink and pinch it between finger and thumb, so its oils fall onto the top. Then drop it in.

### Reading

**epigraph** In 1947, one bar guide printed five different Blackthorns. This glass is built on one of them.

**whoYouAre**

A friend starts a new job tomorrow and is sure they'll get it all wrong. You tell them what an old neighbour used to say: learn everyone's name on the first day, and the rest can wait. It's exactly right, and they write it down. Half of what you know came like that, with somebody's name on it, and you still hear their voice when you say it.

Here's the bit you don't say: you didn't hold on to all of it. Some of what you were told turned out to be wrong, and you let those bits go quietly, because saying so would feel like letting them down. What's left, you've tried for yourself. You can't always say why it works, only who told you, and when someone asks for the reason, you doubt yourself. The real fear is the day nobody asks. If it stops with you, a little of them stops too.

But each time you say "as my neighbour used to say", that neighbour is in the room again, and someone leaves with something to use tomorrow. You do a kindness to two people at once.

**yours**

1. Since the 1800s, households in rural Britain have made sloe gin: the berries of the blackthorn bush, left in sweetened gin and poured at Christmas and New Year. Along with damson gin, it was simply something you did at harvest.

2. By the end of that century it was being made to sell, and America had a short fling with it. Then for decades it went missing from drinks menus. In the countryside, the recipes went on being handed down through the generations, and they still are. Every winter, pubs and fairs hold contests to settle whose is best. One pub, the George Inn at Frant, hosted one called the World Sloe Gin Championships, and in 2016 the judges, tasting blind, gave all of the top six places to sloe gins made at home. One maker, who reports winning it twice, wrote of trying the way fruit liqueurs are made in Croatia: leaving the jars in the sun.

3. That's my favourite part. A blind tasting can't ask who you got your recipe from. It can only ask whether it's good. I like to think every home-made gin on that table began as somebody else's and ended up as its maker's own. That's what you do with what you were given: every name attached, every piece tried, and only what survived handed over.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Blackthorn, a classic that a barman in London was already calling very old in 1922, when it came two different ways. Of the five a 1947 bar guide printed, I kept the one built from gin, sloe gin and dry vermouth, with orange bitters, because the sloe gin is already sweetened and the dry vermouth lets the fruit show. It's stirred until very cold, served small with no ice. At the end, a lemon peel gets one pinch over the glass, for the scent. Use a traditional sloe gin, bought or made by someone you know, and keep one rule: the two gins always make 60 ml between them. Taste the sloe gin first. The sweeter it is, the less of it and the more of the plain gin. I left that call to you as a nod to those pub contests: whatever the recipe says, your own taste decides.

5. You're afraid it ends with you. It doesn't. Somewhere, someone already starts a sentence with your name, and you just haven't been there to hear it. So don't wait for someone to ask, or until you're old. This year, teach one thing you were taught to someone who'll keep it, and tell them the bit you changed, and why. That's how you end up among the people you quote.
