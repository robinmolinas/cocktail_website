## creator-ruler · The Auteur · *Making the Calls* (draft)

- tagline: You'd rather be blamed for all of it than praised for part of it.
- epigraph: There's one dash in this glass you'll never pick out. The rest tastes brighter for it.
- drink: sidecar · shaken · cognac, triple_sec, honey_syrup, lemon_juice, green_olive_brine · a coupe (a stemmed glass with a rounded bowl), chilled
- closing: Make the first one to the millilitre. Then set it down, name what you'd change, and let someone else make the second.

**whoYouAre**

You have the picture before anything starts: how the evening runs, how the room looks, what comes first and what comes last. After that, every piece has to match it, and you can't leave one that doesn't. You send it back, with a note, and then again, until it's right.

People say you're hard to please. The ones who stayed know it's simpler than that: it's the whole of it you care about, never only your part. What you rarely admit is the other habit, the one after everyone's gone home: quietly redoing someone else's part yourself, because fixing it felt quicker than explaining. Nobody asked you to take it all on. You just can't bear to see it come out ordinary. And the people beside you end up doing the best work they've ever done, sometimes without knowing why.

**yours (proposal paragraph)**

5. Next time someone's part comes back not quite right, you'll feel the old pull to stay late and redo it yourself. Leave it on the table instead. Point to exactly what's wrong, and let them make it again. You'll still be making the calls. You just won't be the only one who knows why.


## ruler-creator · The Perfectionist · *On Their Behalf* (draft)

- tagline: You'd rather seem cold than let anyone get the careless version.
- epigraph: Two teaspoons of vermouth went in, for a taste one bartender was told to start liking.
- drink: daiquiri · shaken · bourbon, lemon_juice, simple_syrup, orange_curacao, dry_vermouth, soda_water · fine stemmed cocktail glass (about 210 ml), chilled, no ice
- closing: Measure the vermouth: it's only two teaspoons. And when you turn something down, add the why.

**whoYouAre**

You notice the thing that's slightly off. The line on the page that sits a millimetre low. The dish that should have come out hot. The word on a menu that doesn't mean what it says. Most people let it pass. You can't, and you'd rather seem cold than let it go out like that.

People take it for pride, or for your taste. It isn't, mostly. At the end of every piece of work there's someone who'll never know what you turned down for them, and they're who you're thinking of. You hardly ever mention it, because it sounds like asking to be thanked. So your no arrives alone, without its reason. What you fear is something careless going out under a name that was meant to stand for something. Around you, good becomes normal. People raise their own bar near you, often without noticing they have, and whoever's at the end gets it as it should be.

**yours (proposal paragraph)**

5. I wouldn't ask you to lower the bar. Please don't, for anyone. But from now on, when you turn something down, add the why, the way Chappell did. A no that comes with its reason stops sounding cold. It sounds like care, because that's what it is. And the reason travels where you can't: people start keeping your standard in rooms you'll never walk into, because at last they understand why.
