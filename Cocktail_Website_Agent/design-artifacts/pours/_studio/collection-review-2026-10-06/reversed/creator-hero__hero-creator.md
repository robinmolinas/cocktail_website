**whoYouAre**

You see the finished thing first. Long before anyone else can, you can describe it: its size, its shape, what it will change. And you describe it so clearly that people want to help build it.

What they don't see is the arithmetic you do alone: how far away the end is, what it will cost, whether you're the one slowing it down. Some nights you doubt the whole thing. You never do it out loud, because other people are building on your belief. What frightens you isn't that it won't get finished. It's that you'll make it smaller so it can be.

**yours (proposal paragraph)**

5. So, a proposal. Think of the thing you've been quietly trimming so that it fits: your time, your budget, other people's patience. Put it back to its real size, and write it down that way, not the version that sounds reasonable: clear enough that someone else could pick it up and carry on. Then give it to them. Things this size are never finished by one person. But someone has to hold out for the whole of it.


## hero-creator · The Idealist · *The Long Answer* (draft)

- tagline: You rarely win the row. You make the thing people end up quoting.
- epigraph: This glass starts from a reader's letter to a New York paper, in 1934.
- drink: daiquiri · collins · bourbon, lemon_juice, orange_juice, pomegranate_syrup, soda_water · a large, heavy, round-bowled beer glass (about 400 ml), with one large piece of ice
- closing: Mint in last, the way the reader wrote it. Then see who writes back.

**whoYouAre**

When something matters to you and the arguing starts, you're rarely the loudest person there. Often you go quiet. The right words turn up later, on the way home, and you can lose sleep over the ones you didn't say. Losing an argument, you get over. Staying quiet when it really mattered, you don't.

Then you make something: a page, a song, a picture, a menu, a message that took all evening. In that, you say all of it, plainly, and you mean every word. Sometimes you wonder whether making things instead of arguing is just a nicer way of hiding. It isn't. An argument changes one mind, if you're lucky. What you make travels. It gives people the words they were missing, and room to join in without anyone being talked down to. That's your way of standing up for something, and it brings people together.

**yours (proposal paragraph)**

5. Somewhere there's a thing you made about what you believe, and never put out: the page, the song, the long message sitting in drafts. It's ready. But sending it is the argument, so it waits. Send it, where anyone can see it. Then ask people what they think, and mean the question. Some of the answers will surprise you, and you'll find you were never standing up for it alone.
