## explorer-sage · The Genius · *Show Your Working* (draft)

- epigraph: Once, if you asked what was in drinks like this, you'd hear: rum and fruit juice.

**whoYouAre**

Years ago, someone gave you an answer that was perfectly true and told you nothing. You nodded, and kept the question. It has come along ever since, at the back of an ordinary week, and now and then another piece falls into place. Nobody would guess that one of your questions is older than some of your friendships.

You've stopped mentioning it, because people expect a question to be settled or dropped by now. Some take you for a know-it-all. It's nearly the opposite: you can't rest inside something you aren't allowed to understand. What frightens you is being stuck for good with an answer you can't get behind. And once you do understand, you lay all of it out, plainly, so nobody else has to take it on trust. People get the whole answer from you, free, and every year it cost you was worth it.

**yours (proposal paragraph)**

5. One more thing from Berry's story. On his own, he got "rum and fruit juice". When he went in with Ted Haigh, a loud, sociable fellow historian whom one writer called Berry's opposite, he left with a recipe. "The first Don the Beachcomber recipes I got were because of Ted," Berry said. So take the question you've carried longest, and ask it out loud to the person you know who's least like you, the one who'd ask a stranger anything. Ask before you're sure it's a good question. You may get there years sooner, and on the day it all falls into place, you won't be the only one who's glad.


## sage-explorer · The Philosopher · *Look Again* (draft)

- tagline: The more you like an answer, the harder you look at it.
- epigraph: "Gin" began as a short form of the name of the spirit in this glass.
- drink: old-fashioned · built · cognac, genever_oude, rich_syrup, angostura · rocks glass (about 300 ml), filled with ice cubes
- closing: The brandy goes in before the genever. Love the next neat idea, then learn what it started as.

**whoYouAre**

Over dinner, a friend explains a lovely theory about how something famous really began. It's neat, it has a name and a date in it, and you love it at once. Hours later you're still in your chair, turning it over. People take that for doubt. It's closer to the reverse: you fall for ideas faster than anyone you know, which is exactly why you check the ones you love.

What worries you is your own delight: a lovely idea, passed on with confidence, that turns out to be wrong. And when a favourite doesn't survive a closer look, you let it go without a fuss. Nobody sees what that costs you.

You take other people's ideas as seriously as your own. Someone floats a half-thought, and you follow it further than they dared, and they leave feeling cleverer than they came. It's respect, and it means more to them than you know.

**yours (proposal paragraph)**

5. So keep loving the neat answers. Don't change that. Just, next time one delights you, don't save the going back for midnight and your own chair. Go back with the friend who brought it: "I love that. Want to see what's underneath?" Most of the time, what the two of you find is older and bigger than the story you started with, and it's theirs to keep too. Going back stops being the lonely part, and becomes what the evening was for.
