# Fact card: the Knickebein and Leo Engel

Our words throughout. Facts are free; the sources' sentences are not.

## Facts

| # | Claim (our words) | Source | Tier | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| F1 | The Knickebein is a German (and German American) relative of the Pousse Café: layered liqueurs with a whole, unbroken egg yolk floating in them, drunk in a fixed four-step ritual. | Oxford, KNICKEBEIN (pdf 1137) | primary | |
| F2 | Oxford glosses the name as "knee bend". | Oxford, KNICKEBEIN (pdf 1137) | primary | Why it was called that is **not on record**. Don't build a meaning on the name. |
| F3 | Leo Engel, the German American running the American bar at the Criterion in London, was the first to write the ritual down, in 1878 (*American and Other Drinks*). The drink was already about a decade old: a San Francisco saloon advertised it in 1869. | Oxford, KNICKEBEIN (pdf 1137; refs pdf 1138) | primary | **Engel did not invent it.** He wrote the ceremony down. "Leo's Knickebein" = his version. |
| F4 | Engel's version: equal parts orange curaçao, crème de noyaux and maraschino filling the lower two-thirds of a port glass; the yolk floated on top, unbroken; then whipped egg white; a few drops of Angostura on the white. | Oxford, KNICKEBEIN (pdf 1137); Oxford's modern recipe: 15 ml each in a 60 ml stemmed sherry glass (pdf 1138) | primary | Joy (pdf 41) adds that the white was shaped into a pyramid: **Joy only**, secondary. |
| F5 | The four steps, in our words: smell it; suck the froth off with a deep breath; drink a third of the liquid without touching the yolk; then throw the head back and swallow the rest at once, breaking the yolk in your mouth. A pause between each. | Oxford, KNICKEBEIN (pdf 1137, Engel quoted); *Joy*, pdf 41 | primary | The drinker performs the drink. Paraphrase only; never Engel's capitals. |
| F6 | Thirty years later William Boothby (1908) wrote that it was little known in America, that few bartenders could make one, that it had to be drunk by the directions because the drinker gets "four different sensations", and that the bartender's job was to explain it to the uninitiated. | Oxford, KNICKEBEIN (pdf 1137, Boothby quoted) | primary | "Four sensations" is **Boothby's** phrase (1908), not Engel's. |
| F7 | Some later books had the ingredients shaken together like a flip. Oxford calls this a mistake: it keeps the flavours but loses the idea of the drink. | Oxford, KNICKEBEIN (pdf 1137) | primary | **The turn.** The strange thing survived on paper as an ordinary one. Oxford's judgment; our words. |
| F8 | A 1913 German bar book said it could be made many ways and listed fourteen. | Oxford, KNICKEBEIN (pdf 1137; Leybold & Schönfeld, *Lexicon der Getränke*) | primary | Contests Joy's "didn't catch on" (C1). |
| F9 | The physics: any liqueurs work as long as they're denser than the yolk (about 1.025 g/ml). | Oxford, KNICKEBEIN (pdf 1137) | primary | Tomás's lever for a riff. |
| F10 | A floated yolk is older than Engel's book: Jerry Thomas's 1862 "Pousse l'Amour" set a yolk in maraschino, surrounded it with vanilla cordial and topped it with cognac. | Oxford, POUSSE CAFÉ (pdf 1557) | primary | Never "the first drink with a floating yolk". |
| F11 | Engel (1844–1893): born in Germany, in America by 1867, possibly a soap maker first, tending bar in Brooklyn by the early 1870s; by his own account he'd also worked for Jerry Thomas. | Oxford, ENGEL, LEO (pdf 722) | primary | "Worked for Thomas" is **his claim**; Oxford hedges it. |
| F12 | Hired late 1874 to run the American bar in the Criterion, a new theatre-restaurant complex in Piccadilly. The drinks were a sensation, and so was "Professor" Engel. Some were American only in the sound of them; some American visitors weren't convinced. | Oxford, ENGEL, LEO (pdf 722) | primary | A showman in a theatre building. |
| F13 | His 1878 book was mostly copied, nearly word for word, from Jerry Thomas, plus fourteen "Leo's specialties". It became a cornerstone of British mixology. | Oxford, ENGEL, LEO (pdf 724) | primary | Oxford doesn't say the Knickebein was one of the fourteen. **Don't claim it.** |
| F14 | He left in 1882 for his own bar, Leo's Desideratum, which failed in 1884; tried theatrical agency and management; after his wife died (1888) moved to Paris (New York Bar, Place de la Madeleine), then Monte Carlo (Hotel de Paris), where he stayed until he died. The Criterion's bar never got back the popularity it had under him. | Oxford, ENGEL, LEO (pdf 724) | primary | Dossier only unless Wren wants the man. |
| F15 | On Engel's list you could order drinks with names like Galvanic Lip Pouters, Magnetic Crushers, Boston Nose Warmers and Josey Ticklers. | *Joy of Mixology*, pdf 41 | secondary | Whether he coined them isn't said. "You could order", never "he invented". |

## Legends / cautions
- **C1 "It didn't catch on."** Joy (pdf 42) says so, but it was advertised in San Francisco in 1869 and a German book listed fourteen versions in 1913 (F3, F8). What holds: Boothby, 1908, calls it little known **in America**. Say "never caught on in America" at most.
- **C2 "Engel invented it."** No (F3). He wrote the ritual down.
- **C3 "Four sensations" as Engel's words.** Boothby's, 1908 (F6).
- **C4 The name.** Oxford's "knee bend" only; no story about why.
- **Dalí.** The persona's example, not the drink's history. Nothing on record links Dalí to the Knickebein; don't let an egg motif smuggle him in.
- **Raw egg.** Yolk and white are raw: an egg allergen and a food-safety note for Tomás's Checks. Not veto-free as written.
