# Fact card: the New York Sour

| # | Claim (our words) | Source | Tier | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| F1 | A Whisky Sour with a float of red wine on top. The drink dates to the 1880s and is first attested in Chicago (*Chicago Tribune*, 25 Nov 1883): a bartender there shook up a "pleasant-looking, red-headed" sour. | Oxford, NEW YORK SOUR (pdf 1387) | primary | |
| F2 | That bartender said the claret "makes the drink look well and … gives it a better taste", and that men who drank his sours expected a claret at every bar. | Oxford pdf 1387; *Imbibe!* pdf 96 | primary | He claimed to have invented the float. Wondrich is sceptical (he also claimed the Manhattan), and a second Chicago bartender topped a sour with claret that same year. **Never "invented by".** |
| F3 | In saloon language, any red wine was called "claret", however far it came from Bordeaux. | *Imbibe!* pdf 96 | primary | |
| F4 | Names: Continental Sour, Southern Whisky Sour, Greenwich Sour, New York Sour. In Boston it was called a "New York" Sour as early as 1885 (*Boston Herald*, 13 Dec 1885). New York Sour is the name that lasted, and it's the one in general use today. | Oxford pdf 1387; WHISKY SOUR (pdf 2181) | primary | See C1. |
| F5 | Wondrich: the drink "was broadly popular" (no dates given). The float "earned many fans starting in the 1880s". | *Imbibe!* pdf 96; Oxford pdf 2181 | primary | |
| F6 | A New York Sour "calls for rye" (Wondrich). Oxford's recipe: shake 60 ml straight rye, 15 ml lemon, 15 ml simple syrup (and 7 ml orange juice, optional), strain into a chilled cocktail glass, float 15 ml dry red wine, spin the glass gently. | *Imbibe!* pdf 97; Oxford pdf 1387 | primary | **Served up and cold.** |
| F7 | The float is put on with care: over the back of a spoon held at the surface (Imbibe, about ½ oz), or gently, with a spin of the glass (Oxford). Oxford says egg white and ice blur "the sharp line" between the layers. | *Imbibe!* pdf 97; Oxford pdf 1387 | primary | Why the wine floats isn't on either page (C2). |

## Conflicts
- C1. **When the name arrived.** *Imbibe!* (pdf 96) calls New York Sour the name the drink "finally settled on after Prohibition". Oxford (pdf 1387, by the same author) has the name in Boston by 1885. These are compatible (the name existed by 1885; it became the general name later), but "only after Prohibition" is wrong. Say "known by several names; New York Sour is the one that lasted."
- C2. **"It floats because the sour is denser"** is not on either page. Both treat the float as a careful pour. Any physics line needs the Mixologist's numbers, or it goes.
