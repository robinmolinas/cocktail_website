# Fact card: the Pink Lady, Hazel Dawn and the ojen drink (1911)

Verified by Hester, 2026-09-30, for magician-caregiver (The Fairy Godparent). Text read on screen, Oxford Companion PINK LADY COCKTAIL (Wondrich), pdf 1521–1522, primary.

## Facts

|---|---|---|---|
| F1 | The Pink Lady Cocktail was named after the musical comedy *The Pink Lady*, which opened at New York's New Amsterdam theater in 1911. | Oxford pdf 1521 | primary |
| F2 | It starred Hazel Dawn (1890–1988). | Oxford pdf 1521 | primary |
| F3 | Dawn told a correspondent of drinks historian William Grimes that the drink was created for a surprise party in her honour at Murray's Roman Gardens on 42nd Street. | Oxford pdf 1521 | primary (relay) |
| F4 | The channel is four links: Dawn → an unnamed correspondent of Grimes → Grimes → Wondrich. The bibliography line is "Grimes, William. Personal correspondence, February 22, 2009" (i.e. Grimes writing to the entry's author, not an interview with Dawn). | Oxford pdf 1522 | primary |
| F5 | **When** Dawn told it is not on the page. The only bounds are after the 1911 party and before her death in 1988. | Oxford pdf 1521–1522 (absence) | — |
| F6 | The drink served that evening is "probably not" the one that entered the books. The 1911 Pink Lady was a jigger of ojen (a Spanish anisette) shaken with Peychaud's and Angostura bitters. | Oxford pdf 1521; *SF Chronicle*, May 28, 1911, p. 39 (Erhard, "Interesting News from Gotham"), cited in pdf 1522 | primary |
| F7 | In New Orleans, the 1911 ojen drink became known as the "Pink Shimmy". (Undated on the page.) | Oxford pdf 1521 | primary |
| F8 | In 1913 Jacques Straub published a different Pink Lady, gin and applejack (Wondrich: basically a Jack Rose with a splash of gin). It is this version that entered the canon. | Oxford pdf 1521 | primary |
| F9 | Straub's version was then often simplified (citrus dropped, applejack replaced by more gin) and given egg white or cream or both. By the end of Prohibition, in all but the best bars, it was generally gin, grenadine and egg white: "crude but effective". | Oxford pdf 1521 | primary |
| F10 | Oxford's modern Pink Lady recipe (gin, bonded applejack, lime, grenadine, egg white) is Straub's line, not the party's drink. | Oxford pdf 1521 | primary |
| F11 | William Grimes (b. 1950): journalist, *Straight Up or on the Rocks* (1993), New York Times since 1989. | Oxford pdf 950 | primary |
| F12 | Ojen and other anise liqueurs long remained in use in New Orleans absinthe-style recipes after the 1912 US absinthe ban. | Oxford ABSINTHE FRAPPÉ pdf 55 | primary |
| F13 | Ojen was a sweetened anís: the Morales maker added sugar "like all liqueur makers", and Ojén, unlike absinthe, already had it "in sufficient quantities". | François Monti, "Ojén, la absenta española", *Jaibol*, 2 Jan 2021 (named historian) | secondary |
| F14 | In 1911 two Ojén makers were trading: Morales (from 1830, sold c. 1921) and Fernández (brand on the market by the 1910s, last batch c. 1989). Which maker's ojen the party had is unknown. | Monti 2021 | secondary |
| F15 | Sazerac's Legendre Ojen (2016) was reverse-engineered from Fernández bottles over two years; 84 proof (42%), "a very sweet anisette". No sugar figure published. | Seánan Forbes, *Tales of the Cocktail*, 9 Feb 2016 (trade press); 42° also Monti | secondary |
| F16 | Oxford's "anisette" does not by itself mean sweet: MISTRÀ, "an Italian name for anisette", is "sweetened lightly, if at all". Ojen's sweetness rests on F13/F15, not the category word. | Oxford pdf 1316 | primary |
| F17 | Anethole comes out of solution when (especially cold) water is added, turning the liquid cloudy; chill-filtering the spirit prevents it. | Oxford ANISE SPIRITS pdf 115; LOUCHE pdf 1195 | primary |

| F20 | Spain, Real Decreto 164/2014, art. 6 (in force; repealed RD 644/1982): optional label terms for "Anís". **Dulce:** 35–45% vol., total sugars (as sucrose) **over 200 g/l**, no ceiling; essential oils 0.75–1.5 g/l. **Semidulce:** 35–45%, 50–200 g/l; oils 0.75–1.5. **Seco:** 35–50%, sugars **no more than 50 g/l**; oils **1–3 g/l**. **Extraseco:** over 50% to 55%, ≤50 g/l; oils 1.75–3.75 g/l. Art. 5: "Anisette" as a term needs ≥20% and over 200 g/l sugar. | BOE-A-2014-3251 (boe.es consolidated text, read on screen 2026-09-30) | primary (law) |

## Cautions
- C1 **No time word on Dawn's telling.** "Decades later", "years later", "still called it" and "all her life" are inferences (F5). Say "as she told it" / "she said".
- C2 **The drift the page describes belongs to Straub's drink, not the party's.** The 1911 ojen drink did not turn into gin and applejack; a different drink took the name (F6, F8), and that one was simplified and coarsened (F9). Never "the recipe drifted" or "her drink became…". But never "the party's drink never changed" either (Hester's round-2/3 shorthand, struck): by 1935 the Pink Shimmy was stirred, with Peychaud's only (F18).
- C3 Keep Oxford's "probably". The makers and the party's organisers are unknown: invent nobody, give nobody a motive.
- C4 "Ladies' drink" (Oxford's own framing, pdf 1521) stays out; so do the Waldorf (Straub's source; *A Brother's Care*) and the Jack Rose (Genie backup ground).
- C5 "Unfortunately" is Wondrich's judgement, not a fact.
- C6 A labelled anís dulce is legally >200 g/l with no ceiling (F20), so the law does not pin a sugar value; any figure inside that is unsourced unless a bottle states it. A seco may hold up to 50 g/l. The substitute is a sweet Spanish anís (anís dulce), not 'any Spanish anís': secos exist and would falsify the numbers. The 1911 bottle's sugar level is unknown; any g/100 ml is unsourced.
- C7 **The Pink Shimmy name was owned.** By 1935 Solari's claimed the name as copyrighted and exclusive (F19). Never say the drink carried on "with nobody's credit on it" or "nameless" (Hester's own round-2 line, struck). The 1911 drink went on under a New Orleans name; that is all we may say.
- C8 The booklet's "originated in New Orleans" and "imitated throughout the world" are advertising claims; don't repeat them as fact. They don't contradict Oxford's "became known as" (the name, not the drink).
- C9 The 1935 Pink Shimmy is stirred, Peychaud's only, and pink from the bitters; the 1911 drink (Oxford) was shaken with Peychaud's *and* Angostura. Don't merge the two recipes.
