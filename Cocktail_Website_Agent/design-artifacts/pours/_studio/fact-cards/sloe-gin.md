## Veto ruling
- **Generic "sloe gin (style)" → `nuts`.** The style's own making allows crushed stones (S5) or added almonds (S5, S6), and sold bottles taste of almond (S8). Same reasoning as maraschino. The safe side can't rule them out for an unnamed bottle.
- **Veto-free only as a named bottle** whose own making is on record without crushed stones or added almond. An absence on a label page isn't enough: it needs a positive statement.

### Ruling on "continues" (Wren's ask c)
Pdf 1814 gives three things: households made it "since the nineteenth century"; it "continues" to be made from recipes "handed down within generations"; and, in between, decades off the drinks menus. Read together they support **home-making in Britain from the 1800s to now, handed down**, with the menu decades inside that span. So an order-neutral pairing is fair: *the drinks menus dropped it for decades; in British rural households the recipes went on being handed down.* Not fair: any cause or rescue ("kept it alive", "saved it", "brought it back"), "the trade forgot" (menus only), "outlasted the Fizz", or a family-by-family claim that someone made it *during* a particular decade (the page never says so of any household).

### Asked and not found (Wren's ask a)
No named handing-down on any page read. A WebSearch summary claimed a 2016 championship winner used "a family recipe dating back to 1842" and a Suffolk recipe "handed down from a neighbour's great aunt": **unverified search-summary claims, not usable** (search summaries misattribute; the 2016 winner page I read says nothing of a family recipe). The pour's web budget (2) is spent. Unread lead for later: Oxford's bibliography cites Edward Spencer, *Cakes and Ale* (1897), public domain.
