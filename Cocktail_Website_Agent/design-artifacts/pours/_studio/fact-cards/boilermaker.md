## Conflicts

- **C1. The 1896 Herald story isn't on the page we could read.** Oxford cites "A False Alarm", *New York Herald*, 5 April 1896, p. 4. Chronicling America is behind a Cloudflare wall. Newspapers.com's scan of that issue (32 pages, publication 4528) was read as OCR through the Yonkers open-access API: page 4 (page id 163766420; 41 paragraphs, saved at `work/jester-regular-guy/historian-evidence-herald-1896-p4-ocr.txt`) has no such piece, and no page of the issue has a saloon story matching "alarm", "queer", "whisk" or "consternat". The nearest thing, page 30, is a different comic sketch (an excise inspector and a whiskey-drunk cat). So it's an OCR gap, another edition, or a date slip. **What this means:** the 1896 order stands on Oxford alone (Wondrich, library primary), which is enough for the fact as Oxford states it. Nothing beyond Oxford's sentence may be told: no headline gloss, no plot, no "comedy", no reaction beyond "consternation" and the regular's remark. Robin could ask a library for the Herald microfilm if he ever wants the text.
- **C2. The Spring column's date.** Oxford: 12 December 1933. The page in the archive is dated 6 December 1933, and its own items agree (a "NEW YORK, Dec. 6" dateline; a "ten years ago" item headed "DEC. 6, 1923"). Full-text search of the paper for "Boilermaker and His Helper", 1930–1940, finds only this page. Oxford's date is probably a slip, or the column ran twice. **Say "December 1933"**, never a day.
- **C3. Wren's r1 wording, checked against the page.** (a) "Writes it up as comedy" and "a whole bar was in uproar over nothing" aren't on Oxford's page: the record is "consternation". (b) "Only odd in that room": Oxford says American drinkers in general thought beer an outlandish chaser, so it was odd on that side of the Atlantic, not just in one saloon. (c) "Forty years on it was the plainest order in the bar": the name arrives in the 1930s (F8) and the popularity in the 1950s–60s (F10). "Plainest order" is our phrase.
- **C4. Who coined it.** Nobody, on record. Spring lists it as Ray's specialty: that makes neither man its namer. Oxford calls Spring's form "the original version" and cites the column for it; "the first printing" is our inference from that, so never "first".

## Legends

- **L1.** The union page's Trevithick story (an 1801 steam carriage left burning outside a Cornish pub) is told as "perhaps", with no link to the drink. Legend, and not ours.
- **L2.** Union and web pages say the name comes from boilermakers drinking it after shifts. Nobody's on record saying so: at most "the story goes", and the plan's guard (no namer) stands.
