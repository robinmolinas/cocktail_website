# Fact card: the Kentucky rickhouse

Our words throughout. Quotes under 15 words, copied from pages on screen in the same session (Oxford Companion, primary: BARREL pdf 213, BARREL ROTATION pdf 218, BARREL STORAGE SYSTEMS pdf 219-220, RICKHOUSE pdf 1665, WHISKY, BOURBON pdf 2138-2140). ## Facts

| # | Claim (our words) | Source | Tier | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| F1 | "Rickhouse" is a common name for a warehouse where bourbon and other American whisky age. The word comes from "rick", the name for the wooden structure that holds up the barrels. | Oxford RICKHOUSE pdf 1665 | primary | The building is named after the barrels' support. "Named after its frame" is only true where the racks are the frame (F4). |
| F2 | In Kentucky the rickhouse system is used: barrels rest in vertical wooden racks inside multistory warehouses. | pdf 219 | primary | Present tense on the page ("is used"): limb (3), still in use. |
| F3 | The racks can be up to seven stories high, each story three barrels high. | pdf 219 | primary | Conflicts with pdf 218 (C1). No count in guest text. |
| F4 | In the simplest rickhouses the racks are all connected to form the building's inner core, the floors are fixed to that framework, and the whole is covered with a skin of brick, wood or corrugated tin. | pdf 219-220 | primary | "The simplest rickhouses" only: never every rickhouse. The skin may be any of the three. |
| F5 | In a rickhouse the barrels lie with their bellies to the aisles (pyramid stacks turn their heads to the aisles), so a barrel can easily be rolled out and moved, for instance from the very hot top floor to the cooler bottom. | pdf 220 | primary | "Can", not "are": see C2. |
| F6 | That makes balanced ageing possible, and so does the great amount of air that moves through a rickhouse. | pdf 220 | primary | The air serves the ageing, not the structure. Never "the air holds it up". |
| F7 | Palletised storage, a modern exception finding more and more use (e.g. Irish distillers and several Canadian ones): four to nine barrels stand upright on a wooden pallet, strapped, and pallets are stacked. They can only be moved by machine, and pack very tightly, saving space, at the expense of air circulation. | pdf 220 | primary | A trade-off on the page, never a failure or "cheap". "Irish distillers" as Oxford says, not "all Irish". |
| F8 | Older systems: pyramid stacking (from ships' holds; still used in France and several European countries, two to five high; one barrel is hard to move without taking the pyramid apart, but every bung is reachable, so spirit is pumped out, as in a solera) and Scotland's dunnage (three high on wooden runners, for leaving a cask until it's ready). Both suit low warehouses, where position matters little. | pdf 219 | primary | The page's only date ("back to the origins of barrel aging") is pyramid stacking's, not the rickhouse's. |
| F9 | Barrel rotation means moving barrels during maturation, mostly in whisky. Floors differ in heat and humidity; rotation evens out the cool bottom and the hot, dry top, so the whiskies come out as alike as possible. It is labour-heavy. | pdf 218 | primary | |
| F10 | Rotation is not widely used, especially in non-palletised warehouses, because moving heavy barrels by hand is laborious and risky. Other routes to even ageing: tightly spaced single-story warehouses, and heated ones. | pdf 218 | primary | Tight packing is also a legitimate route to evenness: never "tight is wrong". |
| F11 | For storing spirits, wood is always better than metal, because sparks are dangerous in air full of alcohol vapour. | pdf 219 | primary | Sits in the pyramid paragraph but is stated generally. Context only. |
| F12 | Bourbon need not be made in Kentucky, against popular belief, but Kentucky makes the vast majority; 9.3 million barrels were ageing in Kentucky warehouses in January 2020 (Kentucky Distillers' Association, via Oxford). | pdf 2138 | primary (relaying KDA) | Warehouses, not "rickhouses": the page doesn't say how many are rickhouses. |
| F13 | Bourbon must be stored in a new, charred oak container; in Kentucky's hot, relatively dry summers it generally gets stronger as it ages, because water evaporates before alcohol. "Straight" bourbon has at least two years in oak. | pdf 2140 | primary | "Generally". |
| F14 | A standard bourbon barrel holds 200 litres; full barrels can be stacked five or six high. Oxford calls the barrel one of the most successful pieces of design ever conceived. | pdf 213 | primary | The superlative is Oxford's judgment and a design word (Wren's trap): dossier only. |

## Guards
- G1 No designer, inventor or date (limb 1 is our inference, and lives in the guest's scene).
- G2 "The simplest rickhouses" for the frame-as-core; never every rickhouse, never every bourbon.
- G3 The racks are wood (F2); never "steel frame".
- G4 The air balances the ageing (F6); it holds nothing up. The gaps in a rickhouse come with the open racks: they are built in, not unplanned.
- G5 Pallets: space saved, air given up (F7). A trade-off; and tight single-story warehouses are another sound way to even ageing (F10).
- G6 No claim that the guest's bottle aged in a rickhouse or was rotated.
- G7 Bourbon isn't Kentucky's by definition (F12).
