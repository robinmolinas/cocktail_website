# Fact card: the Vieux Carré and Walter Bergeron (Hotel Monteleone, New Orleans)

Written by Hester, 2026-10-01, for ruler-regular-guy (The People's Representative). Reuse these rows as they stand; don't re-tell them differently.

## Facts

| ID | Fact | Source | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| F1 | The Vieux Carré is "a signature cocktail" of New Orleans's Monteleone Hotel. Oxford calls it "a Manhattan variant". | Oxford, VIEUX CARRÉ, pdf 2102 | primary (library) |
| F2 | Created "in the 1930s" by "then–head bartender Walter Bergeron (1889–1947)", who "had worked at the bar before Prohibition and returned upon Repeal". | Oxford pdf 2102 | primary (library) |
| F3 | Its recipe "first appeared in print in 1937" (Oxford's bibliography: Stanley Clisby Arthur, *Famous New Orleans Drinks and How to Mix 'Em*, 1937). | Oxford pdf 2102 | primary (library) |
| F4 | Name: "named for the French Quarter"; it "translates as 'old square'". Oxford does **not** say who chose the name. | Oxford pdf 2102 | primary (library) |
| F5 | Oxford's recipe: 30 ml each straight rye, cognac and sweet vermouth, 5 ml Bénédictine, 2 dashes each Angostura and Peychaud's; stirred, strained over fresh ice in a large rocks glass; lemon peel and a cherry. This is a modern spec. | Oxford pdf 2102 | primary (library) |
| F6 | Arthur's 1937 recipe (1984 reprint): ½ teaspoon Bénédictine, 1 dash Peychaud's, 1 dash Angostura, ⅓ jigger rye whiskey, ⅓ jigger cognac brandy, ⅓ jigger Italian vermouth; "The benedictine is used…" (cut off). **Three equal thirds, the third being Italian vermouth.** | archive.org `famousneworleans0000arth`, full-text OCR highlight | primary content, reprint; OCR highlight, incomplete |
| F7 | Arthur: "This is the cocktail that Walter Bergeron, head bartender of the Hotel Monteleone cocktail lounge, takes special pride…" (cut off: what follows isn't read). | same | OCR highlight, incomplete |
| F8 | Joy: "invented in the 1930s by Walter Bergeron, then the head bartender at the Monteleone Hotel"; named after the French term for the French Quarter, "the old square"; ¾ oz each rye, brandy, sweet vermouth; filed "French-Italian: whiskey and brandy". | *Joy of Mixology*, pdf 402–403 | secondary (library) |
| F9 | The city's switch: an 1885 *Times-Democrat* interview has a bartender call "brandy drinking in New Orleans… a thing of the past" and say whisky "displaced it". Wondrich: after the Civil War "the old Creole city became much more Americanized"; "Before the war, New Orleans was a Brandy Julep town"; by then whiskey was drunk in Whiskey Cocktails or "the new Manhattan". | *Imbibe!*, pdf 182 | primary (library) |

## Cautions

| ID | Caution |
|---|---|
| C1 | **Bergeron's own side isn't on record** in anything read. Never "the side that won pours the side that lost back in" *about him*. Talk about the city's drinks: the brandy it drank and the whiskey that replaced it. |
| C2 | **The 50-year gap.** F9's switch is the 1860s–1885; the drink is the 1930s, after Prohibition. No page links the two, and none says cognac was out of favour in the 1930s. The link is *our reading*, signposted, and never a live quarrel at the time of the drink. |
| C3 | **Who named it is unknown** (F4). Say "it carries the old quarter's name", not "he named it for…". |
| C4 | **Three equal parts, not two** (F5, F6): rye, cognac and vermouth at the same measure. Never "half rye, half cognac". "Each at full measure" is true; "the two spirits split the glass" is not. |
| C5 | **The hotel's legend is out of guest text.** Secondaries relay the hotel's own story: "according to the Hotel Monteleone, was an homage to the different ethnic groups of New Orleans" (Seidman, *Alchemy in a Glass*, OCR highlight); "It was created as a tribute to the…" (Pearce, *Drink Dat, New Orleans*, 2017, OCR highlight, cut off). Brand-tier, no 1930s source, and it lands on Wren's race trap. Dossier note only: a guest who looks it up will meet this version. |
| C6 | **Year.** Several guidebooks say "1938", which can't be right if the recipe was printed in 1937 (F3). Say "the 1930s". |
| C7 | **Motive** isn't on record (F7 is cut off before any reason). Signpost any "why". |
| C8 | Walter Bergeron (1889–1947) is never Victor "Trader Vic" Bergeron (Oxford pdf 258). |
| C9 | Bitters: 1 dash each in 1937, 2 each in Oxford's modern spec. Peychaud's and Angostura together also sit in O'Reilly's Sazerac (*Imbibe!* pdf 182): keep the Sazerac out of the reading. |
| C10 | "Nearly forgotten locally, even in the hotel" (pdf 2102) stays out (Wren's guard). |

**C3 and C7 revised (my own cautions, struck as mine):** C7's "motive isn't on record" was wrong: F7 was only cut off. The motive for *the drink* is on record (F12: to honour the Vieux Carré, as he says). The motive for *pairing the two spirits* still isn't. C3 stands narrowed: no page says in so many words who chose the name, but F12 makes "he made it in honour of the old quarter" sayable. **C14:** "rye whiskey", never "American rye", for Bergeron's glass: Arthur's recipe says only "rye whiskey", and the bottle's origin in the 1930s isn't on the page.

**F12 extended (round 5):** the sentence runs on "…where the antique shops and the iron lace balconies give sightseers a glimpse into the romance…" (both books' OCR highlights), and then the recipe follows ("Vieux Carré Cocktail, ½ teaspoon benedictine…"). The passage before the recipe gives the honour to the quarter and describes the quarter. Nothing in it explains the pairing of rye and cognac. The note after the recipe ("The benedictine is used…") is still unread.
