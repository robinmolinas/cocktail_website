# Fact card: Clara Bell Walsh and the cocktail party

Our words throughout. Facts are free; the sources' sentences are not. The spine is one library page: Oxford Companion, COCKTAIL PARTY (pdf 512, Eric Felten), read in full on the page on 2026-09-30. It cites the *Wichita Beacon*, 3 April 1917, p. 12 ("A Cocktail Party: Mrs. Walsh of St. Louis Has Hit on a New Feature in Way of Entertainment"). I couldn't read any 1917 paper: Chronicling America and loc.gov returned Cloudflare challenges.

| F10 | **The Bronx.** Oxford: essentially a Perfect Martini with added orange juice "(or other orange flavor)"; "the cocktail sensation of the 1900s and 1910s"; in the US, the drink that "took the cocktail from the barroom to the sideboard", selling untold numbers of cocktail shakers. It fell hard after Repeal: *Esquire* put it first on its list of the ten worst cocktails of 1934 (Dec 1934, p. 40). It hasn't come back since, whether because it's hard to balance (too much orange and it's "insipid", too little and it's a neither-one-thing-nor-the-other Perfect Martini) or because even balanced it's "light and pleasant", "not a spectacular one". Oxford's recipe: muddle an orange wheel cut in eight pieces, 60 ml London dry gin, 15 ml each sweet and dry vermouth, 2 dashes Angostura, shake, strain into a chilled cocktail glass. | Oxford, BRONX COCKTAIL (pdf 358–359, Wondrich) | primary | **Invention contested:** "at least eleven" claimants; the earliest, John E. "Remained high during Prohibition": out (Outlaw ground). **Recipe note for Tomás:** Oxford's own recipe muddles a wheel; it doesn't use juice. **Coincidence to own, if used:** the drink that took the cocktail into people's homes was the drink of the moment at the first party named after the cocktail. |

| F12 | **The Bronx's weakness problem (Wondrich).** It broke through in 1907. The usual complaint is summed up in Zoë Akins's 1913 play *Papa*: "He looks as weak as a Bronx cocktail." Put in enough orange juice to taste it and the drink is weak; leave it out and it's a Perfect Martini. Wondrich calls Malloy "a trimmer, trying to have both the orange and the strength". | *Imbibe!* pdf 237–238 | primary (library) | **So peel with a little juice is not new.** It's the oldest recorded answer. ## Legends
- **L1. "An instant hit"; "within weeks … a St. Louis institution".** Wikipedia (Cocktail party, fetched as wikitext 2026-09-30) attributes both to a *St. Paul Pioneer Press* article of May 1917, citing Felten's 2007 *WSJ* column, and I couldn't read past that column's opening. Unread primary, tertiary relay: **legend-grade, not an anchor, not in the reading.** If Robin can get the column or the 1917 paper, it could be promoted.
- **L2. Alec Waugh invented the cocktail party in 1920s London.** Oxford calls it apocryphal (pdf 512), and Felten (2007) says Waugh himself named Nevinson, 1924, as the first in England. Out.
- **L3. The baby party was held at the St. Louis Country Club.** Web only (VinePair, Mashed, via the family plan; search summary 2026-09-30). Not on Oxford's page. Out.
- **L4. Church-goers came straight from Sunday service.** Web only (plan). Out.
- **L5. Her later divorce; "breaking social taboos".** Web only. Out (plan).

## Conflicts
- **C1. Was there a meal?** Oxford: "dinner" served at one. Curtis 2025: light bites, no meal. **Say only the hour, noon to one.**
- **C2. How fast it caught on.** Oxford gives no pace, only "enduring success". The "within weeks" line is legend-grade (L1). **No pace in guest text.**
- **C3. Which newspaper.** Oxford: "a local newspaper", citing the *Wichita Beacon* (3 April 1917). Laura Carlson (*Atlas Obscura*, 11 July 2017, via her own site's excerpt; Atlas Obscura itself returned 403) cites a *Tacoma Times* article of April 1917. Wikipedia cites the *St. Paul Pioneer Press*, May 1917. The story evidently ran in several papers. **Never name one, and never call it "the local paper".**
