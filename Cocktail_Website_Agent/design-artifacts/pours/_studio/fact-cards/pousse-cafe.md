## Facts
| id | fact (our words) | source | tier | notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| F1 | A Pousse Café is an after-dinner drink of coloured cordials and liqueurs poured one on top of another, so they sit in bands of colour. | S1 pdf 1557 | library | |
| F2 | The bartender usually pours each one slowly over the back of an upturned bar spoon, so it settles gently on the one below, making a very thin band (Oxford's comparison is a pencil). | S1 pdf 1557; sample recipe pdf 1559 ("slowly and steadily") | library | "no thicker than a pencil" is Oxford's phrase: paraphrase in guest text. |
| F3 | Nothing holds the bands apart but their different weights (specific gravities). The heaviest goes in first; liqueurs, made heavier by their sugar, usually go below the spirits. | S1 pdf 1557; S2 pdf 1169 | library | |
| F4 | The sweetest liqueurs are the densest; unsweetened spirits such as brandy and vodka are the lightest "because of their high alcohol content" (so a spirit's lightness is its alcohol, not only its missing sugar), mix in easily, are hard to layer, and usually go on top. | S2 pdf 1169 | library | The physics of "a rushed pour runs them together" (Tomás's method claim) rests here and on F2's "slowly". |
| F5 | There is a second way: pour the lightest first and run each heavier liquid down through the middle, which pushes the others up on top; Oxford says both methods work. | S2 pdf 1169 | library | "sometimes with the help of special gadgets": a home method needs Tomás's check. |
| F6 | Every recipe has to be tested, because the weights vary; Oxford's sample adds that gravities "may be lightened with vodka". Regan: the same liqueur from two producers need not weigh the same. | S1 pdf 1559; S4 pdf 153 | library | Knowing the rule doesn't tell you the result: you test. |
| F7 | The name is French, literally "push coffee": at first it meant any liqueur or spirit taken after dinner to follow the coffee, a use dating to at least the late eighteenth century in Paris. | S1 pdf 1557 | library | |
| F8 | When the name came to mean a layered drink is unclear. German immigrant bartenders, from a country with a long habit of layering liqueurs, may have influenced it ("It is possible"). | S1 pdf 1557 | library | Keep "possible". Never a date for the first layered one. |
| F9 | Late nineteenth-century recipes usually had three to six coloured layers. In the early twentieth century six and seven became normal, and Oxford reports hearing of eleven and fourteen, built as showpieces of the bartender's steady hand. | S1 pdf 1558 | library | "one hears of" stays Oxford's (report). The steady hand is the Entertainer's ground. |
| F10 | By 1903 Daly's bartender's guide called for its own glass: small, stemmed, tightly flared, about 45 ml. | S1 pdf 1558 | library | |
| F11 | Prohibition largely stopped it; after Repeal it was a mainstay of the American bar again; after the Second World War it began a slow slide into obscurity. | S1 pdf 1558 | library | |
| F12 | In 1972 someone wrote to Amy Vanderbilt's etiquette column: "I remember when bars used to serve a fascinating drink called a Pousse-cafe… . You never see them any more. What happened?" | S1 pdf 1558 | library | "Fascinating" is the letter-writer's word. The writer is unnamed. |
| F13 | Vanderbilt's answer quoted "the proprietor of New York's Le Mistral" (what kind of place it was isn't on the page): "Today these drinks are being shaken before being served either straight or over the rocks in an Old Fashioned glass." | S1 pdf 1558 | library | **The answer is Le Mistral's proprietor's, relayed by Vanderbilt**, and it is one New York owner's observation, not a survey. |
| F14 | Oxford: the classic was "still honored in New Orleans, though", where bartender Nick Castrogiovannoni (1893-1979) of Nick's Big Train Bar "enjoyed local fame for making dozens nightly and achieving as many as thirty-four layers". | S1 pdf 1558 | library | **Undated**: the page gives no years for his bar work. "As many as" is Oxford's. No motive on the page. |
| F15 | His obituary ran as "Famed Mixologist 'Mr. Nick' Is Dead", *Times-Picayune*, 8 Nov 1979, p. 20. | S1 bibliography pdf 1559 | library (citation title) | Obituary text unread (not found via ypl.newspapers.com). "Mr. Nick" is the headline's name for him. |
| F16 | In 1980 the *Washington Evening Star* advised readers that asking for a Pousse Café was a way to stump a bartender, who had likely not made one "in a long time, if ever". | S1 pdf 1559 | library | |
| F17 | Oxford says the modern cocktail revival hasn't taken it up, and offers two possible reasons: Fougner's 1940 line that it succeeds only by its appeal to the eye, or that classic recipes are hard to remake because the liqueurs' weights have changed over the decades. | S1 pdf 1559 | library | Two hypotheses ("whether because … or because"): never state either as the cause. |
| F18 | Exception on the page: Anvil, in Houston, serves some thirty a week. | S1 pdf 1559; S5 (Alex Negranza's estimate) | library + secondary | Anvil is Heugel's bar (*regular-guy-ruler*, card `bobby-heugel-lime-crisis-2014.md` F12). |
| F19 | Negranza, in 2016: "What initially started as a fun drink to have guests order from one of our bartenders quickly turned into a drink that people were coming in for." | S5 | secondary | Relayed by *Punch*. Overlap with Anvil owned if used. |
| F20 | *Punch* relays a *New York Times* item of 1903, "A POUSSE CAFÉ SACRILEGE": after a bartender spent a full fifteen minutes on a layered one, the customer stirred it with a straw and drank it down. | S5 | secondary (relay of NYT 1903) | The original unread. Its joke is at the customer's expense ("an air of the provincial"). |
| F21 | David Wondrich, to *Punch*, on drinking one: "Oh, there's the Cognac. Oh, there's the maraschino." His verdict: "Stupid." A 1941 guide (Crosby Gaige's) answered the other way: "The successive sips give varying taste thrills." | S5 | secondary | Both relayed. Gaige is *Curtain Call*'s man (card `crosby-gaige.md`): unnamed if used. |
| F22 | Joy: layered drinks "display the height of showmanship of the bartender". | S4 pdf 153 | library | Entertainer ground: dossier only. |
| F23 | The Grasshopper began mid-century as a kind of Pousse Café (equal crème de menthe, crème de cacao and cream); the B-52 shot is called its legacy. | S3 pdf 938; S1 pdf 1559 | library | Lineage if Tomás wants it. Cream = not in our drink. |

## Counterweights (carry into the room)
| id | counterweight |
|---|---|
| C1 | **The record says it was for the eye, not the tongue**: Fougner 1940 (F17), Wondrich's "Stupid" (F21), *Punch*'s "the eye before the palate". The drink has to answer this in the glass, and the reading must not claim the classic tasted good. |
| C2 | **It became a joke on the customer** (F20, F16; *Punch*: "the universal emblem of terrible customers"). The Illusionist's fear is exactly someone left feeling foolish; the 1903 story is that fear with the joke on the guest. Use only as the fear, never laughing at the drinker. |
| C3 | **Knowing the rule isn't knowing the result** (F6, F17): each recipe must be tested, bottles differ, and old recipes don't layer the same today. That supports "unintended results" and keeps "knowing how doesn't undo it" honest: the principle is known, the outcome is still checked. |
| C4 | **Nick's numbers are fame, not a measured record**: "enjoyed local fame for", "as many as" (F14). *Punch* calls the 34 "record-holding": don't. And nothing says why he did it, or for whom. |
| C5 | **No date for Nick's bar.** Oxford's "though" sets New Orleans against the decline, but gives no year. Never "in 1972, while New York shook them, Nick…". |
| C6 | **The steady hand, practice, showmanship, flame** (F9, F22, the flaming brandy top on pdf 1558) are the Entertainer's ground (*Cold Water First*). |
| C7 | **Family owners in the sources**: Eben Klemm (*On One Condition*) made a six-layer one at Rebelle in 2015 (S5); Crosby Gaige (*Curtain Call*); Fougner (*The Long Answer*); Anvil (*regular-guy-ruler*); Jerry Thomas, the Pousse l'Amour, the Knickebein, Baker's "rainbow-hued" (plan guards). None named in this pour; Anvil at most as "a Houston bar", owned. |
