## Facts: the 1806 cock-tail (library)
| ID | Fact (our words) | Source | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| F1 | A "glass of cocktail" appears in 1803 in a humour item in the *Farmer's Cabinet* (Amherst, NH), with no ingredients given. It's the first on record. **Croswell's is not the first cocktail mention.** | *Imbibe!* pdf 168 | primary (library) |
| F2 | Croswell was "the controversial editor and writer" of the *Balance and Columbian Repository* of Hudson, New York, a political paper (Federalist, against the Democrats). | *Imbibe!* pdf 169 | primary (library) |
| F3 | On 6 May 1806 he printed a "snarky little item" at the expense of the losing candidate in a local election, laid out as a ledger: under "Gain", "Nothing"; under loss, besides the election, a list of drinks including "411 glasses bitters" and "25 [glasses] cock-tail". The list also had rum and brandy Grogs and Gin Slings. | *Imbibe!* pdf 169 | primary (library) |
| F4 | Wondrich does not know whether the drinks list was real ("at least a realistic one"). Candidates then bought drinks for voters (his gloss). | *Imbibe!* pdf 169 | primary (library) |
| F5 | A reader wrote in, printed on 13 May: he had never heard of cock-tail, and asked several questions (is it local, is it new, what does the name mean), the last a jibe of its own about that party's drinkers being turned upside down. Croswell's answer "chiefly focused on" that last one. Whether the letter was genuine: unknown. | *Imbibe!* pdf 169 | primary (library) |
| F6 | The answer: cock-tail is "a stimulating liquor, composed of spirits of any kind, sugar, water, and bitters"; "vulgarly called bittered sling"; an "electioneering potion" that makes the heart "stout and bold" while it "fuddles the head". Then: "It is said also" to help a Democratic candidate, because whoever has swallowed a glass "is ready to swallow anything else". **The jibe is framed as hearsay ("It is said").** | *Imbibe!* pdf 169 | primary (library) |
| F7 | Wondrich: still "one of the most famous and oft-repeated quotations" in American drinking history; the definition, with one or two small changes, described what people meant by a Cocktail for four generations. | *Imbibe!* pdf 169 | primary (library) |
| F8 | Eleven days later the *Sun* of Pittsfield, Massachusetts, a Democratic paper, printed **a letter** taking Croswell to task for "various political crimes", with a swipe at him for "publishing grog stories" and remarks on "cock tail". | *Imbibe!* pdf 169 | primary (library) |
| F9 | Oxford: the first unambiguous appearance in print of the name and the formula together is Hudson, 1806. A 1798 London "Cock-Tail" was probably a non-alcoholic ginger drink. Oxford's bibliography credits Croswell, "Communication", 13 May 1806. | Oxford COCK-TAIL pdf 493–494 | primary (library) |
| F10 | Oxford's Cock-Tail recipe: small Old-Fashioned glass; muddle 1 raw sugar cube with 10 ml water and 3 dashes bitters; add 60 ml brandy, genever or young whisky, and cold water to taste; stir. **No rum, no ice named.** | Oxford pdf 494 | primary (library) |
| F11 | The early Cock-Tail was mainly an eye-opener or morning drink; the formula settled by 1820 and had spread across the US by 1830. | Oxford pdf 493–494 | primary (library) |
| F12 | Jerry Thomas's 1862 cocktails were "more or less as Harry Croswell would've understood them, give or take some lumps of ice". | *Imbibe!* pdf 176 | primary (library) |

## Facts: People v. Croswell (MTSU, secondary)
| ID | Fact (our words) | Source | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| M1 | *People v. Croswell*, 3 Johns. Cas. 337 (N.Y. 1804): New York's Supreme Court upheld Croswell's libel conviction for publishing an article **alleging** that President Jefferson had paid James Callender to make derogatory statements about Washington and Adams. | MTSU | secondary |
| M2 | At trial, the lower court "refused to allow him to prove the truth of what he had said **or his intent** in publishing it". | MTSU | secondary |
| M3 | Alexander Hamilton "handled Croswell's appeal". His idea of a free press: "the right to publish, with impunity, truth, with good motives, and for justifiable ends" (14 words; it continues to government, magistracy or individuals). | MTSU | secondary |
| M4 | Judge James Kent's opinion argued that truth should be a defence to libel, and that the jury should have decided whether the words were criminal. Kent endorsed Hamilton's idea. | MTSU | secondary |
| M5 | Vile's gloss: by Hamilton's definition, a person **could still be convicted for true statements made with bad motives**. | MTSU | secondary |
| M6 | The court's opinion was divided, and that "technically left" the conviction in place. | MTSU | secondary |
| M7 | "By the time of the ruling", the state had **already** adopted a law that "effectively incorporated elements" of Kent's opinion. **No year for the law on the page, and no "because of Croswell".** | MTSU | secondary |
| M8 | Kent's opinion was widely quoted and, per Berns (1970), more influential through the 19th and early 20th centuries than Jefferson's and Madison's views. | MTSU | secondary |
| M9 | The page's picture caption calls him "Rev. Dr. Harry Croswell", pictured c. 1835: he later became a clergyman. Caption only; nothing else on the page. | MTSU (caption) | secondary, thin |
