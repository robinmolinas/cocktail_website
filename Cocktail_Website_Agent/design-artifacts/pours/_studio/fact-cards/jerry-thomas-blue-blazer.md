# Fact card: Jerry Thomas's Blue Blazer (the throw, the cold-water practice, the crowd)

Written by Hester, 2026-10-01, for magician-regular-guy (The Entertainer). Every line read on screen in the same session. Companion card: `jerry-thomas-improved-cocktail.md` (Thomas is *Beside the First*'s man; that card's line 46 left the Blazer unused). Quotes are for the dossier only; the reading paraphrases.

## Facts
| id | fact | source (tier) | scope / guard |
|---|---|---|---|
| F1 | The Blue Blazer is a hot Scotch-whisky Toddy, set alight and poured back and forth between mugs "in as broad an arc as the maker can manage". | Oxford, BLUE BLAZER pdf 297 (Wondrich), primary | "Strongly associated with Jerry Thomas" is Oxford's phrase. Never "he invented it" (C2). |
| F2 | Thomas, 1862: "The novice in mixing this beverage should be careful not to scald himself. To become proficient in throwing the liquid from one mug to the other, it will be necessary to practise for some time with cold water." | Imbibe! pdf 142 (Wondrich quoting Thomas 1862), primary; same sentence in Oxford ROCKS pdf 1672 ("practice … from one mug to another") | Advice **to the novice**, not a record of Thomas's own practice. "For some time" gives no length: never "hours", "years" or "alone" as his. |
| F3 | Thomas "provides little guidance as to how to pull off this feat but makes one salient observation": the cold-water line. | Oxford, ROCKS pdf 1672, primary | Oxford's judgment of how little method he gave. Say "hardly any instructions, except…", not "only". Entry's author is Hero's man: cite as Oxford, never name him. |
| F4 | Thomas said the throw gave "the appearance of a continued stream of liquid fire". | Oxford, ROCKS pdf 1672, primary | His description of the look, 1862 book implied (not dated on the page). |
| F5 | The 1862 book carries an engraving of the Blazer being mixed, "probably the first time it was shown in print"; Thomas kept a photograph of himself making one over his bar. | Oxford pdf 297, pdf 1672 ("probably"); Imbibe! pdf 142 | Keep "probably". The photograph reads as showing off: dossier only (Wren's motive trap). |
| F6 | Chicago, 1883: "This drink is seldom called for over a first-class bar … It is a great country drink, as the 'jays' think more of watching the blaze than they do of the drink." | Oxford pdf 298 ("sniffed"), Imbibe! pdf 142 ("noted"); original: "The Barkeeper", *Chicago Tribune*, Nov 25, 1883 (Oxford bibliography pdf 299) | **A sneer.** Its target is the drink and its country customers. "Jays" (rubes) never in the bartender's voice (Wren). Paraphrase: "a Chicago bartender sniffed that out in the country people cared more for watching the blaze than for the drink". |
| F7 | By the 1880s the flamboyant style was going out of fashion, and the Blazer with it. Matt Higgins, "a cocky young New York mixologist of the new school", would mix it out of the customer's sight. | Oxford pdf 298, primary | **No reason of his own is on the page**, only the fashion context. "Cocky" is Oxford's label. Dossier only. |
| F8 | Kansas City, 1883: "There used to be a dozen men in Kansas City who thought nothing of doing that, but you never see them now"; a Main Street bartender tried it "for fun and nearly burned his hand off". | Imbibe! pdf 142 | The burn stays in the dossier (Wren). The "dozen men" line is usable. |
| F9 | Bartenders made it "with everything but Scotch (rum and brandy were particularly popular)"; popular "through the 1890s"; "by 1900, it was effectively dead, not to be revived for another hundred years". | Imbibe! pdf 142 | Oxford gives "from the 1860s until the beginning of the twentieth century" (pdf 297). Say "for the rest of the century". |
| F10 | It returned in the cocktail revival as "a popular and highly visual way for bartenders to demonstrate their skill", and now lives mostly as "a late-night spectacle". | Oxford pdf 297, 298 | "Demonstrate their skill" leans to showing off: never as the guest's motive. |
| F11 | Thomas's 1862 book called for Scotch alone; the 1863 recipe (via Campbell 1867) mixes Scotch and Irish, with sugar, boiling water and lemon skin, "Serve in small bar tumblers". | Imbibe! pdf 142 | "Small bar tumblers" is the **1863** recipe's, not 1862's. |
| F12 | Caol Ila is "the brand Thomas had in his cellars". | Imbibe! pdf 142, Wondrich's aside, no source given | Say "Wondrich says" or "the historian David Wondrich writes". |
| F13 | To light, the whisky must be preheated or over 54% ABV (Oxford pdf 298); Wondrich: "over 52 percent abv or thereabouts" (Imbibe! pdf 142). | both | Irrelevant unlit. No figure in guest text (C4). |
| F14 | Throwing needs no fire; it was "the proto-technique for shaking", nearly abandoned in the 20th century, kept alive in Spanish and Cuban bartending. | Oxford, MIXOLOGY pdf 1335 | Innocent's Boadas throw is cold; own it in a clause if the throw is named. |
| F15 | Wondrich's modern advice: dim the lights so the flames show "both for your audience (and there's no point in making this drink without one) and, of course, for yourself. You want to see the flames." | Imbibe! pdf 143 | **Modern, Wondrich's, never Thomas's.** It shows the thrower watches the fire: it breaks any "the one person not watching the fire" line. |
| F16 | Some 19th-century barflies "wrote enthusiastic descriptions" of bartenders tossing flaming liquid in an arc over their heads; Oxford says that defies gravity. | Oxford, ROCKS pdf 1672 | The crowd's delight, on the record, grew in the telling. The over-the-head arc is a **legend** (L3). The drink joke that follows stays out. |

| F17 | Wondrich: "from the unsentimental perspective of mixology, the Blue Blazer is not much of an invention, being merely a Scotch Whisky Skin" with the bartender's standard way of mixing cold drinks "And, of course, fire." | Imbibe! pdf 141 | His judgment, attributed. |
| F18 | Thomas's 1862 Whisky Skin: "(Use small bar-glass.)" one wine-glass [2 oz, Wondrich's gloss] of Scotch, one piece of lemon peel, tumbler half filled with boiling water. The 1887 edition "specifies 'Glenlivet or Islay'" and adds an Irish Whiskey Skin. Neither Thomas nor the 1869 *Steward & Barkeeper's Manual* calls for sugar; Wondrich likes 1 tsp demerara. | Imbibe! pdf 140 | "Long strip, no pith" is Wondrich's advice, not Thomas's. "Glenlivet or Islay" is the 1887 edition's, and it's about the Skin, not the Blazer. Wondrich: the Skin "is surely Irish", his inference; label it if used. The Bill Poole shooting and Lincoln's play on the same page stay out. |
| F19 | The 1863 Blazer: "Wineglass Scotch and Irish whisky mixed [1 oz each]", sugar, boiling water, set alight, poured between mugs; "Serve in small bar tumblers. Add piece of lemon skin, pour mixture into glass blazing, and cover with cup." | Imbibe! pdf 142, printed from Campbell, *American Barkeeper* (1867), "that is, Jerry Thomas, 1863" | The 1863 book is lost (Oxford pdf 2019): this is its recipe as Campbell reprinted it. "[1 oz each]" is Wondrich's gloss. The cup goes over a glass poured **blazing**, so on the page it most likely puts the flame out (compare Oxford pdf 298: "Snuff each mug with the bottom of the other"): our inference. Never "Thomas covered it to keep the aroma in". No reason for the two whiskies is on the page. |
| F20 | Wondrich suggests cask-strength Redbreast with cask-strength Caol Ila, and says Thomas "would have had to" use cask strength because anything weaker is very hard to light. | Imbibe! pdf 142 | Cask strength is for lighting, in Wondrich's words. |
| F21 | Wondrich's basis for the cellar line: "Judging by the contents of his bar's cellar, which included barrels of a nice fifteen-year-old Caol Ila malt, Jerry Thomas was an early adopter" of Scotch. | Imbibe! pdf 137 | **Barrels**, fifteen years old. Wondrich doesn't name the record he's going on. |
| F22 | Caol Ila is Islay's largest-volume malt distillery, built 1846. Most of what it makes goes into blends, and the first official single malt bottling came in **2002**. The standard 12- and 18-year-olds are heavily peated malt, but the smoke is "relatively tame by Islay standards". | Oxford, CAOL ILA pdf 402 (Mahoney), primary | Thomas never had this bottle. Say "the distillery whose malt Thomas kept in his cellar, Wondrich writes", never "the whisky he poured". |
| F24 | The Whisky Skin: "For a time Whisky Skin was a popular drink, celebrated onstage … and in verse. It's still a damned good one." Irish pure pot still whisky was "the Irish trademark" until blends replaced it (Oxford, IRISH DISTILLERS LIMITED pdf 1079). | Imbibe! pdf 140; Oxford pdf 1079 | "For a time": never "a classic" for the Skin. The play is Lincoln's, so leave it out. |

## Conflicts
- C1 First in print: 1862 (Oxford pdf 297) vs "turning up in print in the late 1850s" (Imbibe! pdf 142). State no first year.
- C2 Invention: "solely my own" (1863) is doubted (1812 "blue blazes"; the St. Nicholas Hotel bartenders), Oxford pdf 297; yet Oxford pdf 2019 counts it one of two of his own drinks in the 1862 book "as far as can be established", and he owned up to it in 1883–84 (Imbibe! pdf 228). Say "the drink he's remembered for", never "invented".
- C3 Fire and taste: "may be something to" the fire burning out the sting (Oxford pdf 297) vs "little to no effect on the taste … purely done for show" (Oxford FLAIR pdf 797). Claim neither.
- C5 The 1863 measures: the text says "Wineglass Scotch and Irish whisky mixed" and "one wineglass of boiling water"; Wondrich's brackets read them as 1 oz each of whisky and 1½ oz water (Imbibe! pdf 142), while on the Skin he reads a wineglass as 2 oz (pdf 140). "Half and half" and "less water than whisky" are his reading, never the text's.
- C4 Ignition strength 54% (Oxford) vs about 52% (Wondrich).
