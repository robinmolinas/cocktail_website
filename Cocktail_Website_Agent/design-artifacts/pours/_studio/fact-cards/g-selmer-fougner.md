| F9 | He answered readers' questions patiently, "no matter how stupid" (one asked whether all scotch was really made in Australia). | Oxford pdf 833 | primary | "Stupid" is Wondrich's word for the questions, not Fougner's. Paraphrase in guest text. |
| F10 | When he didn't know an answer, he threw the question open to his readers. ## Facts: the Ward Eight, through the column

| # | Claim (our words) | Source | Tier | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| W1 | In 1934, while Fougner was seeking more information on the Ward Eight, a reader sent the *Sun* a recipe. Wondrich prints this one rather than the only pre-Prohibition recipe, which he calls lacklustre. | *Imbibe!* pdf 243–244 (source line: Fougner, "Along the Wine Trail", 1934) | primary (library) | **The drink exists in the history book because the column asked.** Single source for this letter. |
| W2 | The reader's reasoning: the Ward Eight was built on a whisky sour, "to eliminate certain objectionable features" of it; it was a warm-weather drink; always served in a large, heavy beer-type glass with a large round bowl. | *Imbibe!* pdf 243 | primary | The reader's claims about how it "always" was: attribute them to the reader. |
| W3 | His recipe: juice of a lemon, a barspoon of powdered sugar, a large whisky glass three-quarters full of **Bourbon**; a rather large piece of ice in the glass; three or four dashes of orange bitters, three dashes of crème de menthe, half a jigger of grenadine; filled with plain water **or** seltzer; half slices of orange, pineapple, a cherry or two. | *Imbibe!* pdf 243–244 | primary | "1 or 2 large ice cubes", "large beer-goblet", shaking gently and rye are **Wondrich's execution notes**, not the reader's. |
| W4 | When fresh mint is available, the crème de menthe goes and a lightly bruised sprig of mint goes in: "This is an improvement." Many prefer half an orange's juice to the bitters. Sugar and grenadine to taste; what matters is good liquor and care in mixing. | *Imbibe!* pdf 244 | primary (reader's words) | "Improvement" and "Many prefer" are the reader's. **Placement:** the reader adds the bruised sprig *in the glass, with the orange slices*; shaking it ("shake gently so as not to brutalize the mint") is Wondrich's method. The improvement is mint *instead of crème de menthe*, not a way of mixing. "Three-quarters full" of "a large whisky glass" gives no volume: ~90 ml is Wondrich's 3 oz reading. Rechecked r3 against Tomás's quotes: all four verbatim. |
| W5 | Wondrich: the drink is "always considered to be a rye drink", and the sole pre-Prohibition recipe (*The Cocktail Book: A Sideboard Manual for Gentlemen*) says rye; he uses rye, mint and orange juice. Oxford's 1907 recipe is rye too. | *Imbibe!* pdf 244; Oxford pdf 2121 | primary | **Bourbon is the reader's; rye is the record's.** The room picks and says why. |
| W6 | A second letter to the *Sun*: the veteran Boston bartender Charlie Carter's claim that he invented the drink (Puritan Club, 1903). | *Imbibe!* pdf 243; Wondrich 2012 (web) | primary / secondary | **Year conflict (C1).** Content is an election celebration: Wren's politics trap. Dossier only, except the bare fact that more than one reader wrote to the *Sun* about this drink. The book says Carter "claims" in the letter; the 2012 article says the letter "claims it for" Carter: authorship soft too. |
| W7 | Oxford's recipe notes a 1934 variant, 45 ml rye plus 15 ml amontillado, "as suggested by an old Boston bartender". | Oxford pdf 2121 | primary | **No channel on the page.** The entry's bibliography doesn't cite Fougner, and Wondrich's 2012 article has no sherry. So it can't be claimed as the column's, and it must not be merged with Carter (same city, same year in one source: tempting, unproven). |
| W8 | The Ward Eight's origin is contested: the Locke-Ober 1898 election-eve story (Hussion only started there in 1900), Billy Kane, Charlie Carter 1903. Grenadine is original to it; with the Jack Rose it is one of the first American cocktails to use the syrup. Esquire named it a top-ten cocktail of 1934. Out of use by the 1950s. | Oxford pdf 2120 | primary | Origin = legend. The election and Lomasney stay out (Wren). *Not Too Polite* set it aside as a runner-up. |

## Craft cross-check (Tomás's, verified r3)

- *Flavor Matrix* pdf 200: pomegranate is "mostly fruity", but it's also "characterized by sharper aromas such as anise and mint". Mint is **not** in its Best or Surprising Pairings (citrus, melon, basil, tea, lemongrass, chiles, avocado; mushroom, basil). So it's an aroma the fruit shares, not a pairing the book makes. The page describes the fruit and its juice or molasses, not a grenadine.
- Rye is the **older** record (the sole pre-Prohibition recipe; Oxford 1907), and bourbon is the reader's departure. Never call rye "the correction".

## Conflicts

- **C1. Carter's letter: 1934 or 1936?** *Imbibe!* pdf 243 says "a circumstantially detailed 1934 letter"; Wondrich's own 2012 *Imbibe* article says "A 1936 letter to The Sun". Same author, two years: state no year.
- **C2. Bourbon or rye** (W3 vs W5): the reader's word vs the record's. Say whose.
- **C3. Did Carter's letter answer Fougner's question?** Not on the page. Only the recipe-writer is placed "when Fougner was seeking further information". Never "two readers answered him".

## Sibling watch

- *Till Spring* (caregiver-magician) y4: "The grenadine is my own pomegranate syrup…". A house pomegranate grenadine as **the spark** is taken as a touch; grenadine itself is free (it's the Ward Eight's own).
- *A Brother's Care*: the Rob Roy letter went to this column (pdf 1667). Never a letter saving the truth.
- *Not Too Polite*: "The sugar is the argument" (epigraph). *Everybody's*/*Four Shares*: the crowd angle is voice here, not sharing.
