# Patrick Gavin Duffy

| # | Fact | Source | Tier | Notes / guards |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| F1 | Born Patrick Joseph Duffy in Ireland (1868–ca. 1955); started at New York's Ashland House in 1884, aged sixteen, and became its head bartender. Wrote the Manual as "Patrick Gavin Duffy", reason unknown. | Oxford DUFFY (pdf 687); *Imbibe!* pdf 91 (County Roscommon; head bartender from about 1891 to 1894) | primary | Pseudonym: Oxford's "for whatever reason". Never guess why. |
| F2 | A contemporary described him as natty, with a waxed moustache, a vandyke and a carnation bud in his lapel; Oxford calls him an advocate of fastidious service and white-jacket formality. | Oxford pdf 687 | primary | His own foreword agrees (F9: "I personally fancy a carnation"). |
| F3 | Wondrich: unlike Jerry Thomas, Duffy "kept his mouth shut and his ears open"; his memoir is mostly about the people he served. | *Imbibe!* pdf 91 | primary | The Sullivan–Morgan meeting is Regular Guy's old candidate-B anecdote: out of the Legend. |
| F4 | He left the trade: Oxford and Wondrich have him return to Ireland in 1907 and come back in 1921 to run rooming houses in Brooklyn. | Oxford pdf 687; *Imbibe!* pdf 91–92 | primary | **C1** below: the 1933 interview says he sold his saloon in 1915. Tell no year for leaving. |
| F5 | **His own account of why he wrote it (1 Dec 1933):** he'd been "seeing liquor coming back these last three years"; with "not much to do but to manage my two houses in Brooklyn", he started to compile the recipes. He'd thought of liquor returning with the old bartenders "dead and gone" and a new generation trained in speakeasies. | *Brooklyn Daily Eagle*, 1 Dec 1933, p. 2 (page 686031305; second OCR copy p. 14, 693813448, agrees) | primary (newspaper OCR, image not opened) | A motive on the record (reported interview, his words in quotation marks). "Three years" is his figure. Oxford's "work on his magnum opus" from 1921 is a different start: never give the writing a length. "A gentleman's drink" (his phrase) is gendered: dossier only. |
| F6 | He said he had his own experience to go on, and "visited bars and read up on the subject". | same | primary | Squares with F12 (the borrowing). Never "from memory". |
| F7 | He put the popular dry-era mixtures in, and marked "the more fantastic" of them with a little circle (not every one) ("try these at your own risk", the reporter's gloss); his words: "I wouldn't want it said I recommended them." | same | primary | The circle is the reporter's description of the book. |
| F8 | The publisher introduced him on 28 November 1933; the book was announced for 5 December, loose-leaf so recipes could be added, with over 700 cocktails, and he starred the drinks he considered "in the best tradition". The Eagle (1 Dec) has it "out this week"; the Plain Dealer (10 Dec) "just off the presses", with free supplements for a year. | *Florida Times-Union*, 10 Dec 1933, p. 37; *Eagle* 1 Dec; *Plain Dealer*, 10 Dec 1933, p. 59 | primary (OCR) | Stars and circles: his judgement in marks. Counts differ (700 cocktails / "1,000 drinks" in the Eagle): no number in guest text. |
| F9 | His foreword, as a reviewer quoted it: bartending is "an old and honorable trade. It is not a profession"; he deplored long conversation between bartender and customer; "I personally fancy a carnation". | *Virginian-Pilot*, 13 Dec 1933, p. 4 | primary (OCR, quoted by a reviewer) | The same foreword hopes the highball returns: the highball stays out (highball.md F8). |
| F10 | **The year is 1933.** Oxford pdf 687–688 (Long & Smith, 1933) and *Imbibe!* pdf 92 say 1933; Oxford pdf 1323 and *Joy* pdf 63, 348 say 1934. The December 1933 press settles it: on sale by early December 1933. | F8 sources; *Joy* bibliography pdf 429 gives "Alta Publications, 1934" | primary | **Inference:** the 1934 date is a later printing under another publisher (Joy's own bibliography names Alta). |
| F11 | Repeal took effect on 5 December 1933. | *Joy* pdf 62 | primary | So the book was out days before the ban ended (F8 "out this week", 1 Dec). Prohibition is a date only. |
| F12 | The Manual borrowed: Wondrich says it "plundered" Ensslin's 1916 book "wholesale"; Regan says it leaned on Craddock's Savoy book, ordered by base spirit (Craddock was alphabetical), with Bénédictine for absinthe. Oxford counts it among dozens of Repeal anthologies, "particularly ambitious". | *Imbibe!* pdf 241; *Joy* pdf 63–64; Oxford pdf 1323 | primary | Never "his recipes" or "his words" for the book's contents. What's his: the order (by base spirit), the marks (F7–F8) and the judgement. Ensslin owned in the dossier only. |
| F13 | Its standing: "one of the two or three books that did the most to bring back the bartender's craft after Repeal" (Wondrich); in print in various formats for forty years (Oxford). | *Imbibe!* pdf 92; Oxford pdf 687 | primary | Two judgements, each the author's. |
| F14 | The Herald Punch: dedicated to the *New York Herald* in 1901, his one drink "that ever got any traction" (Wondrich, who also calls him "no mixologist"): juice of one rich orange, a teaspoon of powdered sugar, rye, St. Croix rum to flavour, shaken, strained into a sour glass. He signed the letter conveying it with his infant son's name, Henry Griffin Duffy. | *Imbibe!* pdf 92, 304 (footnote); Oxford pdf 687 lists it among his original recipes | primary | **Not checked:** whether the 1933 Manual prints it. Never "the recipe from his book". Why the son's name: unknown ("for reasons of his own"). |
| F15 | A contemporary sneer at the genre (27 Dec 1933): a columnist found "divers books" teaching cocktails "as though it were a new art. How silly", naming Duffy's Manual; the same column says Duffy mixed "his famous punch" for his publishers' guests before Christmas. | *Brooklyn Daily Eagle*, 27 Dec 1933, p. 10 | primary (OCR) | The sneer is at the books' idea, not at Duffy. Which punch: not said. |
| F16 | **Counterweight, same interview (1 Dec 1933):** he didn't approve of women bartenders, didn't think much of mixed drinking, and called liquor a spur to the heart in sickness. | *Eagle* 1 Dec 1933 | primary | Never told, never softened into praise of his "standards". Medicine is family ground left alone. Flagged to Wren and Robin. |

| F17 | Santa Cruz rum (St. Croix, Danish Virgin Islands until the US bought it in 1916) was a leading 19th-century American import; Prohibition switched the island's last distilleries to bay rum, and after Repeal its column-still rum "had little resemblance to the earlier style". | Oxford SANTA CRUZ RUM (pdf 1732–1733, Wondrich) | primary | A modern St. Croix rum is a style stand-in, never Duffy's rum. |
| F18 | Wondrich's half ounce of rum is what "a jury of Americans who can stand their liquor" came up with in Paris; he gives no date or source for the jury. Duffy's text says only "enough St. Croix to flavor". | *Imbibe!* pdf 92 | primary | Never "argued" or "fixed". |

## Conflicts
- **C1** Leaving the trade: 1907, to Ireland (Oxford, *Imbibe!*) vs sold his saloon "in 1915 to retire and attend to his real estate" (*Eagle* 1933). No year in guest text: "he'd been out of the trade for years".
- **C2** How long he was head bartender: *Joy* pdf 49 "for twelve years prior to Prohibition" vs *Imbibe!* pdf 91 (about 1891 to 1894). No duration.
- **C3** Publication year: resolved to 1933 (F10).
