# Fact card: Robert Louis Stevenson and Fanny, Silverado (1879–80) and after

Hester, 2026-10-01, for outlaw-lover (The Fugitive). All primaries are Gutenberg plain texts, fetched and grepped in this session (no page numbers in the e-texts: chapter + printed TOC page given). Quotes kept short; the reading uses our own words.

| ID | Fact | Source (tier) |
|---|---|---|
| F1 | He did not cross as a steerage passenger: he went "by the second cabin" so he'd have a table to work at, though "anxious to see the worst of emigrant life". The second cabin sat "in the very heart of the steerages". | *The Amateur Emigrant*, "The Second Cabin" (*Essays of Travel*, 1905 Chatto, TOC p. 3; Gutenberg #627). Primary. |
| F2 | His parents at first objected, not over the divorce ("extremely liberal views on that subject") but because his choice was "an American and a stranger"; once his mind was "irrevocably made up, they yielded without reserve" and prepared to welcome her. His father promised an allowance; Nellie remembers "the exceeding happiness it gave him". | Nellie Van de Grift Sanchez, *The Life of Mrs. Robert Louis Stevenson* (1920), ch. V (TOC p. 55; Gutenberg #24332). Primary-memoir: Fanny's sister, a loving witness. |
| F3 | Help from his father "from the hour of his marriage always amply and ungrudgingly given". | Colvin, introduction, *Letters*, Swanston ed. vol. 24 (Gutenberg #31809). Primary (friend and editor). |
| F4 | Married 19 May 1880, quietly, by the Rev. Mr Scott, two witnesses. | Sanchez ch. V. |
| F5 | The mountain's reason was medical: the bunk-house's one virtue was "freedom from the fog which we dreaded for the sick man". The reading gives no reason. | Sanchez ch. V (she was there: "seemed to me"). |
| F6 | The tunnel's mouth was the one place "where coolness always held sway" (Sanchez); Stevenson: the squatters kept "their wine in the tunnel that once rang with picks". | Sanchez ch. V; *The Silverado Squatters* (1906 Chatto impression, Gutenberg #516), "Episodes in the Story of a Mine". |
| F7 | "Four of us squatters": himself and his wife, "the King and Queen of Silverado"; Sam, "the Crown Prince"; Chuchu (setter crossed with spaniel), "the Grand Duke". First job "by which we took possession": cutting away poison oak, Fanny standing back. | *Silverado*, "The Act of Squatting". |
| F8 | "rent-free, into a ready-made house" is Stevenson on the *idea's* appeal, said with an eye on Pine Flat (Geysers road) before Silverado. Tell it as what tempted them, not as a description of Silverado. | *Silverado*, "The Act of Squatting". |
| F9 | The book's last paragraph: one candle "in the neck of a pint bottle", yet the house "seemed literally bursting with the light"; "You would have said a conflagration". The far watcher is conditional: "if there were any hunter belated or any traveller" on "the opposite hill-tops", he "must have stood, and watched and wondered". Never "people saw". | *Silverado*, "Toils and Pleasures" (last sentence of the book). |
| F10 | Epigraph (as printed in the 1906 impression; first edition unchecked): Cicero, *De Off.* I.xx. Sourced English (Walter Miller, Loeb 1913, public domain, §70): such men "have had the same aims as kings--to suffer no want, to be subject to no authority, to enjoy their liberty, that is, in its essence, to live just as they please." Stevenson's Latin prints *agerent*; Miller's text reads *egerent* ("suffer no want"). | Gutenberg #516 front matter; Cicero, *De Officiis*, tr. Miller (Gutenberg #47001), I.69–70. Primary + standard translation. |
| C1 | **Counterweight, read to the end:** Cicero's next sentence (I.xxi.70) calls the retired life "easier and safer" but public life "more profitable to mankind"; §71 faults those who retire without a valid excuse (excused: scholars, the ill). Stevenson quoted only the praise. Say "Stevenson put Cicero's line over his book", never "Cicero says stopping is freedom". | Miller, I.70–71. |
| F11 | Past the page: in Samoa "the word 'home' first began to have a real meaning" for them (Nellie's gloss); they bought land near Apia, chose the name Vailima ("five waters"), and set about clearing the forest and building the house. | Sanchez ch. VIII "The Happy Years in Samoa" (TOC p. 167). |
| F12 | On his last afternoon, 3 Dec 1894, "according to their custom he took his morning's work for her criticism". At Thanksgiving days before, he spoke of his wife as "all in all to him when the days were very dark" and of "their undiminished affection" (Sanchez's report of his speech, not his words). | Sanchez ch. VIII, end. |
| C2 | **Stays out:** Sanchez ch. IV dates Fanny's devotion "from the day of their first meeting at Grez" (1876, her first marriage): the affair guard, like the Gosse letter. Samoa's reason is health ("quest of ... health"): no reason given, as with the mountain. Death and illness never named. | Sanchez ch. IV, VIII. |
| F13 | **Correction to my own r2 line** ("no source lets us say together until he died"): Sanchez ch. V counts it from the wedding: her choice was rewarded with "fourteen rich years in his companionship". That dating passes the affair guard. The surrounding paragraph is about his illness: use the span, not the paragraph. | Sanchez ch. V. |
| F14 | **Why the mountain, in full:** Fanny (1883 letter, via Sanchez) was told nothing else would save his life, and they couldn't afford a mountain resort. So health *and* money. In June he wrote he never left "our little platform in the canyon". The reading gives no reason, and never says they went up to be free. | Sanchez ch. V. |
| F15 | Highball lineage: Oxford HIGHBALL (pdf 1005) says the Highball's roots go back to the Austrian Spritzer, white wine and soda, and to Byron's "hock and soda-water" (glossed as German white wine), taken as a hangover cure; by the 1830s Britain swapped the wine for brandy to fix its "want of strength". | Oxford, primary (library). |
| F16 | Cold holds bubbles: at a given pressure a liquid holds more CO2 as it gets colder. | *Liquid Intelligence* printed p. 289 (pdf 293). |
