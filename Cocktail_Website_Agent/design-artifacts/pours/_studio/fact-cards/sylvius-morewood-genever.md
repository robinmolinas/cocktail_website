## Legends
- **L1 "Dr. Sylvius of Leiden invented gin (or genever)."** Still widely repeated (F1). Oxford: no claim; genever is older (F10). Tell it granted first ("Maybe, but…"), never mocked.

## Conflicts
- **C1** Oxford gives Morewood's 1824 title two ways (pdf 1968 "…Both Ancients and Moderns in the Use of Inebriating Liquors"; pdf 1884 "…Ancient and Modern Nations in the Use of Wine and Other Liquors"). Title out of guest text. His 1838 book's title begins *A Philosophical and Statistical History* (pdf 1302): never used (Wren's trap).
- **C2** What Kämpfer meant is Oxford's reading ("seems to have been referring"), and Kämpfer did call the spirit "newly invented" (F6). The guest text may say Kämpfer seems to have meant Sylvius's own version; never that the record proves it.
- **C3** Replacing Sylvius with another named inventor (Coolhaes, the 1495 author) would repeat Morewood's step. No page names an inventor of genever.

## Guards
- **G1** Medicine out: the subject of Kämpfer's 1691 conversation, the "long and high-sounded names" line, genever's medicinal origins (pdf 872), Coolhaes's medicinal spirits. Sylvius is "a chemist in Holland" in the narrator's voice.
- **G2** "Professor at Leiden" for Sylvius is on Oxford's page only as Morewood's words. Use it as his words, or as part of the neat answer ("a professor in Leiden, the story went"). Not checked elsewhere (web blocked).
- **G3** Never "Kämpfer never said it", "Morewood misquoted him", or "Morewood made it up".
- **G4** "Most authorities", never "everyone"; "have begun to correct", never "have set it right".
- **G5** No span of years in the epigraph (Wren f). Dates are fine in the reading.
- **G6** Oude is never "aged" unless a named label says so.
- **G7** The 1495 recipe is "seen as" the first; never "the first gin" or "genever was invented in 1495". The unknown author: one clause at most (not a "nobody knows" landing).
- **G8** Oxford's SYLVIUS entry is Wondrich's: cite Oxford, never name him.
