# Fact card: the Crusta

Our words throughout. Quotes under 15 words, read on screen 2026-10-04: Oxford CRUSTA (pdf 610, Monti), BRANDY CRUSTA (pdf 344, Wondrich), SUGAR RIM (pdf 1953–1955, Wondrich); *Imbibe!* pdf 233–234 (with the 1862 recipe reprinted); *Joy* pdf 255–256.

## Facts

| # | Claim (our words) | Source | Tier | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| K1 | A New Orleans drink of the mid-1800s, made by the bartender Joseph Santini. | Oxford pdf 610 ("1850s"), 1953 ("at least since the 1850s"); *Imbibe!* 233 (took over a bar c. 1850, opened his own five years later); *Joy* 255 ("mid-1800s") | primary | Say "mid-1800s". Which bar is contested (St. Louis Hotel or Jewel of the South, Oxford 610; City Exchange or Jewel, *Imbibe!*): out. His birth year differs (1817 / ca. 1818): out. Italian-born, from Trieste. |
| K2 | The drink owes its fame, and its name, to how it's dressed: a sugared rim on a small wine glass, with a long ribbon of lemon peel set inside like a collar. "Crusta" is the crust the sugar makes. | Oxford pdf 610; SUGAR RIM pdf 1953 | primary | "Dressed by definition" is fair as our gloss of 610. |
| K3 | It was a cocktail (spirit, syrup, bitters, curaçao) with a little lemon juice added: the first recorded cocktail variant with citrus in it. | Oxford pdf 344, 610; *Joy* 256 | primary | Scope "first recorded". |
| K4 | **Contested:** Oxford's CRUSTA calls it the step that paved the way for the Sidecar; Oxford's BRANDY CRUSTA says the Sidecar's ancestry runs better through the Daisy, because the Crusta's lemon is only an accent. Wondrich himself wrote in 2007 that evolution brings us from the Crusta to the Sidecar. | Oxford 610 vs 344; *Imbibe!* 233 | primary | Never "the Sidecar's ancestor" as fact. "A Sidecar-shaped drink" or "an early cocktail with lemon in it". Same author, two views. |
| K5 | The 1862 printed recipe: gum syrup, bitters, spirit, a little curaçao and lemon, mixed with ice and strained into a "fancy red wine-glass" with a sugared rim and the peel of half a lemon pared in one piece ("Pare half a lemon"; Oxford 610 also says half; the whole lemon is Regan's, *Joy* 256). It ends: "Then smile." The method says "a little lemon juice"; Oxford 610 quotes Thomas's word as "a dash" (likely his ingredient list, which *Imbibe!* replaces with Wondrich's bracketed amounts): never "says only". | *Imbibe!* pdf 234 | primary (1862 text reprinted) | Never name its printer (Jerry Thomas is *Beside the First*'s man). The method is only "mix": Oxford's modern recipe stirs (pdf 610), Regan shakes (*Joy* 256). Maraschino came later (`nuts` in our table): curaçao is the 1862 liqueur anyway. |
| K6 | The sugar goes on the outside of the rim; rub with lemon, roll in sugar, let it set about an hour so it doesn't run. | Oxford SUGAR RIM pdf 1955 | primary | Sugar inside the drink comes from the syrup, not the rim. |
| K7 | Never a big success: a cult drink with few but devoted fans (*Imbibe!*), an epicure's drink until Prohibition (Oxford 344); still, it appeared in most cocktail books until World War II (Oxford 610). | as cited | primary | "Never caught on" needs the second half. |
| K8 | By the turn of the century, as drinks got drier, sugar rims began to look old-fashioned: a New York newspaperman sneered at them in 1908. | Oxford SUGAR RIM pdf 1953, 1955 | primary | About sugar rims in general, not the Crusta. |
| K9 | Australia kept a version as a standard drink from the 1930s to the 1980s, with orange juice and Australian brandy. | Oxford pdf 344 | primary | Dossier. |
