## The drink (library)
- **D1** A Prohibition-era cocktail of Bacardi rum, pineapple juice and grenadine, named after the silent film star. Oxford pdf 1252.
- **D2** Basil Woon's *When It's Cocktail Time in Cuba* (1928) credits Fred Kaufman, bartender at the Hotel Sevilla-Biltmore, Havana, who invented it to commemorate her visit. Oxford pdf 1252 (Woon via Oxford). **Contested: see C1.**
- **D3** Newspapers confirm she holidayed in Havana in December 1922, "when Kaufman most likely created his drink". Oxford pdf 1252. The year of the drink is Oxford's inference, not a record.
- **D4** "Purportedly" one of the three most-ordered cocktails in Prohibition Cuba, after the Daiquiri and the El Presidente. Oxford pdf 1252. Keep the hedge ("reportedly").
- **D5** Popular at Constante's La Florida, where Woon saw him make six at a time. Oxford pdf 1252 (see `constante-ribalaigua-floridita.md`).
- **D6** Oxford's recipe: 60 ml pineapple juice (preferably fresh), 30 ml white rum, 5 ml grenadine, shaken, chilled cocktail glass. pdf 1252.
- **D7** Oxford MARASCHINO (pdf 1228) says maraschino "plays a key role in the Mary Pickford". Regan's recipe (Joy pdf 341): 1½ oz light rum, 1½ oz pineapple, 1 barspoon maraschino, grenadine; he calls it the formula "reportedly used" by Juan Coronado in Washington. **See C3.**
- **D8** Regan: named for the star "once known as America's Sweetheart". Joy pdf 341.
- **D9** Oxford's bibliography for the entry includes a 1922 *Hollywood Magazine* piece on Fairbanks and Pickford touring. pdf 1252. (Fenced: Fairbanks.)

## The woman (PBS, secondary)
- **P1** Born Gladys Louise Smith, Toronto, 1892, eldest of three. Called "the Girl with the Golden Curls"; "America's Sweetheart"; fans called her "our Mary". Most popular woman of her day.
- **P2** Stage debut 1898, as a child, to help support the family after her father died. (Father's drinking: fenced.)
- **P3** At 15 she waited weeks outside David Belasco's office for an interview, then marched in. He cast her in *The Warrens of Virginia*.
- **P4** **The name.** Belasco recommended "a new, more star-like name". "Mary Pickford" was made from a surname in her mother's family and a fib about her middle name: hating Louise, she told Belasco it was Marie, then adapted that to Mary. The page doesn't say who chose "Pickford".
- **P5** Biograph (1909): Griffith offered five dollars a day, three days a week. She demanded ten a day and more for extra work. He accepted.
- **P6** She worked out that film needed a new kind of acting: she toned her performances down to make them more intimate, and showed feeling through her eyes, not big gestures.
- **P7** With her mother's encouragement she moved company to company, always negotiating higher pay; 1913, Zukor's Famous Players, $500 a week.
- **P8** 1919: with Chaplin, Fairbanks and Griffith she founded United Artists, created to distribute the founders' own films and break the distributors' hold on profits. Never before had filmmakers taken so much control of the business side. Her words: "The satisfaction was, in one word, freedom." She went on: a "heady wine"; once tasted, impossible to go back to working for someone else.
- **P9** **The truer cost.** The financial demands of United Artists (her own company) pressed her to limit herself artistically, to roles she knew her fans would love. *Pollyanna* (1920) fixed the little-girl persona further.
- **P10** 1928: her mother died of breast cancer. "In her grief", Pickford cut off her famous curls, and fans lambasted her. Her own later words: "You would have thought I had murdered someone, and perhaps I had, but only to give her successor a chance to live." PBS's narration (not her quote): she knew she could no longer play teenagers.
- **P11** First major star to attempt a talkie; results disappointing. Last film *Secrets* (1933). (Later years fenced.)

## Conflicts and guards
- **C1 Who made it.** Oxford (Woon, 1928): Fred Kaufman, Sevilla-Biltmore. Regan (Joy pdf 341, unsourced): Eddie Woelke, "an American bartender who worked in Havana". Two library books, two makers: **name neither in guest text.** "A bartender in Havana" is safe. Oxford is the stronger source (a 1928 contemporary), recorded here, not told.
- **C2 When.** "Most likely" December 1922 (Oxford). Never "made in 1922" flat; "during Prohibition" or "probably during her 1922 visit".
- **C3 Recipe.** Oxford contradicts itself (no maraschino at pdf 1252; maraschino "key role" at pdf 1228); Regan adds it. Never "the original recipe" or "as it was first made".
- **C4 "Made for her visit"** is Woon's account via Oxford only; Regan says "named for". Safe: "named for her"; "made for her visit" carries "the story goes" or "by one 1928 account".
- **C5 She never drank it on record.** No page says she tasted it.
- **C6 The haircut's motive.** On the record: grief (PBS narration) and her own "murdered someone … her successor" line. "Strategy", "power move", "took control of the image" as motive are ours: signpost them.
- **C7 "Herself".** The page's verb is "cut off". Agency is hers; no page says she held the scissors. No scissors in her hand in the image.
- **C8 United Artists distributed; it didn't "own" anyone's films.** Say "to distribute their own films".
- **C9 "Into her thirties"** is our arithmetic (b. 1892, teenage roles to 1928, aged about 36); the page says "teenagers". Label ours or avoid.
- **C10 Co-founders.** Fairbanks is one of the four. Fenced from guest text, so say "with Chaplin, Griffith and others" or "with three other filmmakers", never a list that implies three.
