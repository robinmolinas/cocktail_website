## Wild Apples (Thoreau)
| id | fact | source | tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| W1 | The essay is headed "(1862.)" in *Excursions* (1863). The *Atlantic Monthly* (November 1862) came from a search summary and hasn't been checked: state no magazine and no month. | l. 5845–5848 | primary |
| W2 | His "wild" apples are mostly trees grown from cultivated stock that came up untended, sown by birds or cows, in pastures, woods and roadsides. He ties the apple to himself: "our wild apple is wild only like myself, perchance", one that strayed into the woods from the cultivated stock. (Don't quote the rest of the sentence: its "aboriginal race" wording.) | l. 6170–6172 | primary |
| W3 | Pasture seedlings are cropped by cows year after year (he counted rings on foot-high bushes about twelve years old) and grow into a dense, thorny clump. His joke: "In their thorniness, however, there is no malice, only some malic acid." | l. 6250–6257, 6273–6278 | primary |
| W4 | After twenty years or more of browsing, the clump is so broad that "they become their own fence"; a shoot out of reach rises into a tree; the clump below dies back, and "the generous tree" lets the now harmless cows into its shade, to taste its fruit and spread the seed. **Counterweight:** the same pages use fighting words ("foes", "tactics", "defeats its bovine foes"). | l. 6280–6304 | primary |
| W5 | Their time is the last of October and the first of November. Farmers don't think it worth gathering them; "the farmer thinks that he has better in his barrels, but he is mistaken". | l. 6376–6382 | primary |
| W6 | "Those which the farmer leaves out as unsalable, and unpalatable to those who frequent the markets, are choicest fruit to the walker." | l. 6451–6453 | primary |
| W7 | Indoors: the wild apple "being brought into the house, has frequently a harsh and crabbed taste"; "The Saunterer's Apple not even the saunterer can eat in the house"; indoors "you miss the November air, which is the sauce it is to be eaten with". One taken from his desk in his room is "unexpectedly crude", sour enough to set a squirrel's teeth on edge "and make a jay scream". **Both "crabbed" and "crude" are his words** (the family plan said otherwise: wrong, struck). | l. 6453–6467 | primary |
| W8 | Outdoors: "it is necessary that you be breathing the sharp October or November air"; "What is sour in the house a bracing walk makes sweet"; some "might be labelled, 'To be eaten in the wind.'" | l. 6475–6484 | primary |
| W9 | He applies it to himself: "one thought for the field, another for the house"; he would have his thoughts, like wild apples, be food for walkers, and won't warrant them palatable if tasted in the house. | l. 6540–6542 | primary |
| W10 | Cider: "No wonder that these small and high-colored apples are thought to make the best cider." **The sentence before:** "I still begrudge them to the cider-mill. Perhaps they are not fairly ripe yet." Others send them to cider; he would rather eat them. | l. 6429–6434 | primary |
| W11 | The frozen-thawed apple (late November into December): sound wild apples that freeze and then thaw are "filled with a rich, sweet cider, better than any bottled cider that I know of"; "your jaws are the cider-press". **"It is a fruit never carried to market, that I am aware of" is about this frozen-thawed apple only**, with his own hedge. Never apply it to the October wild apple. | l. 6699–6728 | primary |
| W12 | The companion: "those crabbed apples with which I cheated my companion, and kept a smooth face that I might tempt him to eat. Now we both greedily fill our pockets". He brought someone out, teased him with a sour one, then they shared the thawed ones. Season: winter, not October. | l. 6718–6722 | primary |
| W13 | Grafted apples were chosen for mildness, size and bearing; the nursery "Favorites", "None-suches" and "Seek-no-farthers" "commonly turn out very tame and forgetable". | l. 6418–6426 | primary |
| W14 | His invented names include the Saunterer's Apple ("you must lose yourself before you can find the way to that"), the Beauty of the Air, the Truant's Apple, the Hedge-Apple, the Frozen-Thawed. | l. 6596–6626 | primary |
| W15 | His prediction: the wild apple "will probably become extinct in New England"; he links the decline to "the temperance reform and the general introduction of grafted fruit", and tells of an owner who cut down an orchard "for fear they should be made into cider". Whether the prediction came true is **unsourced**: never state either way. | l. 6730–6745 | primary |
| W16 | Wild apples belong to the Pomaceae, "uniformly innocent and kind to our race" (his phrase). Archetype word: never quote it. | l. 6429–6430 | primary |

## Emerson's sketch of Thoreau
| id | fact | source | tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| E1 | Printed as "Biographical Sketch. By R. W. Emerson" at the front of *Excursions* (1863). The eulogy, the funeral and the *Atlantic* of August 1862 are not on this page (and deaths stay out anyway). Say "his friend Emerson wrote". | l. 59–60 | primary |
| E2 | The cost, in a friend's words: refusing the usual paths, Thoreau kept "his solitary freedom at the cost of disappointing the natural expectations of his family and friends". | l. 88–93 | primary |
| E3 | Emerson grants him "his large ambition of knowledge and action", aimed at "the art of living well" rather than any narrow craft or profession. **Conflicts with E6 (C2)**, and "the art of living well" sits near the Fugitive's "live as you please". | l. 95–98 | primary |
| E4 | "He chose to be rich by making his wants few, and supplying them himself." | l. 152–153 | primary |
| E5 | Emerson's own hedge on "chose": "He chose, wisely, no doubt, for himself, to be the bachelor of thought and Nature", but also "Perhaps he fell into his way of living without forecasting it much, but approved it with later wisdom." (C3) | l. 128–132 | primary |
| E6 | "I cannot help counting it a fault in him that he had no ambition. Wanting this, instead of engineering for all America, he was the captain of a huckleberry party." Then: pounding beans may lead to pounding empires some day, "but if, at the end of years, it is still only beans!" He meant it: the sentence starts "I so much regret the loss of his rare powers of action". (Excursions spells "huckleberry party", no hyphen.) | l. 643–649 | primary |
| E7 | The next sentence softens it: "But these foibles, real or apparent, were fast vanishing" in the growth of "a spirit so robust and wise". And his study of nature "inspired his friends with curiosity to see the world through his eyes". | l. 651–655 | primary |
| E8 | The huckleberry party was real and warm: he was "always ready to lead a huckleberry party or a search for chestnuts or grapes", with the young people he loved. | l. 175–179 | primary |
| E9 | **Counterweight: Thoreau didn't say no gently.** "There was somewhat military in his nature"; "as if he did not feel himself except in opposition"; "It cost him nothing to say No; indeed, he found it much easier than to say Yes"; "a born protestant"; he "refused to pay a tax". | l. 101, 125–126, 159–166 | primary |
| E10 | "he drank no wine"; "He did not like the taste of wine". Nothing on the page says he drank cider or any mixed drink. | l. 126, 147 | primary |
| L1 | The pencil story (he made a pencil as good as London's, friends saw his way to fortune open, and he said he'd never make another) is Emerson's telling only, l. 73–82. I haven't checked it against the record of his later work in the family business. **Legend-grade: keep out.** | l. 73–82 | primary (as Emerson's account) |

## The Stone Fence and the cider
| id | fact | source | tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| S1 | The Stone Fence, also called Stone Wall or Sampson, is an early North American drink from the colonial era; as Sampson it is recorded as early as 1750. Roughly equal portions of spirit ("originally rum, and then whisky") and sweet cider or, preferably, hard cider; applejack at times; sometimes served hot with sugar, or with a lemon peel or a clove-studded orange peel. | Oxford pdf 1931 | primary |
| S2 | Jerry Thomas's 1862 recipe: a large bar-glass, a wine-glass (2 oz) of bourbon, "2 or 3 small lumps of ice", filled up with sweet cider. Wondrich reads Thomas's sweet cider as non-alcoholic, and calls the drink by then a ghost of the older one, which was hard cider and New England rum. | *Imbibe!* pdf 160; Oxford pdf 1931 | primary |
| S3 | **The bridge:** Wondrich notes that "as late as 1869" the *Steward & Barkeeper's Manual* said a tart, full-flavoured, alcoholic crab-apple cider was "frequently used in preference to ordinary cider". Channel: Wondrich quoting the 1869 manual (unread). | *Imbibe!* pdf 160 | primary (relay) |
| S4 | War legends attached to the drink: Ethan Allen and Fort Ticonderoga ("according to legend and a good deal of historical fact", Wondrich) and Oxford's Bunker Hill line. **Never.** | *Imbibe!* pdf 160; Oxford pdf 1931 | — |
| S5 | Today: the traditional, "arguably best" cider varieties have a good amount of tannin and are high in sugar and acid; cider's current strongholds include the northeastern United States; in the US fermented cider is "hard cider". | Oxford APPLES pdf 130 | primary |
| S6 | Arnold's "quite good" wild apples were from the Tien Shan forest of Kazakhstan and western China (tasted in 2007, via Phil Forsline's collection), not New England pasture apples; he hopes to find one with the rich taste, high acid and high sugar cocktail work needs. Highly tannic cider apples in cocktails he hadn't yet tried (p. 349). **Not Thoreau's apples: never merge.** | *LI* pdf 353–354 | primary |
| S7 | In Victorian England "Cobbler's Punch" was sometimes a name for a Stone Fence (gin or whisky and cider). | Oxford pdf 486 | primary |

## Conflicts
- C1 **The base:** Oxford "originally rum, and then whisky"; Thomas 1862 bourbon with sweet cider; the rum-and-hard-cider form is Wondrich's reading of the older drink (framed through the Allen legend). No 1750 recipe is on any page. Claim "the older way, with rum and hard cider", never "the original recipe".
- C2 **Ambition:** Emerson grants a "large ambition" (l. 96) and later counts it a fault that he had "no ambition" (l. 646). Use either only as Emerson's words.
- C3 **Chose or fell into:** Emerson's "He chose" (l. 128, 152) vs "Perhaps he fell into" (l. 131). "Chose to be rich by making his wants few" is fine as Emerson's line; "Thoreau chose a small life on purpose" is not a fact.
- C4 **Dates:** the page dates the essay 1862 and the book 1863; magazine and month unverified.

## Guards
- The indoor/outdoor taste is always "Thoreau wrote", never a fact about apples.
- Never "Thoreau drank" anything; he drank no wine (E10).
- "Never carried to market" belongs to the frozen-thawed apple, with "that I am aware of" (W11).
- Thoreau's kindness in refusing isn't on the page; Emerson says the opposite (E9). The gentle refusal is the guest's.
- Walden, the jail, the tax, *Civil Disobedience*, his death, the funeral: never. War legends (S4): never. Pencil story (L1): never.
