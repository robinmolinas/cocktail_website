## Facts
| ID | Fact (our words) | Source | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| F1 | At root, the Tom Collins is an extra-large glass of gin punch lengthened with soda water and ice. | S1 pdf 2041 | |
| F2 | It's one of the best-known and longest-lasting mixed drinks, as popular in the 1950s as in the 1870s, and it gave its name to the tall, narrow Collins glass. | S1 pdf 2041 | Oxford's own renown judgment ("one of the best known and enduring mixed drinks of all time"). Paraphrase it; don't make it "the most famous". |
| F3 | The name comes from the John Collins, after John Collin (c. 1771–1843), headwaiter and proprietor of Limmer's Old House in London, who helped make the drink popular in its punch form. | S1 pdf 2041 | "Helped to popularize", never "invented". |
| F4 | In America after Prohibition, the Tom Collins was made with London dry gin and the John Collins with bourbon. | S1 pdf 2041 | **Conflict:** JOHN COLLINS (S2 pdf 1110) defines it as genever-based. Scope any bourbon "John Collins" to America after Repeal. |
| F5 | The Rum Collins dates from the 1930s. The Vodka Collins began to appear in the 1930s and was everywhere by the 1950s. | S1 pdf 2042 | **The page for "a renowned classic" in y4** (Robin's "let the drink sound worth having"). |
| F6 | Oxford: most bartenders insist a Collins is built in the glass and stirred, not shaken. | S1 pdf 2042 | **Conflicts with S6 p. 138** (short shake). Claim our method as ours, never as what makes it a Collins. |
| F7 | *Codex*: a Collins is basically a sour served tall with ice and seltzer, and its sweet-sour family includes fizzes and sparkling-wine drinks. Their build: measured seltzer into the empty glass, a short shake of about five seconds, strained onto the seltzer (which mixes it), ice last, nothing to stir. Room-temperature seltzer goes flat. | S6 p. 138 | Their method, so in guest text it's "the way one bar book builds it", never "the traditional way" (Oxford F6 differs). |
| F8 | *Codex*: in Collins-style drinks they often set citrus and sweetener 1:1, unless they want it extra-tart. | S6 p. 139 | A rule of thumb. Their reason (carbonic acid makes it taste sharper) **conflicts with S7** ("carbonation doesn't taste acidic", LI pdf 292). Never tell the reason. Tomás caught this, round 2. |
| F9 | The classic Rickey has no sugar; its variations occasionally have "a scant amount" of sweetener. | S3 pdf 1661 | So "it has sugar, so it isn't a Rickey" goes too far. Say the classic Rickey has none, and that sugar here is the balance. |
| F10 | Oxford calls the French 75 (gin or brandy, lemon, sugar, Champagne) essentially a Champagne or "Imperial" Tom Collins. | S4 pdf 850 | **Sibling:** *Just This Once* (regular-guy-outlaw) is a French 75. That makes it a Collins cousin in the same row. |
| F11 | *Codex*'s authors (founders of a craft bar) admit bartenders "talk a lot of smack about vodka", themselves among the worst in the early 2000s. To deny its value, they say, is arrogant and "inhospitable". Lots of people love it for its honesty: you get what you see. | S6 p. 69 | Their opinion, in their voice. In y4 it's "the authors of one well-known bar book", and quote under 15 words or paraphrase. "Honesty / what you see" sits near *A Brother's Care*'s epigraph (Tomás's flag). |
| F12 | In the 1990s, a pioneering craft bar kept no vodka, "on principle". | S8 p. 92 | The trade's snobbery on the record. **Keep the bar and the people unnamed:** the speaker is Toby Maloney (*Had to Be Serious*'s person), and the bar is Petraske's. One person per story. |
| F13 | Oxford: vodka is the world's best-selling spirit; it's prized as relatively flavourless, colourless and odourless, and its nuances are faint once it's mixed. 40% is the recognised standard strength, and the EU minimum is 37.5%. | S5 pdf 2104–2106 | The 37.5% supports Tomás's recipe note. "Best-selling" is present tense in a 2021 book, so date it if used. **Mild conflict with F11's next paragraph** (the *Codex*: "neutrality" misses the point). Say neither "flavourless" nor "hidden depths" as fact. |
| F14 | *Codex*: in a Mule, the vodka core keeps the focus on the ginger, the lime and the fizz. | S6 p. 140 | About the Mule. "Vodka lets the lime show" in our Collins is **our reasoning** (Tomás's), not the book's. |
| F15 | Oxford: by the 1890s, the cooler's loose shape overlapped with the Collins, the highball and the rickey. | S9 pdf 568 | Background only. Never quote it as "a Collins is a kind of rickey". |
