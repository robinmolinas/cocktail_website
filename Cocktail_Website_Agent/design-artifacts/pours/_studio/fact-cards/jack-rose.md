## Facts
| ID | Fact (our words) | Source | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| F1 | The Jack Rose is applejack, lime juice and grenadine. With the Bronx and the Clover Club, it was one of three popular drinks that, in the 1900s, made it acceptable for a cocktail to contain citrus juice. | S1 pdf 1089 | Renown plus history in one clause. Paraphrase: Oxford's run is "made it permissible for a cocktail to include citrus juice". |
| F2 | For much of the 20th century it was the last applejack cocktail still standing. | S1 pdf 1089 | |
| F3 | Its creation was credited, in its day, to several people: a gambler in the 1912 Becker-Rosenthal murder case, a Jersey City bartender, the stewards of two clubs. An older and better-supported attribution: in 1899, a *New York Press* reporter wrote that at Fred Eberlin's downtown bar, where bartender Frank Haas mixed for the city's stockbrokers, the jack-rose was "the pet of many connoisseurs". | S1 pdf 1089 | **Fenced:** the gambler and the murder case (Outlaw ground). Haas is "older and better supported", not proven as its inventor: never "Haas invented it". |
| F4 | Haas had been at that bar since the 1870s. In 1913 he gave his recipe: applejack, raspberry syrup, lemon and orange juice (a Whisky Daisy made with applejack). | S1 pdf 1089 | |
| F5 | The name apparently comes from the Général Jacqueminot, or "Jack", rose, a popular hybrid the drink resembles in colour. | S1 pdf 1089 | "Apparently": tell it as likely, never as settled. |
| F6 | By 1913 it had spread beyond downtown New York and changed. The mixed citrus gave way to straight lemon juice, or the trendier lime; raspberry syrup gave way to grenadine; some added gin, vermouth or egg white. The simple three-ingredient version survived the 20th century, barely, into the revival. | S1 pdf 1089–1090 | **The lemon-or-lime choice is in this drink's own record.** It's a page for the taste step. Lime was the fashionable newcomer (consistent with Oxford CITRIC ACID pdf 466: limes not widely available until the 1930s). |
| F7 | Oxford: not one of the revival's standard-bearers, but "a solid classic that has its adherents". In the revival's early days Boston had a Jack Rose Society made up of most of the city's top bartenders. | S1 pdf 1090 | Oxford's own measure of renown: "a classic", never "famous" or "best-known". |
| F8 | Oxford's recipe: 60 ml applejack, 15 ml lime, 15 ml grenadine, shaken, strained into a chilled cocktail glass. | S1 pdf 1090 | **Base conflict (C1).** |
| F9 | The *Codex* prints it as a CLASSIC: 1½ oz Laird's 100-proof straight apple brandy, ½ oz Clear Creek 2-year apple brandy, ¾ oz lime, ¾ oz House Grenadine, apple slice. Its grenadine, "made from pomegranate juice and spiked with orange oil", is "both juicy and tangy" and "highlights the apple brandy"; the lime "provides a counterpoint to the grenadine's richness". | S2 p. 129 | The taste line describes **their** grenadine (orange oil, plus malic and citric powders, S3). Ours has neither, so never carry it over as a claim about ours. |
| F10 | *Codex* House Grenadine: equal weights of pomegranate juice and unbleached cane sugar, with malic and citric acid and orange extract, blended until the sugar dissolves. They say bars that reduce pomegranate juice get something "murky and dense". The neon-red grenadine of their childhood Shirley Temples was high-fructose corn syrup, "but it was pretty and we loved it", and their recipe honours that memory. | S3 p. 47 | **Counterweight travels with it:** the authors loved the neon stuff. This is no exposé. |
| F11 | *Codex*: by law, applejack is a blend of apple brandy and neutral grain spirits. The authors prefer Laird's 100-proof straight apple brandy, made only from apples. Calvados is a close cousin of Cognac; Laird's apple brandy is nearer American whiskey. | S4 p. 160 | Supports the recipe's "not applejack". |
| F12 | Oxford: at the start of the revival, buying a bottle of Laird's Bonded Apple Brandy was a rite of passage for young bartenders, and the gateway to forgotten classics including the Jack Rose. | S5 | |
| F13 | Oxford (via Wondrich): the 1913 Pink Lady that entered the canon was basically a Jack Rose with a splash of gin. | S6 pdf 1521 | Sibling note: *Their Night* (magician-caregiver) is an anís Pink Lady and kept the Jack Rose out (its card's C4). No clash. |

## Conflicts and fences
| ID | Note |
|---|---|
| C1 | **Base:** Oxford's recipe is applejack (F8); the *Codex*'s is straight apple brandy (F9). Ours follows the *Codex* and says "not applejack". In guest text, never "the classic is made with apple brandy". Say "I make it with apple brandy". |
| C2 | **Name:** "apparently" the Jacqueminot rose (F5). The gambler "Bald Jack" Rose and the 1912 murder case stay out (legend and Outlaw ground). |
| C3 | **Grenadine taste:** the *Codex*'s "juicy and tangy" is its own orange- and acid-spiked grenadine (F9, F10). |
| C4 | **Grenadine as know-how is a collection motif.** *The Long Answer* (hero-creator) y2 already tells the guest that 1930s American grenadine was mostly imitation and the good makers used real pomegranate (Fougner, 1936). *By Design* (lover-ruler, Ruler column) closes on "Make the grenadine yourself, the day before." If our reading makes "what's really in the grenadine" the guest's know-how, it's the third pour with that shape. |
| C5 | **Inventor:** no page proves who made it first. Haas and Eberlin's are "older and better supported" (F3). |
| C6 | The 1913 *Evening Telegram*'s "cocktail war" among brokers: a headline only, unread. Out. |
