# Fact card: Simon Ford, Plymouth Gin and the brand ambassador

Written 2026-10-01 by Hester for regular-guy-outlaw (The Scoundrel). Every quote below was copied from the page in the same call it was read.
Sources: Oxford Companion, FORD, SIMON (pdf 828), primary (library). Simonson, *A Proper Drink*, pp. 108–112 (printed), reliable secondary (interviews on the record).

## Facts
| # | Fact | Source |
|---|---|---|
| F1 | Born 1972 in Hastings, England, raised in Bath. Oxford calls him "a bartender and a bon vivant turned brand ambassador and finally liquor company cofounder", who pioneered the brand ambassador role, "especially in the area of brand education". | Oxford pdf 828 |
| F2 | Oxford: Ford "convinced his employers to pay for" distillery visits, historical walking tours and celebrations of bars and classic cocktails. Oxford gives the motive as **both**: extending the sponsoring brands' influence *and* the invited bartenders' education and professional standing. The model "was copied and employed across the liquor and bar industries". | Oxford pdf 828 |
| F3 | Seagram's laid Ford off in 2000. He used his severance to open a cocktail bar, Koba, in Brighton. In 2002 Nick Blacknell sent him to New York to launch Plymouth, then down to five thousand cases a year. Simonson: "a suicide mission". | *Proper* p. 109 |
| F4 | The brief, as Ford remembered it: find "the fifty best cocktail bars in America". Simonson's portrait: "Baby-faced, chatty, antic, and upbeat", he "laughed after every other sentence and was hard to dislike". | *Proper* p. 110 |
| F5 | Colin Appiah's memory of meeting him in Bath: "Simon Ford, nice to meet you, sir. First day at work." Appiah was so charmed he told Ford whom to look up in London. | *Proper* p. 110 |
| F6 | Ford: "I never once sold a bottle." He asked bartenders, "can I teach you about my gin?" He "became known among bartenders as not a pest, but a helpmate." | *Proper* p. 110 |
| F7 | When Jim Beam took over Plymouth's distribution, they "wanted nothing to do with whatever a brand ambassador might be". Blacknell's deal: "Give him three months", and if unimpressed they could tell him to get rid of Ford. "Ford stayed." | *Proper* p. 111 |
| F8 | He convinced Plymouth to invite a handful of American bartenders to the distillery in England. It began a form of bartender travel in which "Humble drink-slingers" who had never been offered "so much as car fare" were flown to Cognac, Kentucky, London, Peru, Brazil, Jerez, Poland "and beyond". | *Proper* p. 111 |
| F9 | Simonson: it was also "the start of profligate spending habits for the glad-handing Ford." Blacknell on the Plymouth trips: "he probably didn't tell me how much it cost"; "I didn't really mind; he was doing a good job." | *Proper* p. 111 |
| F10 | "Years later", at Pernod Ricard promoting Absolut, he proposed lavish press dinners: a celebrity Chicago chef's tasting menu, a different vodka drink with each course. He had spent $60,000 when his bosses cut the entire budget. "Rather than drop it, Ford forged ahead": five dinners, at a cost of $330,000. | *Proper* p. 111 |
| F11 | Ford: "I thought I might get fired," "but the events were so well-received I got funding to do more." | *Proper* p. 112 |
| F12 | Plymouth went from five thousand to a quarter of a million cases a year in five years. Klemm: Ford turned a local movement into an international one. | *Proper* p. 112 |
| F13 | Klemm called him the most important person in the industry of the previous fifteen years. Simonson: "a name completely unknown to the drinking public". | *Proper* p. 108 |
| F14 | Cofounded the 86 Company in 2012 (Fords Gin). Brown-Forman bought Fords Gin in 2019. Long-time host of the Spirited Awards show at Tales of the Cocktail. | Oxford pdf 828 |

## Conflicts and limits (keep out of the reading)
| # | Issue |
|---|---|
| C1 | **Start date.** Oxford has him at Plymouth "starting in the late 1990s". *Proper* has a Seagram's layoff in 2000 and Blacknell sending him in 2002. No start year in the reading. |
| C2 | **Pernod.** Oxford: Pernod Ricard's acquisition of Plymouth in 2009. *Proper*: he and Blacknell went to Beefeater, owned by Pernod Ricard. Don't say how or when he got to Pernod. |
| C3 | **The dinners are undated** ("Years later"). The page doesn't say whether $330,000 includes the $60,000. Quote "five dinners" and the cut budget; any sum goes in exactly as printed. |
| C4 | **Open or hidden?** The page doesn't say whether he told his bosses before the dinners. That they were press dinners, and so public, is *our inference*. "Probably didn't tell me how much it cost" is Blacknell's guess about the Plymouth invoices, not about the dinners. |
| C5 | **"Let off" is our word.** On the page: "Ford stayed" (Beam), "I didn't really mind" (Blacknell), "I got funding to do more" (Ford). Nobody on the page says "forgave". |

## Guards
- Simonson's "The brand ambassador is a confidence man" (p. 109, about the role) never reaches guest text. Nor do "boozy", "hedonistic mayhem" or partying (p. 109, Oxford).
- The chef is a famous Chicago chef, never named: he and his Aviary belong to *Unrehearsed* (jester-explorer).
- Saunders, Reiner, DeGroff and Klemm appear on p. 110. They are other pours' people (*Making the Calls*, Hero, *On One Condition*), so leave them out or use one clause at most.
- *Who's In?* (ruler-jester) quotes Ford as a witness to LAB (pp. 100–101). Here he's the subject, so no LAB.
- The 86 Company founding is the Entrepreneur's shape (*Loose on Top*): one clause at most.
- **Counterweight, p. 108:** in-house ambassadors replaced the freelance consultants. Reed called it "a kick in the teeth for us". That was the industry's shift, not Ford's cheek, and those consultants weren't newer or ordinary people. It's recorded so the room sees it.
- **Living person.** One web search (2026-10-01) showed him still publicly tied to Fords Gin, and its snippets showed no red flag. That's a search summary, so it's not citable. Date any present-tense line.

## Plymouth gin and the French 75 (added 2026-10-01, round 3)
| # | Fact | Source |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | Investors led by Paul Murphy and Charles Rolls bought Plymouth from Allied Domecq in 1998. It was then "bottled at a stingy 37.5 percent ABV". They returned it "to close to its original proof" and to grain spirit. Oxford credits the revival partly to the brand's "support for the modern cocktail revival. See … FORD, SIMON." **Never say Ford (or Blacknell) saved Plymouth alone.** | Oxford PLYMOUTH GIN pdf 1541 |
| P2 | The 1860 Plymouth was bottled at 44% with seven botanicals. Today it is "a bit sweeter" and "a bit more earthy" than London dry, and "stands alone in flavor profile". **Today's label ABV (41.2%) is NOT on any page we have**: unsourced, and the web budget is spent. | Oxford pdf 1538, 1541 |
| P3 | **Pernod's purchase: 2008** (PLYMOUTH pdf 1541) **vs 2009** (FORD pdf 828). Oxford contradicts itself (extends C2), so no year. | Oxford |
| P4 | FRENCH 75: "essentially, a Champagne or 'Imperial' Tom Collins". Formula and name first appear together in 1927 (*Here's How!*). The traditional recipe is strained "into a highball glass filled with cracked ice". Arnaud's cognac version, "served up, in a flute", is "all three counter to the original recipe". The renaissance brought back the arguments over "up or on ice, flute or highball glass". | Oxford pdf 850–851 |
| P5 | The Codex's French 75 uses Plymouth gin, in a flute ("the swankier version"), and notes that "Some bartenders prepare a French 75 in a tall glass with ice like a Collins". Its rule: Champagne adds proof and flavour, so cut the sour base to "half the gin, and two-thirds of the lemon juice and simple syrup". | *Codex* p. 142 |
| P6 | The Codex files sparkling-wine sours in **the Daiquiri's extended family** ("sours that have bubbly ingredients added"), not the Highball's. | *Codex* p. 138 |
| P7 | French 75 guards: Prohibition, "intoxicating", the field gun and the "irresponsible" Jazz Age line (pdf 850–851) stay out. Arnaud's and Chris Hannah are Outlaw-plan ground. The McElhone/Harry's claims are "mostly bogus" (Jester's L1). | Oxford; plans |
