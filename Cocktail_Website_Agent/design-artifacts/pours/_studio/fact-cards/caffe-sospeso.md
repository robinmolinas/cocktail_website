# Fact card: the *caffè sospeso* (suspended coffee), Naples

Opened 2026-10-01 by Hester for regular-guy-caregiver (The Good Samaritan). Every quote below was on screen in this session (S1, S2, S4 by curl + strip; S3 by WebFetch, returned verbatim in Italian after curl got HTTP 406).

## Sources
- **S1** Sylvia Poggioli, "EU Embraces 'Suspended Coffee': Pay It Forward With A Cup Of Joe", NPR, *The Salt*, 24 April 2013 (npr.org/sections/thesalt/2013/04/24/178829301/…; member stations republished it 25 April). Secondary, established press. Quotes Luciano De Crescenzo on the record.
- **S2** Paolo Mancini (Department of Institutions and Society, University of Perugia), "Caffè sospeso (Italy)", Global Informality Project encyclopedia (in-formality.com, oldid 5444). Secondary, academic encyclopedia entry with references. Independent of S1.
- **S3** Luca Palamara, "Dal caffè al tampone sospeso, la solidarietà di Napoli", *Euronews* (Italian edition), 19 November 2020. Secondary, established press. Independent of S1.
- **S4** Snopes, "Suspended Coffee" (fact check). Secondary. Used only for what it says it can't establish, and for showing that the anecdotes in circulation are relays.
- **S5** *ANSA*, 23 September 2023: read for the family plan, **not re-read this session**. Corroboration only, never an anchor.

## Facts
| ID | Fact | Source |
|---|---|---|
| F1 | **The custom:** in a Naples bar, someone pays for a second coffee for a person who can't afford one, to be had later. Three independent sources: "A customer pays in advance for a person who cannot afford a cup of coffee" (S1); "leaving a free coffee for an unidentified future customer" (S2); "ne pagherà un altro, il sospeso, per qualcuno che non se lo può permettere" (S3). | S1, S2, S3 |
| F2 | **The ask:** whoever comes in asks the bar whether a coffee is waiting. S1 (Poggioli's narration): "Is there anything suspended?" S2 gives the question as "'Is there a suspended coffee?'" (the Italian original was lost in the page's markup). **Two independent sources: Wren's condition is met for the ask.** | S1, S2 |
| F3 | **Only in S1:** the barista "would keep a log", and would "nod and serve" the one who asked. Narration, not De Crescenzo's quote. | S1 |
| F4 | **The giver doesn't choose:** the coffee is for "an unidentified future customer" (S2); in De Crescenzo's words, for whoever "entered later" (S1). | S1, S2 |
| F5 | De Crescenzo, to NPR ("he recalls"): someone who'd had "a break of good luck" paid for two cups, not one; the second was "offered to the rest of humankind"; and at that time there were more poor customers than well-off ones. On the record, via a reporter. | S1 |
| F6 | **Mancini's reading (interpretation, a scholar's, labelled):** the question is "a gentle query" that keeps both giver and receiver "in the background"; "the coffee falls down 'from the clouds', not from the donor's hands" (he cites Schwartz 1967). A direct "Would you pay for a coffee for me?" would stress "possible economic differences between the two persons". | S2 |
| F7 | De Crescenzo used the custom as the title of a book, *Il caffè sospeso* (Mondadori). Mancini: the book "does not detail this particular practice". **Book year conflicts** (Mancini's reference says 2005; the Italian catalogues and Wikipedia say 2008): give no year. The De Crescenzo lines in F5 come from NPR's interview, not the book. | S1, S2 |
| F8 | **Only in S1:** it waned in the postwar boom years; for decades it lived mainly at Christmas; it came back with the eurozone crisis; NGOs and the mayor declared 10 December "Suspended Coffee Day". | S1 |
| F9 | **Only in S1:** it spread: more than 150 cafés in Bulgaria; in Barcelona a young man, Gonzalo Sapina, started a network in "a few short weeks"; in France, cafés with the logo "café en attente". | S1 |
| F10 | Present tense in both independent sources: it is described as a practice that exists now (S2 "an old tradition exists"; S3 "Chi prende un caffè … ne pagherà un altro"). | S2, S3 |
| F11 | 2020: the same idea was used for Covid tests in Naples ("Oggi i sospesi non sono i caffè, sono i tamponi"). **Fenced:** medical (Wren's trap 8). | S3 |

## Conflicts and legends
| ID | Note |
|---|---|
| C1 | **Age: four sources, three answers.** S1 "some one-hundred years ago" (and it waned postwar); S3 "risale alla fine della seconda guerra mondiale"; S2 "We can only speculate about the origins"; S4 "we can't say how old or widespread the practice might actually be". **No date, no era, no "old" with a number.** "An old Naples custom" is safe (S2's own words, "an old tradition"). |
| L1 | **Legend:** friends arguing over the bill overpaid, and the extra coffee was left for whoever came in (S2, "According to some literary sources", Pazzaglia and Pipolo 1999). Never in the reading. |
| L2 | **Legend:** "right after the war, many gentlemen had lost everything" and paid for two (S4 relaying an unnamed traveller's account). Never in the reading. |
| C2 | **Motive out:** Mancini's superstition reading ("who knows what may happen to me?") is his speculation about an "unspoken dynamic", not anyone's motive on the record. |
| C3 | **Sibling fences in the sources' own words:** "donors and recipients never meet each other" (S1) is *Serviceable*'s ground; "the recipient doesn't have to show gratitude" (S1) is *Till Spring*'s. |
| C4 | **Source words that aren't ours:** "less fortunate" (De Crescenzo via S1), "charity" (S1), "beggar" and "needy" (S4), "homeless" (Starbucks 2013 coverage). Wren's trap 9. |
| C5 | **The drink:** the sources say "coffee"/"caffè"; S1 names "an espresso or a cappuccino" as Italy's everyday drink, not as the sospeso. Espresso in our glass is our build, never "the sospeso was an espresso". |
| C6 | Out: the Bulgarian self-immolations (S1), Starbucks's 2013 UK tie-in, Cappuccino rules and Venice's 1683 coffeehouse (S1 padding). |
