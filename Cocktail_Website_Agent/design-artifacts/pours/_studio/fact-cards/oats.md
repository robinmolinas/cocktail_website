# Fact card: Oats

| # | Claim (our words) | Source | Tier | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| F1 | Oats are uncommon in spirits today: expensive, and viscous and hard to work with on their own. | Oxford OATS pdf 1414 | primary | |
| F2 | Oats went into Irish pure pot-still whiskies alongside malted and raw barley until the 1950s. | Oxford OATS pdf 1414; SINGLE POT STILL pdf 1808 | primary | Scope: pure (single) pot-still whiskey, not every Irish whiskey. Which whiskey Sheridan poured is not on record. |
| F3 | Oats now have a small revival as a whisky grain. | Oxford MASH pdf 1253; OATS pdf 1414 | primary | So "today's Irish whiskey has no oats" is too strong; "needn't have any" is fine. |
| F4 | By the late 18th century Irish whisky was commonly one-third each green barley, malted barley, and oats or rye. | Oxford MASH BILL pdf 1256 | primary | Oats *or* rye: don't turn it into "a third oats". |
| F5 | Toasting grain: the bran's compounds, when cooked, give roasted and toasted aromas (nuts and cocoa). | Flavor Matrix, Grain, pdf 136 | primary | "Nearly flavourless until cooked" is said of *refined* products like white flour, not of whole rolled oats. "Nuts" is an aroma word here, not an allergen. |
| F6 | Precedent for steeping oats and straining them out in a whisky drink: Atholl brose (oatmeal soaked in water, strained, with honey and scotch). | Oxford ATHOLL BROSE pdf 168 | primary | So our technique isn't new. The 1475 spiked-well story is "custom holds": legend. |
| F7 | Ireland, around 1600: the flavoured spirit (usquebaugh: good barley malt, imported botanicals such as saffron, raisins, fennel seed) "appears to have been the manor-house spirit", served to important English guests like Fynes Moryson (1603). The common people's spirit was different: by the 1600s the Irish outside the English pale distilled their own grain spirit from "rough black oats", perhaps with a little barley malt, unflavoured. | Oxford WHISKY, IRISH pdf 2151–2152 (headword inferred from page order: whisky, corn pdf 2148 → whisky, grain pdf 2149 → Irish) | primary | Oxford hedges "appears". "Entertainment of the great men … of the vulgar" is a 1772 Dublin historian quoted by Oxford. Nickname "balcaan"/"bust-head" and "by beer glassfuls" (Howell 1645): tone, keep out. |
| F8 | "Eventually, the two spirits began to merge": by the early 18th century the oat spirit got better and the usquebaugh lost its flavourings, and "it was all 'whisky'" (the name from the 1730s). | Oxford pdf 2152 | primary | The merge is of the two spirits, not a claim that oats stayed in every whiskey. |
| F9 | After the 1785 malt duty the big Irish distilleries made their wash from some malt plus "a mix of raw barley, rye, and oats". | Oxford pdf 2152 | primary | Links F7–F8 to F2 (oats until the 1950s). |

## Coincidence to own, if F7–F8 is used
- The oat spirit's story (the fine one for important guests, the plain one for everyone, then one whisky for all) has the same shape as Sheridan's (F1–F2 on the Irish Coffee card). Own it in one plain clause; never imply the one caused the other.

## Conflicts
- C1: the share of oats. OATS pdf 1414 says "generally … between 10 percent and 30 percent of the mash"; SINGLE POT STILL pdf 1808 says "relatively small proportions of oats and rye or wheat". Give no number.
- C2: easier or harder? OATS pdf 1414: in a mix with other grains, oats "paradoxically can make the mash easier to manipulate" (general, present tense, not said of Ireland). MASH pdf 1253: rye and oats "are the most challenging to work with, as they are quite glutinous". Contested: keep "oats made the mash easier" out of guest text.

## Unverified
- U1: Flavor Matrix Grain chart (pdf 137). OCR text only (the page image can't be rendered here). The OCR shows milk/cream, coffee and *bourbon* whiskey, not Irish, and doesn't show which ring they sit on. No reading line may rest on it.

## Not a source for the allergen
- "Glutinous" (pdf 1253) means sticky, not gluten. The gluten classification rests on STUDIO-RULES 4 (safe side), the Mixologist's call, not on Oxford.
