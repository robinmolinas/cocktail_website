# Fact card: Roy Olmstead and *Olmstead v. United States* (1928)

Written by Hester, 2026-10-01, for outlaw-regular-guy (The Gangster). All pages fetched and read in full this session (curl + strip; FJC PDF text extracted with PDFKit).

## Sources
- **HL** = Daryl C. McClary, "Olmstead, Roy (1886-1966)", HistoryLink.org Essay 4015, posted 11/13/2002 (corrected 2010, 2011). Secondary; cites Norman H. Clark, *The Dry Years* (1988) and PNQ 1963, plus Seattle P-I and Times.
- **LII** = *Olmstead v. United States*, 277 U.S. 438, argued Feb. 20-21, decided June 4, 1928. Cornell LII. Primary.
- **FJC** = Richard F. Hamm, *Olmstead v. United States: The Constitutional Challenges of Prohibition Enforcement*, Federal Judicial Center, 2010. Reliable secondary (government historical office, named scholar).
- **HW** = Hong Ta, "How to Bootleg Nicely", *Spark*, Humanities Washington, Dec 4, 2023 (interview with Steve Edmiston, lawyer). Secondary, weaker (interview).
- **KATZ** = *Katz v. United States*, 389 U.S. 347, argued Oct. 17, decided Dec. 18, 1967. Cornell LII. Primary.

## Facts
| id | fact | source | notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| F1 | Born 18 Sept 1886, Beaver City, Nebraska; came to Seattle 1904; joined Seattle police 16 May 1907 (FJC says 1906); sergeant 1910; youngest lieutenant, "the baby lieutenant". | HL; FJC p. 29 | Police start year conflicts (1907/1906): keep out. Lieutenant: HL acting 1917, permanent 1919; FJC 1916. Keep year out. |
| F2 | Arrested 22 March 1920 unloading Canadian whiskey at Meadowdale; dismissed from the force; pleaded guilty, fined $500. | HL; FJC p. 7 | |
| F3 | **Before Prohibition, as an officer, he sold his probation recommendations and took bribes**, which gave him capital. | FJC pp. 7, 29 | Counterweight. He was himself an enforcer who let himself off. |
| F4 | **He bribed the Seattle police and the King County sheriffs** to ignore his operation. The Supreme Court's own statement of facts: the wiretaps showed his dealings with Seattle police, messages securing release of his arrested men, and "direct promises to officers of payments". | FJC p. 7; LII (Taft, facts) | The reading can never imply he was clean. |
| F5 | He forged documents (manifesting cargo for Mexico) to avoid Canada's $20/case export tax, and sold 30% cheaper than rivals; rivals put out of business turned to "the violent occupation of hijacking". | HL; FJC p. 7 ("forged") | Smuggling/sea is fenced out of the family anyway. |
| F6 | **No-guns rule:** he did not allow his employees to carry firearms. HL: "He told his men he would rather lose a shipment of liquor than a life" (reported speech, **no quotation marks on the page**). HW gives a different wording in quotation marks: "No amount of money is worth a human life". | HL; HW | Two secondaries, two wordings: the rule is solid, **his exact words are not**. Paraphrase, never quote as his. The rule as recorded covers his *employees*; nothing says he himself carried none, and no source records a shipment actually lost because of it. |
| F7 | HW: he "encouraged his bootleggers to use bribes rather than violence"; refused to cut liquor with toxins; no narcotics, gambling or gun-running; the "Good Bootlegger" title. | HW (single, interview) | Bribery is part of the same code. "Good Bootlegger" may be said as a nickname per HW; HL calls him "King of the Puget Sound Bootleggers", FJC "King of the Northwest Bootleggers". |
| F8 | No source read records violence by his own men. A 1920 P-I headline in HL's bibliography: "Two Leaders of Police Among Prisoners After Bullets Fly at Autos" (shooter not stated). | HL sources | Keep the bullets out. |
| F9 | By 1924 "a popular figure on the Seattle scene", rubbing elbows with the mayor. Married Elise Caroline Parché (Campbell) 5 Aug 1924; FJC: "known as Elsie". Radio KFQX; agents "speculated" about coded bedtime-story messages. | HL; FJC p. 29 | The coded-message story is speculation (Wren: stays out). |
| F10 | Wiretaps placed months before Oct 1924, on four homes and the chief office, by four federal prohibition officers; Taft: inserted "without trespass". | HL; LII | |
| F11 | **Wiretapping was a crime under Washington law**: 1909 statute, intercepting a telephone message is a misdemeanor (Remington 1922 § 2656(18)), quoted by **Taft's majority itself**; Brandeis: "By the laws of Washington, wire tapping is a crime." | LII | The majority conceded the act was a state misdemeanor and admitted the evidence anyway (common law). |
| F12 | The government's own brief, quoted in Brandeis's footnote: the Prohibition Unit of the Treasury "disclaims" wiretapping and the Department of Justice "has frowned on it". | LII (Brandeis fn) | The system's own agencies disowned the method it used. |
| F13 | Convicted 20 Feb 1926 with 20 others; four years at McNeil Island, $8,000 fine. Released 12 May 1931. | HL | |
| F14 | **Decided 5-4.** Dissents: Holmes, Brandeis, Butler, Stone (Stone concurring in Holmes's and Brandeis's). | LII | Not "two justices": four. Two (Holmes, Brandeis) made the "government breaking the law" point; Butler confined himself to the Fourth Amendment. |
| F15 | Holmes: government "ought not to use evidence obtained and only obtainable by a criminal act"; "dirty business"; "a less evil that some criminals should escape than that the government should play an ignoble part"; it "makes no difference" that the crime is under state, not federal, law. | LII | Yes, "dirty business" turns on the officers' crime (the state misdemeanor). FJC attributes Brandeis's "lawbreaker" line to Holmes: an FJC slip; LII is the record. |
| F16 | Brandeis: officials "shall be subjected to the same rules of conduct that are commands to the citizen"; "If the government becomes a lawbreaker, it breeds contempt for law"; "the right to be let alone". The "potent, the omnipresent teacher" sentence begins "Our government". | LII | Paraphrase in any reading ("Our" sentence; and our-words rule). |
| F17 | Pardoned by Franklin D. Roosevelt, 25 Dec 1935, "mainly due to the efforts" of Elise; fine and $2,288 costs remitted. | HL | |
| F18 | After release: Christian Science practitioner; visited jails and prisons in the Puget Sound area; King County Jail every Monday morning in his last years. | HL | Wren: to the dossier (In One Piece owns visiting). |
| F19 | Died 30 April 1966, aged 79; HL: "would always be known in Seattle as 'King of the Puget Sound Bootleggers.'" | HL | What Seattle remembers, per HL, is the title, not the rule. |
| F20 | *Katz v. United States*, decided **18 Dec 1967** (Stewart): Olmstead's "trespass" doctrine "can no longer be regarded as controlling"; wiretap-type listening is a search. Harlan (concurring): must be recognized as overruling Olmstead. Black (dissenting): Olmstead was never overruled. | KATZ; FJC p. 24 | Brandeis's *Fourth Amendment* view won; Katz did not rule on the state-crime point (Holmes's). Say "the Court later set aside that ruling's reasoning" or "came round to the dissent's view on wiretaps", about 19 months after he died. HW says "relied on Brandeis' dissent": interview, not the opinion. |

## Guards
- Never imply he was clean: bribery of police (F3, F4) is on the Supreme Court's own record.
- No quotation marks on his words (F6).
- "Bound him first" and "cost him cargo" are not on the page (F6).
- Four dissents, 5-4 (F14).
- Katz date and scope (F20).
