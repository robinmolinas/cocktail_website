# Fact card: Aeneas Coffey and the Coffey (column) still

Verified 2026-10-01 by Hester for magician-explorer (The Alchemist). Library = primary; web = secondary (named, dated).

## Facts
| id | fact | source | tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| F1 | Coffey (1780–1852) entered the excise at its lowest level in 1800 ("land-waiter, guager and searcher"); bayonet wounds in the "poitín wars" with smugglers. | Oxford, COFFEY, AENEAS, pdf 541 | primary |
| F2 | Surveyor for Dublin City 1809; inspector-general of excise in Ireland 1819 (Oxford). Wikipedia, citing the DIB, gives inspector-general 1816–1824: the appointment year conflicts, so never state it. | pdf 541; en.wikipedia.org "Aeneas Coffey" (lead only) | primary / lead |
| F3 | Left the excise in 1824, "at the age of forty-four", and bought the Dodder Bank Distillery in Dublin. pdf 752: "inspector general of excise in Ireland until 1824". Mulvihill: "spent years hunting illicit whiskey makers only to switch in 1824 and open his own distillery." | pdf 541, pdf 752; Mary Mulvihill, *Irish Times*, 26 Feb 2004 | primary; secondary |
| F4 | His prototype stills were wood and iron, a single column, steam-heated like Stein's. "This was soon replaced" by two copper columns (analyzer, rectifier), perforated copper plates in place of Stein's horsehair ones, and rectifying pipes to remove residual oils. The prototype is **undated and unplaced** on the page; one prototype design, no count of attempts. | pdf 541 | primary |
| F5 | Oxford's terms for "better": simpler, more robust, easier to manufacture; stronger and purer spirit; more economical to heat; better rectifying. Against Stein's: "the simpler and more effective of the two" (pdf 2169). Against Cellier-Blumenthal's: "almost as simple … and just as effective", its advantage being the split column, "much easier to install" (pdf 1901). | pdf 541, 2169, 1901 | primary |
| F6 | Counterweight: the grain spirit was "higher strength, purer (and thus blander), and much cheaper to make" than pot-still malt. | pdf 2169 | primary |
| F7 | Scorn: "The leading Irish distillers initially scorned Coffey's stills"; the quote ("These things no more yield whiskey than they yield wine or beer") is from the Big Four Dublin firms' *Truths about Whiskey*, **1878**. | pdf 541 (bibliography pdf 542) | primary |
| F8 | Uptake: within ten years of the patent, thirteen Irish distilleries had adopted it; Grange, the first Scottish, 1834; eleven more by 1850. | pdf 541 | primary |
| F9 | Counterweight: the Irish resisted "successfully": in 1887 Barnard found only 5 of 28 Irish distilleries with Coffey stills, all working for export; "Coffey-still whisky was not sold in Ireland." Mulvihill: Scotland "won the world whisky market" with it, "a blow from which Irish distillers are still recovering." | pdf 2153; *Irish Times* 2004 | primary; secondary |
| F10 | Built on others: Stein (1828) took "the first steps", on Cellier-Blumenthal's principles; "Coffey greatly improved this design and made it more robust." Never "he invented continuous distillation." | pdf 541; pdf 419; pdf 660 ("perfected by, among others") | primary |
| F11 | Significance: "revolutionized the British spirits industry by making continuous distillation commercially viable" (pdf 543); "the first commercially viable continuous still adapted to distilling grain" (pdf 541); the Coffey still is "the ancestor of most modern continuous or column stills" (pdf 1618). Note: "remains the basis for the modern column still" is said of **Cellier-Blumenthal** (pdf 419), not Coffey. | pdf 543, 541, 1618, 419 | primary |
| F12 | Later: moved the business to London, died in Bromley, Middlesex, 1852; firm to son Philip, then (1872) to John Dore, last foreman (Dore: *Down the Line*'s ground, leave out). Two brothers who worked for him (the Carters) invented the percolation gin still (pdf 1495). | pdf 541, 1495 | primary |
| F13 | What Coffey's still ran on: Oxford says only "adapted to distilling grain" (pdf 541); in Scotland it made whisky "from mixed grains" (pdf 2169). No page names Coffey's own grain. Cheap imported maize and wheat replaced local cereals in patent stills "by the mid-nineteenth century" (MASH BILL, pdf 1256): the trade's, not dated to Coffey. | pdf 541, 2169, 1256 | primary |
| F14 | Scotch grain whisky today "tends to be made from corn (maize) or soft winter wheat" with some malted barley; some distillers use mixed mash bills, rye or 100% malted barley (WHISKY, GRAIN, pdf 2149). Wheat "is common in the production of scotch grain whisky" (WHEAT, pdf 2129). So the grain is the bottle's, not the style's. | pdf 2149, 2129 | primary |
| F15 | Counterweight for the glass: grain whisky is lighter than malt, "will age more quickly (after twelve years, it tends to taste mostly of the barrel)" and "on its own easily disappears in a mixed drink" (pdf 2149). | pdf 2149 | primary |

## Conflicts (keep out of any reading)
- C1 **Patent year:** 1828 (STILL, CONTINUOUS, pdf 1901), 1830 (pdf 541, 752, 2169; Mulvihill 2004), 1831 (COFFEY STILL, pdf 543). *Joy of Mixology* (pdf 419) says "circa 1860": wrong, never used. State no year.
- C2 Inspector-general year (1819 Oxford vs 1816 Wikipedia/DIB). Use only "1824" for the leaving.
- C3 "Left" (Oxford) vs "resigned" (Wikipedia citing DIB; DIB itself unreadable here, JS wall). Say "left".
- C4 Born 1780, "one source" says French-born (pdf 541); age 44 is Oxford's.
