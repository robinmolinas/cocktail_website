# Fact card: Embury's Canadian cocktail and maple syrup

| E1 | Oxford MAPLE SYRUP lists "David Embury's Canadian Cocktail, with Canadian whisky, bitters, and curaçao" among drinks where maple "turns up". | Oxford pdf 1226 (Shawn Soole) | The sentence runs the drinks together. |
| E2 | **Corrected 2026-10-01 r5 (my r3 row was wrong).** Embury's CANADIAN: "This cocktail is straight Canadian whisky with 2 or 3 dashes each of curaçao and Angostura and 1 teaspoonful of sugar syrup to each drink. Maple syrup is sometimes used in place of the sugar." The entry ends there (next: CANASTA). No whisky measure, no method, no glass in the entry. | Embury 1958/1961/2008, OCR highlight (all three agree) | **Struck: "Embury's CANADIAN has no maple".** My r3 highlight was cut off one sentence early. Oxford pdf 1226 was right. Never say his entry gives a stir, a glass or a measure of whisky. The maple is his noted variant ("sometimes used"), not his main recipe. |
| E3 | Embury's CANADIAN COLLINS: "Use Canadian whisky and, in place of sugar syrup, use maple syrup." | Embury, OCR highlight (index: Canadian Collins p. 284 in 1958 ed.) | The sourced maple + Canadian whisky pairing is a Collins (highball), not the cocktail. |
| E4 | A **different** drink, "a specialty of the Canadian Club of New York City" (its entry ends "…dashes Angostura to each drink. Stir or shake."): "It can be improved upon by cutting down somewhat on the maple syrup and adding a few dashes of lemon juice. Compare the Applejack Rabbit and the Old Vermont". | Embury, OCR highlight | Name and base not captured. The improvement is less maple *and* lemon. Not about the CANADIAN; never transfer it. |
| E5 | Applejack Rabbit: 1 maple syrup, 1 lemon, 1 orange, 6 apple brandy; Embury: "Don't shy away from it. It is not half bad." Maple sugar "has the same disadvantage" as dry sugar vs syrup. | Embury, OCR highlight | |
| E6 | Oxford: maple "is quite penetrating, and if not deployed carefully, it can dominate a drink". | Oxford pdf 1226 | Soole's warning, not Embury's. |
| E8 | Oxford EMBURY (pdf 719): *The Fine Art of Mixing Drinks*, "originally published in 1948", "the seminal work" for "many decades"; he held that cocktails "should never have more than a hint of sweetness"; **counterweight:** in 1946–47, as attorney for the National Interfraternity Council, he "outspokenly led" efforts to prevent the racial integration of college fraternities. | Oxford pdf 719 | "Famous book" is safe. The sweetness rule is his own, on record, and it's the honest source for holding the maple back. The counterweight is Wren's call: name the book, never praise the man. The 1948 edition wasn't read, so no "1948 recipe". |
| E7 | Canadian blends came to be called "ryes" during and right after Prohibition, when "much of the bootleg trade came from our northern neighbors", who then used much rye. | *Joy* pdf 423 (reliable secondary) | Verified on the page. |
