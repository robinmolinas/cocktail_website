# Fact card: Lord Invader and "Rum and Coca-Cola"

Our words throughout. Facts are free; the sources' sentences are not. Quotes below are dossier-length and stay out of any pour unless Wren places one.

## Facts

| # | Claim (our words) | Source | Tier | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| F1 | Rupert Grant, a Trinidadian calypso singer whose stage name was Lord Invader, wrote the words of "Rum and Coca-Cola" in early 1943. | Khan, Finding 1 ("during February, 1943"); Baron ("Between December, 1942 and January, 1943"); SFW p. 4 ("circa January 1943") | primary | **Month conflicts across sources: say "in 1943" or "during the war", never a month.** Born Port of Spain, 13 Dec 1914 (SFW p. 3). |
| F2 | He sang in Mohamed Khan's calypso tent in Port of Spain. Khan testified that calypsos were first submitted to a board of censors chaired by the Commissioner of Police before they could be performed, and that this song went through it. | Khan (Khan "in whose tent Grant performed"; booklet back cover: "Victory Calypso Tent, 95 Edward Street") | primary | **It's Khan's testimony: tell it as "songs had to pass the police censors first", sourced to how the tent worked, never as a ban on this song.** Nothing says the censors objected to it. |
| F3 | The song was a hit in Trinidad: the 1948 court found it "was sung a great deal by both natives and soldiers". | Baron | primary | The soldiers sang the joke about themselves. "Natives" is the court's 1948 word: never in guest text. |
| F4 | What it laughed at: the 1947 court called its chorus a comment on "a development in the economy of Trinidad" too important to escape the calypso shows. The 1948 court called it, in part, a "ribald comment" on the American soldiers and the islanders. | Khan; Baron | primary | **Wren's guard: say the target as what the soldiers' dollars were doing to the island; never the women, never the lyric's subject.** SFW p. 4 (Ramsey): calypsonians called the US bases, slyly, "the American social invasion". |
| F5 | The defence argued the lyric was "salacious, immoral and lewd"; the court rejected it, and noted that the defendants' own verses were no more edifying. | Khan | primary | Good for the Subverter (the people who took the joke called the original dirty) but it must not drift into the lyric's subject. |
| F6 | Morey Amsterdam, an American comedian, heard it in Trinidad in September 1943, took it back to New York and copyrighted it in 1944 as his own (with Jeri Sullavan and Paul Baron). The Andrews Sisters' record was a hit by the end of 1944. | Khan ("in his capacity as a public entertainer"; copyright Dec 1944); *Baron* ("arrived in Trinidad as an entertainer for the U.S.O.", stayed about a month); SFW p. 4 ("the American comedian Morey Amsterdam") | primary + secondary | **The joke was taken by a professional comedian.** The judge found the taking "deliberate and intentional", and said Amsterdam's testimony would have been more convincing had he "frankly avowed the true situation". |
| F7 | The first 200,000 Andrews Sisters records named Amsterdam as the composer of words and music. | Baron | primary | "Sung back by people who never hear what it was about" (Wren's fear) has its page here and in F11. |
| F8 | Grant learned of it in January 1945, reached New York late in February 1945, and protested to Feist's general manager that he was the composer. The court accepted that statement "as the basis of this decision". | SFW p. 4; Khan | secondary + primary | |
| F9 | **The plaintiff was Khan, not Grant.** Grant had given Khan the rights for the tent's 1943 booklet ("All Rights Reserved by M. H. Khan"); on the stand he said "I don't own no rights". The court found Grant the author (Finding 1), found infringement, enjoined the defendants, ordered an accounting and the infringing copies destroyed. Affirmed on appeal (December 1947, SFW p. 5). | Khan, Findings 1–8, Conclusions I–IV; Baron (cites the affirmance) | primary | **Never "he sued" or "he won it back".** Say "the court found the words were his" / "a court in New York put his name on it". SFW p. 5 says "Invader's case ... was sustained": Cowley's framing; the record's plaintiff rules. |
| F10 | The tune wasn't his: it was Lionel Belasco's "L'Année Passée" (1906), which Grant learned from Belasco's cousin Cyril Monrose in 1942, changing a few notes of the last phrase. He didn't copyright the music because he believed it was Belasco's. Belasco's publisher won the melody case in 1948. | Baron | primary | **Never "his tune", "his song" for the music, or "he wrote the song" without "the words".** |
| F11 | The radio refusal: many US networks at first refused to play a song about an alcoholic drink. | Oxford, COCKTAIL MUSIC (pdf 504) | primary | SFW p. 14 (citing *Time* 29 Jan 1945 and *PM*): banned for free advertising, "lustiness in the lyrics" and alcohol. **Either way, never "banned for its bite / its satire".** |
| F12 | **He kept joking, on the record (point 3).** In New York around January 1946, while the case was pending, he recorded "Yankee Dollar", which *Down Beat* (11 March 1946) called "a sort of sequel" to "Rum and Coca-Cola". Its chorus turns his own fame into the punchline: having sung the hit doesn't help him without the dollars. | SFW pp. 12–13 (Disc 5008, album 614) | secondary | The verses are about Port of Spain women and the soldiers' money: **only the chorus's self-mockery is usable, never the women.** "The joke on himself" is our reading of Cowley's gloss: label it. |
| F13 | **He kept aiming up (point 3, stronger).** Around 24 March 1959 he recorded "Crisis in Arkansas" (Folkways FW 8733), aimed at Governor Orval Faubus, who had kept nine Black students out of Little Rock Central High in 1957. Its chorus mocks the governor's black bow tie and tuxedo: he calls Black people names while dressed in black. | SFW pp. 23–24 | secondary | A joke at power, told openly, fourteen years after the theft. Keep to the bow-tie joke: the verse line about breaking the governor's tongue stays out. Wren decides whether civil rights reads as "a political side today" (my view: segregation is history, not a party). |
| F14 | Also on the record: in 1946 he added a verse to "God Made Us All" after a South Carolina police chief was acquitted of blinding the Black veteran Isaac Woodard. | SFW p. 15 | secondary | **Authorship contested:** SFW cites the *Sunday Guardian* (17 Jan 1943) for Lord Pretender singing "God Made Us All" in Trinidad; Seeger's spoken intro says Invader made it up for People's Songs. Never "he wrote God Made Us All". Serious, not a joke: dossier only. |
| F15 | **The ending, after the page (point 5).** The defendants didn't pay him until seven years later. On 26 Feb 1955 the Trinidad *Evening News* reported he was going to the US to collect the money he'd won. Folkways correspondence shows that even after the settlement he was always short of money. | SFW pp. 4–5 | secondary | **Counterweight, carried openly:** the court gave him the authorship, not a fortune, and late. The figure conflicts ($132,000 in the 1955 paper; $150,000 on Wikipedia): **no figure in the reading.** Never "he got rich", "he won it back". |
| F16 | He kept recording for Moses Asch at Folkways to the end: children's calypsos (1958), *Calypso Travels* (1959), a 1960 album, and a last session on 6 June 1961. He died at Brooklyn hospital on 15 October 1961, after a short illness and two operations. | SFW pp. 5–6 | secondary | **Year conflicts inside SFW:** the back cover says 1914–1962; p. 6 gives the dated death, 1961. Prefer no year; if needed, "in 1961", flagged. The Smithsonian now keeps the recordings (this CD). |
| F17 | Pepsi reportedly approached him in 1945; he recorded a cheerful Pepsi-Cola song around 1947 that stayed unreleased for about fifty years. | SFW pp. 13–14 (*Chicago Defender* 24 Mar 1945) | secondary | **Counterweight, dossier only:** a guest who looks him up may find it. Not a sell-out on the record (nothing transpired), but never claim he refused. |
| F18 | Rum with Coca-Cola is older than the song: Oxford says it was already popular in Venezuela by 1911, and the Cuba Libre is "also known as" Rum and Coca-Cola. *Joy* says the drink became popular in the 1940s, popularised by the Andrews Sisters' version of the calypso "penned by Lord Invader". | Oxford pdf 107, pdf 613; *Joy* pdf 71 | primary | **Never "the song's drink" or "he named it".** "Popular in the 1940s, carried by the song" is *Joy*'s claim. |

## Guards (summary)
- Khan sued, Grant was found the author; the music was Belasco's. "His words", never "his song" alone in a claim about ownership.
- No month for the writing; no dollar figure; no death year without the flag.
- The censors are Khan's testimony about the tent's routine.
- Radio: drink, advertising, lyrics. Never "the joke was banned".
- The lyric's subject and the women never appear; the target is the soldiers' money.
