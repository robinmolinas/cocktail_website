# Fact card: the Caipirinha, cachaça, and Brazil's legal definition

Hester, 2026-10-01, for regular-guy-innocent (The Bumpkin). Every quote below was on screen in the call that wrote it. Tiers: Oxford = primary (library); Brazilian decrees = primary (law, planalto.gov.br, fetched 2026-10-01).

## The drink (Oxford, CAIPIRINHA, pdf 383-384, David Wondrich)
- **F1** Cachaça, sugar, ice and muddled lime (often other fruits); "considered by non-Brazilians to be the national drink". In Brazil, the most famous of the batidas. (pdf 383)
- **F2** Before the 1950s it "appears to have been" called *pinga com limão*, cane spirit with lime. One of its original names "appears to have been 'Batida Paulista'", in the 1950s. (pdf 383) Hedged twice by Wondrich: keep "appears".
- **F3** *Pinga com limão* is "of no great antiquity": found only from the mid-nineteenth century (folk song cure for the evil eye; Paraty's 1856 cholera ration; Wells's refresco, 1880s). (pdf 383) **Never "centuries-old".** Cures and cholera: never in guest text (Wren).
- **F4** In São Paulo, first half of the 20th century, the Batida Paulista showed American influence, "not only in its incorporation of ice": some with simple syrup, some with egg white and a "splash" of gin, which Mário de Andrade called "sophisticated" (1944; Wondrich's English, in quotation marks; Andrade wrote in Portuguese, in an article titled "Os eufemismos da cachaça"). (pdf 383) Andrade's tone is unknown: neither a sneer nor praise.
- **F5** "Some drinkers, however, rejected such innovations." (pdf 383) The page does not say those drinkers made the caipirinha (Wren's note a).
- **F6** "A third way, one that incorporated the ice from the new drink into a nostalgic, rustic preparation built simply in the glass." Wondrich's reading, by analogy with the Old-Fashioned. (pdf 384)
- **F7** Name: *caipirinha*, roughly "Little Hillbilly"; "Caipira" is "a southern Brazilian epithet for a person from the rural hinterlands". Occasional by the 1950s, widespread by the end of the 1960s; by then known throughout Brazil. (pdf 384) Who named it: not on the page. "Little Hillbilly": never in guest text (Wren).
- **F8** Abroad: "another twenty years" to catch on outside expatriate communities; "a strong vogue in Europe" in the 1980s, "where it remains popular". US: "more mentioned than served" (out). (pdf 384)
- **F9** "Fashionable city dwellers found cachaça too rustic and backward-looking" and chose vodka: the Caipiroska, which "first appears in the early 1980s". Others chose white rum or other fruits. (pdf 384)

## Conflict inside Oxford (keep the contested details out)
- **C1** CACHAÇA (pdf 380, Frederick H. Smith): "the Batida Paulista, is better known by its alternate name, the Caipirinha", and it "made it onto the global stage in the 1970s", while cachaça was in "one of its periodic bouts of unfashionableness" in Brazil. Against F6/F8: Smith makes the Batida Paulista and the Caipirinha one drink, and dates the drink abroad to the 1970s, not the 1980s. **So:** (a) the caipirinha as a *separate* drink that turned the improvers down is Wondrich's account, told as his ("as one historian tells it") or signposted; (b) no decade for its success abroad; (c) "the city swapped the cane out in the same decade Europe fell for it" can't be stated.
- **C2** DAWA (pdf 631, Wondrich): Kenya's near-national drink is "a Kenyan take on the Caipirinha, or rather the Caipiroska" (vodka), Carnivore, Nairobi, 1980. So "the one the world orders" is unsafe: abroad, the vodka version travelled too.

## Cachaça (Oxford, CACHAÇA, pdf 377-380, Frederick H. Smith)
- **F10** Loved and scorned: by the end of the 19th century "an essential, if complicated, icon of Brazilian identity, both loved for its deep traditions and scorned for its perceived rusticity and backwardness". (pdf 379)
- **F11** Burton (1869) noted a Brazilian "prejudice against mixing" cachaça; it eased only in the 20th century, with the batida. (pdf 380)
- **F12** Brazilian law since 2001: cachaça from pure cane juice; 38-48% ABV. (pdf 377, 379) Out of guest text: "peasants and the enslaved" (pdf 378), Burton's "lowest grade" (pdf 378-379), backwoods stills that refill bottles (pdf 379).

## The law (primary, planalto.gov.br, fetched 2026-10-01)
- **L1** Decreto nº 4.851, 2 Oct 2003, amending art. 81 of the 1997 beverage regulation: "Caipirinha é a bebida típica brasileira", 15-36% ABV, "obtida exclusivamente com Cachaça, acrescida de limão e açúcar" (made exclusively with cachaça, with lime and sugar added). Revoked by Decreto 6.871/2009.
- **L2** Decreto nº 6.871, 4 Jun 2009, art. 68 § 5 (art. 68 = "bebida alcoólica mista ou coquetel"): the mixed drink at 15-36%, "elaborada com cachaça, limão e açúcar, poderá ser denominada de caipirinha (bebida típica do Brasil)". Revoked by Decreto 12.709/2025.
- **L3** Decreto nº 12.709, 31 Oct 2025, art. 74 (in force): "Caipirinha é a bebida típica da República Federativa do Brasil, industrializada", 15-36%, "elaborada com cachaça, limão e açúcar".
- **Scope guards:** these decrees regulate drinks produced and sold as products (the 2025 text says "industrializada"); they don't govern what a bar or a home pours. "Exclusively" is the 2003 wording only. Never "banned the vodka version" or "made it illegal": the law says what may carry the name. Safe line: *Brazil's law defines a caipirinha as cachaça, lime and sugar, and has since 2003.* Our translation of the Portuguese is ours.

## Legends (never as fact)
- **G1** The widely told origin as a 1918 Spanish-flu remedy (lime, garlic, honey) is not in Oxford, which finds only the mid-19th-century record (F3). Legend; and cures are out of guest text anyway.
