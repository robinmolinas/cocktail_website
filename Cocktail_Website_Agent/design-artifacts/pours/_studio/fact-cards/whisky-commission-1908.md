# Fact card: the 1908 British commission on what "whisky" may mean

Verified 2026-10-01 by Hester for ruler-sage (The Judge). Library = primary (Oxford Companion); web = secondary, here only archive.org full-text OCR highlights (labelled; cut-off highlights are incomplete and are never completed from memory). Guest text never says "Royal" (archetype pun): "a British commission", "a commission".

## Facts
| id | fact | source | tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| F1 | Grain whisky as a drink dates to the 1830s, when continuous stills were taken up in the Scottish Lowlands; it was light-flavoured but pure, and cheap enough to sell to city factory workers. | Oxford, WHISKY, GRAIN, pdf 2149 | primary |
| F2 | The pot-still malt makers and the continuous-still operators argued, heatedly, over whether the new spirit was whisky at all. | pdf 2149 | primary |
| F3 | The 1908 Royal Commission on Whiskey and Other Potable Spirits settled that argument "(legally, anyway)" — Oxford's own hedge: the legal question closed, the argument didn't. | pdf 2149 | primary |
| F4 | Its outcome, per ADULTERATION: definitions and labelling rules, without banning blending and without stopping column-still spirit from being labelled whisky. | Oxford, ADULTERATION, pdf 70 | primary |
| F5 | The definition as later books quote it: whisky is a spirit distilled from a mash of cereal grains saccharified (starch turned to sugar) by the diastase (enzymes) of malt; Scotch whisky is whisky so defined, distilled in Scotland. **No still type is named.** Daiches follows the definition with the point that pot-still malt fits it too (highlight cut off). Lucas (1920): grain spirit so made "is just as much whisky as" (cut off). | archive.org FTS highlights: Wilson, *Scotch: Its History and Romance* (1973); Daiches, *Scotch Whisky: Its Past and Present* (1976/77); McDowall, *The Whiskies of Scotland* (1967); Lucas, *Legal Chemistry* (1920) | secondary (OCR highlight) |
| F6 | Witnesses gave evidence in person in 1908: F. J. Kelly for Booth's gin (pdf 317); a Demerara rum distiller, unnamed (pdf 1697); Herman Jansen on genever, *Minutes of Evidence* vol. 2 (pdf 875). No whisky-side witness is named in the library. | pdf 317, 1697, 875 | primary |
| F7 | The grain side was not small: the Distillers Company Ltd began in 1877 as a defensive alliance of six of the largest Scottish grain distillers, and became the world's largest whisky company by the 1930s. | Oxford, DCL, pdf 666 | primary |
| F8 | Afterwards, the authenticity argument won the public: through decades of marketing it "has prevailed" (Oxford's general statement, US straights and Scotch single malt together). | pdf 70 | primary |
| F9 | Grain whisky's "acceptance as a distinct category" is credited to the 1908 commission; the category is current (Scotland, Ireland, Japan). | pdf 2149 | primary |
| F10 | Blending pot- and column-still spirits in bond was allowed by an 1860 act of Parliament (23 & 24 Vict. c. 114); blends of the two quickly became the norm in Scotland. So blending was already legal before 1908: the commission left it legal (F4). | Oxford, BLENDING, pdf 284 | primary |
| F11 | Blended Scotch is malt and grain whisky together: Usher's 1853 blend of malt and grain; single malt and blended malt are by law 100% malt (so a blend without "malt" in its category name carries grain). | Oxford, WHISKY, pdf 2132; BARLEY, pdf 209 | primary |
| F12 | The pot-still side wasn't only Scottish: the big Dublin distillers scorned the column still (*Truths about Whiskey*, 1878). | Oxford, COFFEY, pdf 541 (card `aeneas-coffey.md` F7) | primary |

## Conflicts (keep out of any reading)
- C1 **The label condition.** pdf 2149 says the new spirit was whisky "as long as you called it 'grain whisky'", not plain whisky. pdf 70 says the commission did *not* stop column-still spirit being labelled whisky, and the definition quoted by four later books (F5) names no still. Oxford contradicts itself; the books outside it side with pdf 70. **Never state the "grain whisky" label condition.**
- C2 **Report year.** Oxford cites the *Interim Report* (1908) throughout; the definition (F5) belongs to the commission's report, but no highlight read here dates it. Say "a British commission in 1908" for the sitting and the evidence; give no year for the final definition.
- C3 What prompted the commission, and who chaired it, are not in the library and were not read: tell neither.

## Our readings (never facts)
- R1 The newer, cheaper spirit was ruled whisky against the older makers' wish: F2 + F4 + F5 support "ruled whisky"; "against their wish" is inference from "heated debate" (F2). Say "the older makers said it wasn't".
- R2 It protected the drinker of the cheaper bottle: our reading, from F1 + F4. No one on the page says so as a motive.
