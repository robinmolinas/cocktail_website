# Fact card: Henry Africa's, the fern bars, and the Lemon Drop

Opened 2026-10-01 by Hester for ruler-explorer (The Reformer). Every quotation was read on screen in the same call it was copied from; quotations stay under 15 words. Tiers: Oxford Companion and *A Proper Drink* are the library (primary / reliable secondary); the *San Francisco Chronicle* is secondary (web, named reporter, fetched in full).

## Facts
| ID | Fact | Source |
|---|---|---|
| F1 | Norman Jay Hobday (1933-2011) opened Henry Africa's in San Francisco in 1969. | Oxford pdf 995 |
| F2 | It opened on the corner of Broadway and Polk; it moved to Van Ness Avenue and Vallejo (by 1971). | Proper p. 51; Chronicle 1986 item |
| F3 | The saloons before the singles bars were "dark, clubby, male-dominated". | Oxford pdf 995 |
| F4 | Many singles bars were decorated like 1890s Gilded Age lounges, which "had often banned unescorted women". Oxford flags the irony itself ("Not without irony"). | Oxford pdf 995 |
| F5 | They "fancifully resurrected" the era's brass rails, Tiffany lamps and potted plants. A revival of a look, not furniture kept from an old room. | Oxford pdf 995 |
| F6 | Fern bars turned barrooms into bright rooms where "both sexes could mingle on equal terms". | Oxford pdf 770 |
| F7 | The turn was made by feminists, ecologists "but by nostalgists" too; the rooms reflected the sixties obsession with "retro camp". Nostalgia is on the page; so is camp (half a joke). | Oxford pdf 770 |
| F8 | Fern bars "can take credit for bringing America's saloon culture back from the dead", by attracting young professionals who had largely left drinking for drugs. | Oxford pdf 770 |
| F9 | Most fern bars died in the Reagan era. | Oxford pdf 771 |
| F10 | Henry Africa's closed in 1986 (lease renegotiation, per the 1986 item). | Oxford pdf 995; Chronicle 1986 item |
| F11 | The craft revival's principles included: keep "the gender-inclusive bars" (and the women behind the bar since the 1970s), and reach back before Prohibition for the craft. | Oxford pdf 1332 |
| F12 | Hobday "loved his bar as much as his customers did" and legally took the name Henry Africa, keeping it until his death. | Oxford pdf 995 |
| F13 | **Hobday's own account (2011):** when he spotted the site it was a bar "with blackened windows" owned by a former ballplayer. | Chronicle 2011 |
| F14 | **Hobday's own account:** "there wasn't enough money to remodel", so he brought in plants: "They gave it a nice, lush effect and did a hell of a job for what they cost." | Chronicle 2011 |
| F15 | **Hobday's own account:** "It was bright and cheery. People started swarming in there." A friend called it a fern bar, and the name stuck. | Chronicle 2011 |
| F16 | **Hobday's own account:** his mother lent him the money on condition he name the bar after an old boyfriend of hers, Henry Africa, who had been in the Foreign Legion. | Chronicle 2011 (his telling; label it so) |
| F17 | The Chronicle: he was "so enamored of the Henry Africa persona" that he changed his name legally. | Chronicle 2011 |
| F18 | Hobday's Lemon Drop: Oxford calls it his "most enduring original cocktail". | Oxford pdf 995 |
| F19 | The original Lemon Drop was "a more elegant affair", served up in a sugar-rimmed cocktail glass; later it devolved to a chilled vodka shot with a sugared lemon wedge. | Oxford pdf 1175 |
| F20 | Henry Africa's used premium spirits and fresh juices to order. | Oxford pdf 1175 |
| F21 | It later became a staple of Harry Denton's Starlight Room, owned by a former Henry Africa's bartender (Harry Denton). | Oxford pdf 1175 |
| F22 | Hobday's brother, Jack Slick, ran the Balboa Café with Doyle Moon; Hobday owned the Dartmouth Social Club nearby. | Proper p. 52; Chronicle 2011 (Dartmouth) |

## Conflicts (keep the contested detail out)
| ID | Conflict |
|---|---|
| C1 | **First fern bar.** Chronicle 2011: "the world's first and definitive fern bar". Oxford pdf 995: the Trident (Sausalito) was three years earlier; pdf 1173: "arguably the first". TGI Friday's (1965) also claims the singles-bar concept (pdf 2014). Never "first". |
| C2 | **The Lemon Drop's recipe.** Oxford pdf 1175 (Cate's modern recipe): vodka or citrus vodka, simple syrup, lemon; no liqueur. *Proper* p. 52: at Henry Africa's, "vodka, triple sec, lemon juice, and simple syrup". The original's exact build is unknown; never "his recipe". |
| C3 | **Fresh lemon in the original.** Oxford pdf 1175 says the house's drops "would have undoubtedly included" fresh lemon: Cate's inference from the house habit (F20), not a record of the drink. Tell the house habit, not the drink's juice, as fact. |
| C4 | **When he sold.** Chronicle captions: 1983 ("the year he quit"); obituary text: sold "10 years later"; 1986 item: ran it "until 1985". Keep the sale year out; the 1986 closing is solid. |

## Inferences and legends (labelled)
- L1: "Out of love for the old saloon" is **nowhere on the record** for Hobday. His stated reason for the look is cost (F14). The love on record is for *his own bar* and its persona (F12, F17). Any "he kept it because he loved it" is the bartender's signposted reading, and it must not contradict F14.
- L2: Hobday's motive regarding women is not on any page I read. A web summary said he was "catering to women"; its source is untrusted and unread. Women on equal terms is the fern bars' (F6), not Hobday's stated aim.
- L3: The mother's-boyfriend story (F16) is Hobday's own telling, 40 years on. Tell it as his telling.

## Guards
- Out: "meet markets", "pickup joints", sexual revolution, the "bordello" line, AIDS (pdf 771), drugs (F8's "recreational drugs" clause), the Mickey Finn robbery in the obituary, Hobday's illness and dialysis, taxidermy, TGI Friday's, the Korean War.
- The "mask" of two shots of vodka (pdf 995) is the sourced purpose of the candy taste: never replaced by a kinder motive.
- Citrus vodka is *Still Yours*'s; no Absolut.
- "Remodel": F14 says he couldn't afford to remodel. That isn't "he kept the old furniture" (F5: the look was brought in: plants, antiques, leaded glass lamps, per the Chronicle). Say what he didn't do, and what he added.

## The Bell-Ringer (added 2026-10-01, round 3, for Tomás's rinse)
Primary: James C. Maloney, *The 20th Century Guide for Mixing Fancy Drinks* (Chicago, copyright 1900; Library of Congress stamp "APR 9 1900"), archive.org `20thcenturyguide00malo`, djvu text read in full for every Bell-Ringer. Period press: *National Police Gazette*, 16 Aug 1902 and 29 Aug 1903 (archive.org `sim_national-police-gazette_*`, djvu text). Secondary: Regan, *Joy of Mixology* pdf 41.

| ID | Fact | Source |
|---|---|---|
| B1 | Maloney's book prints a family of Bell-Ringers: Brandy, Cocktail (whisky, Old Crow), Sour, Punch, Gin, Manhattan, Martinez, and "The Col. Taylor 'Manhattan Bell-Ringer'". | Maloney 1900, contents and recipes |
| B2 | In the rinsed ones, the rinse goes into the glass while the drink is being stirred, "evenly coated all over inside", then the drink is strained in. The text doesn't say to tip the rinse out, and doesn't say to keep it. | Maloney 1900 (Brandy, Cocktail, Manhattan, Col. Taylor) |
| B3 | Rinse amount: 1 teaspoon (Brandy, Cocktail, Sour); 1/2 teaspoon (Gin, Manhattan, Col. Taylor). | Maloney 1900 |
| B4 | **The rinse is "Eagle Creme D'Apricot"**, which Maloney glosses as "abricotine" ("8 ounces abricotine (Eagle Creme D'Apricot)"). | Maloney 1900 |
| B5 | **It was red:** the Gin Bell-Ringer's rinse is meant to "give the glass a red appearance"; elsewhere he adds it "just enough to make a distinct red". | Maloney 1900 |
| B6 | Gin, Manhattan and Martinez Bell-Ringers: after straining, rub fresh-cut lemon around the edge of the glass. | Maloney 1900 |
| B7 | The *Police Gazette* listed "James C. Maloney, Chicago, Bell Ringer Cocktail" among new recipes received (1902) and printed it "By Jas. C. Maloney, 2364 East Madison St., Chicago" (1903), with "Abricotine" as the rinse. | *Police Gazette* 1902, 1903 |
| B8 | Today "abricotine" names a clear, colourless Valais apricot brandy (Oxford pdf 47); apricot "brandy" can also mean a sweet, coloured liqueur, "more reasonably" called apricot liqueur, and the eau-de-vie can usually replace the liqueur "for a less sweet and more delicate cocktail" (pdf 133). | Oxford pdf 47, 133 |

Conflicts and inferences:
- C5: **Liqueur or eau-de-vie?** On the page, Maloney's is a *crème* and red (B4, B5): the coloured liqueur, not today's clear eau-de-vie. Its sugar is unrecorded (inference from "crème": sweet). The word "abricotine" changed meaning between 1900 and Oxford (B8). An eau-de-vie rinse is our swap, owned as ours (Oxford pdf 133 supports the swap as practice).
- C6: **"Gay Nineties"** is Regan's frame. The dated record is 1900-1903. Say "a Chicago bar guide of 1900", never "1890s". "Long-gone category" is Regan's judgment; Maloney's book shows one man's family of drinks, not a city's custom. Whether Maloney invented it is unrecorded (the Gazette credits the recipe "By" him).
- C7: Regan calls Maloney a bartender; the 1900 book gives only his Madison Street address. "A Chicago bartender" may be told with Regan as the source.
- Guard: B6's lemon on the rim is Maloney's, for those drinks only; never "the Lemon Drop's rim came from him".
