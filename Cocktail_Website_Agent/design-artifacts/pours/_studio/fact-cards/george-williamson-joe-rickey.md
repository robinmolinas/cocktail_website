## Facts
| ID | Fact (our words) | Source | Tier | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| F1 | The Rickey: any spirit (traditionally bourbon, rye or gin), half a lime squeezed and dropped in, soda; no sugar in the classic. | S1 pdf 1661 | primary | Recipe detail: Heugel card R1–R7. |
| F2 | It was popularized at Shoomaker's bar in Washington, DC, in the late 1880s by Col. Joseph Kerr "Joe" Rickey (1842–1903), a lobbyist from Missouri, **and** bartender George Williamson (1849–1915). | S1 pdf 1661 | primary | Oxford's opening sentence names the two together. |
| F3 | Shoomaker's ("Shoo's") was run by August Noack from the 1880s until it closed in 1917, "much of that time" together with head bartender George Williamson. | S2 pdf 1789 | primary | No page gives Williamson's own start or end years at the bar. Say "for years" / "much of the bar's life", never "decades" or "thirty years". |
| F4 | Oxford also calls Williamson the bar's principal barkeep and its "president" (Oxford's quotation marks, no gloss), with a long association with the Rickey. | S1 pdf 1663 | primary | "president" unexplained: never glossed (see C4). |
| F5 | Wondrich: somewhere between 1883 and 1889, Rickey had Williamson, the saloon's head barkeeper, start making them for him; Wondrich calls him "beloved". | S3 pdf 117 | primary | "Beloved" is Wondrich's word: attribute or leave out. |
| F6 | Wondrich: the colonel showed bartenders in many places how to make his drink; Shoo's was the first place anyone noticed. | S3 pdf 117 | primary | Paraphrase: Wondrich's own run "instructed bartenders everywhere how to make it" is copy. |
| F7 | Rickey told the NY *Herald* (1893) he "was not the author" of the drink but "merely its introducer to the East", then gave an origin account, the first of at least three published in his lifetime. | S1 pdf 1662 | primary | His 1893 account credits his St. Louis friend Enno Sander (pdf 1663), not Williamson. |
| F8 | Rickey's 1893 account (as Oxford relays it): Sander's drink was whiskey, soda and ice; Rickey added lemon; at Shoo's in the late 1880s it began catching on with lime instead, "which Rickey disliked"; Rickey put the change down to one of the bartenders, "more than likely" Williamson (Oxford's inference). | S1 pdf 1663 | primary | **Conflict C1.** The dislike sits in Oxford's narration of his account; no quotation of Rickey's own words for it. |
| F9 | Brown (1930), via Oxford: Williamson was the Rickey's actual inventor; an unknown stranger told him how Caribbean drinks used half a lime, gave him limes, and asked for rye instead of rum; next morning Williamson made one for Rickey, who approved ("was said to"). | S1 pdf 1663 | primary (relay of a 1930 book) | Brown's book unread. "Was said to" carries into any telling. |
| F10 | Oxford: whoever invented it, and whether Rickey drank it or not, his friends drank it and tied it to him. | S1 pdf 1663 | primary | |
| F11 | The name is attributed to two friends, Rep. William Henry Hatch and Fred Mussey, said to have been there when it was made and later to have come in asking for a "Joe Rickey". Drink and name were going round by the end of 1889. | S1 pdf 1663 | primary | Wondrich: in the *Washington Post* by 1889 (S3 pdf 117). |
| F12 | The handwritten recipe Rickey gave a Syracuse paper in 1895: large glass, ice, whiskey or gin, lime juice, carbonated water (and a last line, fenced). | S1 pdf 1662; S3 pdf 118 | primary | Both books. The lime is in the colonel's own written recipe, whatever the origin. |
| F13 | Rickey's whiskey: "reportedly" Belle of Nelson bourbon (Oxford); Wondrich says there is agreement he preferred it, while "some say rye, some say bourbon" for the drink. | S1 pdf 1663; S3 pdf 118 | primary | Strength differs: tell it as "reportedly". |

## Conflicts
| ID | Conflict |
|---|---|
| C1 | **Lemon → lime, and "disliked".** Three tellings: Rickey's (via Oxford, he used lemon; lime came from the bar; he disliked it), Brown's (a stranger's limes; Rickey approved), Wondrich's (lime from the start; no lemon). Never tell "Rickey disliked the lime" as fact. Safe: "in Rickey's own telling, the switch to lime came from one of the bartenders; in Brown's, a stranger handed Williamson the limes", and F12. (Struck 2026-10-04 r4: my r2 shorthand "in two of the tellings the lime came from behind the bar" misread Brown, where the limes are the stranger's.) Same ruling as the Heugel card's fence (2026-10-01). |
| C2 | **Who invented it**: Sander (Rickey 1893), Williamson (Brown 1930), Rickey (Wondrich pdf 117 "he invented"; Crockett 1935; the Syracuse 1895 headline "He Invented Rickeys"), or a stranger with limes. Only "some said". |
| C3 | **Ownership at the moment**: Oxford RICKEY says he had owned it "for a couple of years" after 1883; SHOOMAKER'S says bought 1883, sold three years later; Wondrich says he owned it "at the time" (1883–1889 window). Never "his own bar" for the lime/first-drink scene. |
| C4 | **Williamson's title**: "head bartender" (S2), "principal barkeep" and "president" in quotes (S1), "head barkeeper" (S3), "secretary" of the company (S5 fragment). Use "head bartender" only. |
| C5 | **Address**: Oxford 1331 E Street on Rum Row (S2); Wondrich "on Pennsylvania Avenue" (S3). No street in guest text. |
| C6 | **Base**: rye or bourbon (S3), Belle of Nelson "reportedly" (S1), the bar's own rye (S2), rye in Brown's tale (S1). Say "whiskey"; both bases are on the page. |
