## Facts

| S1 | A mix of gin, liqueurs, fruit juices and soda water; a mainstay of mid-twentieth-century drinking; also called the Straits Sling (Singapore was part of the Straits Settlements, 1867–1946). Not a true sling in the old American sense, and not exclusive to Singapore even before it became famous. | Oxford pdf 1803, 1936 | primary | "One of the most storied and romantic names" is Oxford's phrase. |
| S2 | By the late nineteenth century British drinkers, especially in the Asian colonies, had added liqueurs and citrus to the gin sling. Near the century's end, drinkers in Singapore began adding a splash of cherry brandy, first documented in a 1903 Singapore newspaper ("pink slings for pale people"). | Oxford pdf 1803 | primary | |
| S3 | **The first rough recipe (1913):** at the Singapore Cricket Club, whose bar thought slings vulgar, a sling was got by ordering its parts in one order: "one Cherry Brandy, one Domb [i.e., Bénédictine], one Gin, one Lime Juice" (Oxford's brackets), ice and soda water, a few dashes of bitters, "and then enjoyed a really decent Sling". **Who ordered: Oxford says "a member"; *Imbibe!* pdf 122 (same author) says the younger members. No number in guest text.** Not "one by one" or "one at a time" (my r4 shorthand, struck in audit v1 H7). | Oxford pdf 1803 (source: *Singapore Weekly Sun*, 20 Sep 1913, p. 7, in the bibliography pdf 1805) | primary (relaying a 1913 anecdote) | **A bar that wouldn't make it, and a guest who built it from parts.** It's an anecdote: tell it as "a 1913 story". "Domb" = Bénédictine is Oxford's own bracket. |
| S4 | Plenty of places in Singapore did make it: the John Little department store's bar had the top reputation, then the Raffles Long Bar, then Robinson's. Similar slings were made across British Asia, with a good deal of latitude: Bénédictine often replaced or supplemented by curaçao, cherry brandy (Heering or Bols) by red wine or sloe gin. | Oxford pdf 1803–1804 | primary | **The latitude is on the page:** a build with curaçao in place of Bénédictine is within the period's own practice (see V1). |
| S5 | By 1910 a bar in Salzburg advertised a "Singapore Gin Sling"; by the mid-1920s the drink was known around the world. It became tied to the Raffles as the city's leading hotel, and the bar eventually claimed it. | Oxford pdf 1804; RAFFLES LONG BAR pdf 1596 | primary | Oxford: the Raffles claim "doesn't quite square with the historical record". |
| S6 | From 1974 the Raffles manager, Roberto Pregarz, remade it: much less of the pricey Bénédictine and Cherry Heering, pineapple and orange juice added with the lime, a splash of Cointreau. It was sold as the "original recipe", said to survive only in the memory of the veteran barman Ngiam Tong Boon, who had "supposedly" invented it in 1915. The result was "overtly 'tropical'", where the original had been "boozy, rather austere". | Oxford pdf 1804 | primary | The Pregarz quote ("improved" it, same ingredients) is from a 1986 interview; Oxford thinks it was "a bit disingenuous". Tell it gently: never call him a liar. |
| S7 | Efforts to free the original from the "sweet, rather sticky" modern Raffles version "have not generally been successful". | Oxford pdf 1804 | primary | Oxford's judgement. |
| S8 | **Oxford's recipe (1910s version):** in an ice-filled highball glass, 45 ml gin, 15 ml lime juice, 15 ml Cherry Heering, 15 ml Bénédictine; top with chilled soda water, stir, dash Angostura on top. | Oxford pdf 1804 | primary | This is Wondrich's build of the 1910s drink, not a printed period recipe. Say "the way Oxford gives the 1910s Sling", never "the 1915 recipe". |
| S9 | Cherry Heering: founded 1818 by Peter Heering in Copenhagen, still made in Denmark, today on a neutral-spirit base, owned by De Kuyper. Most recipes that call for cherry brandy, the Singapore Sling included, mean this sweetened, cherry-flavoured kind, not a clear distilled kirsch. | Oxford pdf 446 | primary | The maker's site (2026) calls it "the world's first cherry liqueur", which is brand copy: never repeat it. |
| S10 | Bénédictine: made from twenty-seven plants and spices, **including nutmeg**, among angelica, hyssop, saffron, cinnamon and clove; 40% ABV. The 1510 monk origin is a company claim with no independent trace (Oxford). | Oxford pdf 256 | primary | **Veto, safe side:** Robin's rule is that nutmeg counts as nuts, and the table's `benedictine` row has `contains: []`. That's Tomás's call to fix; I'd class it `nuts`. The monk story is a legend: out, or labelled. |
| S11 | *Imbibe!*: the club's directors thought gin slings a vulgar novelty, served only to visiting clubs; the younger members disagreed and, in doing so, "recorded the drink's original formula for us". | *Imbibe!* pdf 122 | primary (library) | "Original formula" is Wondrich's word. Ours: "the earliest recipe we have". The refusal is out of guest text (Tomás's guard, Wren r5). |
| S12 | Wondrich's notes: a Singapore measure of spirits then was a fifth of a gill, "or 1 ounce", so 1 oz of each spirit, 1 oz of fresh lime and 1–2 oz of sparkling water; gin London dry or Old Tom; bitters Angostura; build in a Collins glass over ice, stir briefly, a straw; "If you must garnish", a spiral of lime peel. | *Imbibe!* pdf 122 | primary (library) | The spec's rocks glass, single cube, 90 ml soda and no garnish are ours. |
| S13 | The cherry brandy was red: the 1903 "pink slings", the *Straits Times*'s 1913 remark on the drink's colour, and the only cherry brandies in Singapore's liquor adverts of the day being red Bols or Heering. Some preferred claret or sloe gin to colour it ("not without merit"); his tweak: lime down a quarter, Bénédictine down half, gin up half. | *Imbibe!* pdf 122 | primary (library) | The adverts are evidence, not recipes (audit v1 T2). *Joy* pdf 389 prints the tweak (1½ / 1 / ½ / ¾) and files the Sling under "sparkling sours" (pdf 388). |

## What a glass may claim
- **"The older Sling"** / "the Sling as it was made before a hotel sweetened it in the 1970s" / "the way Oxford gives the 1910s version" (S6–S8). The bartender may say "properly" as his own opinion, not as a fact.
- A build with **curaçao in place of Bénédictine** is still the old drink's own latitude (S4). Say so in the dossier; in guest text, "one of the ways it was made back then".
- The 1913 order, its parts named "one… one…" (S3), told as a bar order from 1913, with no number of people.

## What can never be claimed
- **"The original"**, "the real one" or "the authentic recipe" (Oxford: the original is known only in rough form, with wide latitude, S3–S4, S7).
- **Raffles invented it**, **Ngiam Tong Boon in 1915**, or "the Raffles recipe" as the true drink (S5–S6).
- **What the guest ordered** (the night isn't placed: Dionysos card P1), or which Sling the two senior bartenders didn't know (*Proper* p. 72 gives neither). Never "the old one nobody knew": the page says only that nobody knew how to make "that drink".
- **That he learned the Sling from the book he bought.** The page says he bought his first cocktail book and kept going back. It doesn't say he found the Sling in it or made one afterwards. "I like to think" at most.
- Bénédictine's 1510 monk as fact (S10). "World's first cherry liqueur" (brand copy, S9).

## Guards (other pours)
- **Fougner is the Idealist's** (*The Long Answer*): Oxford's Sling bibliography cites five of his columns, and his card (F10) lists the Sling among the drinks his readers lit up. Never name him or readers' letters here.
- "Pink slings for pale people" reads medical: dossier only.
- **Shape watch for Tomás:** Oxford serves it in a highball glass over ice with soda, which is gin + tall glass + ice + a fizzy top, the same as *Worth the Trip*'s triple (highball · London dry gin · tall glass full of ice). The plan reserved this pairing's slot as Daiquiri · gin · rocks glass, one large cube.
