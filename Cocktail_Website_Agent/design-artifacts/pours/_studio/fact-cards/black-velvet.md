# Fact card: the Black Velvet

Our words throughout. Facts are free; the sources' sentences are not.

## Facts

| # | Claim (our words) | Source | Tier | Notes |

| F1 | Champagne and stout (or porter), nothing else. It first turns up in the 1830s as a German students' drink called *Menschenfreund*, "Philanthropist". | Oxford, BLACK VELVET (pdf 281; Wondrich) | primary | The earliest record we have. Why that name: **not on record**. |
| F2 | It had a name for fuelling bad behaviour, and it was a favourite of the young Bismarck; British and American biographies of him in the 1870s pointed to it as a sign of his wild youth. | Oxford, BLACK VELVET (pdf 281) | primary | **Dossier only.** Wrong shape for this guest (recklessness, a statesman). |
| F3 | Outside Germany it barely existed until then. Some called it strange and almost unbelievable; others took it up. | Oxford, BLACK VELVET (pdf 281) | primary | Oxford quotes "strange and almost incredible beverage". Quote ≤ 6 words if at all. |
| F4 | In Britain it went by several names: Bismarck, Imperial Shandy (Oxford: exact, as mixology), and Velvet, for its texture. | F5 | It stayed a minority taste, almost a curiosity, until the 1920s. ## Legends / cautions
- **The London mourning story** (a club bartender creating it for the death of Prince Albert, 1861) is the version usually told. It's **not in any of our books**, and Oxford's record starts in 1830s Germany, so it can't be the origin. If told at all: labelled legend, dossier only. Never "invented in London".
- **"Philanthropist" as a joke**: the name is on record; any reading of why (irony, kindness) is ours and must be signposted.
- **Bismarck / Rhodes**: true, and wrong for the guest. Dossier only.
