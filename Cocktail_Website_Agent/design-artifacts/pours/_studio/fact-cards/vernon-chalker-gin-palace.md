| F11 | Jacob Briars, then a bartender at Matterhorn in Wellington, New Zealand, recalled it as the first time he'd seen anyone use "more than a dropper of Noilly Prat", and the first time he'd seen orange bitters used. | *Proper* p. 142 | reliable secondary | His experience only: never "nobody in Australia used vermouth". | F12 | Noilly Prat's Original Dry was among the first dry vermouths exported and is "considered the archetype for dry vermouth". | F13 | Martini proportions over time: the original formula was equal parts gin and vermouth; two-to-one (French or Italian) appears in the late 1890s and is the standard by the 1910s; three-to-one in WWII; by 1954 a Martini meant at least five-to-one, an Extra Dry meant no vermouth at all, and 1952 had a fad of jokes about adding the least vermouth possible. | Oxford MARTINI, pdf 1247 | primary | US drinking. Never "Australia in the 1990s" from this page. |
| F14 | The early Martinis with more vermouth than gin used **sweet** vermouth (and Old Tom): Jerry Thomas's 1887 Martinez, 1 oz Old Tom to 2 oz vermouth, which Wondrich calls a "reverse-proportion Martini". | *Imbibe!* pdf 198-199; Oxford BASE OF A MIXED DRINK pdf 229 | primary | So a dry-vermouth drink with more vermouth than gin is **not** "the old Martini". Gin Palace's "more vermouth than gin" Martini (F8) names no vermouth. |
| F15 | Noilly Prat makes Original Dry (the archetype, claimed to follow an 1813 family recipe) and Extra Dry (made for the American market in 1955, lighter). *Codex* recommends Extra Dry, as one of the few dry vermouths long available in the US. | Oxford NOILLY PRAT pdf 1395; *Codex* p. 73 | primary / reliable secondary | Which Noilly Briars saw in 1997 is **not on record**. Don't tie a version to his memory. |
| F16 | Tokaji: a rich, sweet wine from the Tokaj region, in Hungary (the region reaches into Slovakia); Furmint is its main grape.
