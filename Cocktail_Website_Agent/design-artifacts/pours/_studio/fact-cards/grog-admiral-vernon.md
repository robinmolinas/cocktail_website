# Fact card: grog and Admiral Vernon's order (1740)

Written by Hester, 2026-09-27, for caregiver-ruler (The Nanny). Verified once; reuse, don't re-tell differently.

| # | Fact | Source | Tier | Notes / fences |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| F1 | In 1740 Admiral Edward Vernon introduced grog to the British Navy: the daily rum ration, until then drunk neat, was to be issued mixed with water. | Oxford, GROG (pdf 951); timeline (pdf 2248); *Joy* pdf 20 | primary | Date of the order 21 August 1740 is secondary (wordorigins.org; diffordsguide). Year alone is primary. |
| F2 | His stated concern: the "pernicious custom" of seamen drinking their allowance in drams, "often at once". | Oxford, GROG (pdf 951) | primary | Quote via Oxford. Under 15 words. |
| F3 | The reason Oxford gives: sailors had to climb high into the rigging, and drunken unsteadiness was a liability. | Oxford, GROG (pdf 951) | primary | Oxford's reading of the danger, not a line in the order. |
| F4 | Four parts water to one of rum (half a pint of rum to a quart of water), split and served twice a day rather than all at once. | Oxford, GROG (pdf 951); SPIRITS IN THE MILITARY (pdf 1868) | primary | Hours (10–12 and 4–6) secondary: wordorigins.org. Ratio later varied; two to one from 1937 until 1970. |
| F5 | Vernon allowed thrifty men to buy lime and sugar (from savings on their provisions) to make it more palatable, effectively turning it into punch. | Oxford, SPIRITS IN THE MILITARY (pdf 1868–1869) | primary | Palatability was the stated reason. Vitamin C / scurvy was a benefit noted later, not Vernon's motive. Transcriptions differ ("husbandmen"/"husbands"): don't quote that word. |
| F6 | The order said the mixing was done in a scuttled butt (an opened cask kept for the purpose) and dispensed openly, so the men could be sure they weren't defrauded of their full allowance. | Oxford, SPIRITS IN THE MILITARY (pdf 1868–1869) | primary | "On deck" and "in presence of the lieutenant of the watch": secondary (wordorigins.org; diffordsguide). |
| F7 | The name: Vernon reportedly wore a coat of grogram (a gum-stiffened waterproof fabric), was nicknamed Old Grogram/Old Grog, and the drink took the name. | Oxford, GROG (pdf 951), "reportedly"; SPIRITS IN THE MILITARY (pdf 1868) "reportedly" | primary, but reported | **Legend-grade**: tell with "the story goes". |
| F8 | "Grog" is first found in print in February 1749 (a newspaper, "short Allowance of Grog"). | wordorigins.org (Dave Wilton), fetched 2026-09-27 | secondary | Only if needed. |
| F9 | The daily tot, always mixed into grog, stayed a formal ritual announced by the bosun's call "Up spirits!"; the ration was cut in 1824 and 1851. | Oxford, RUM, NAVY (pdf 1701) | primary | |
| F10 | The last issue was 31 July 1970, "Black Tot Day", marked with mock burials at sea and black armbands. | Oxford, RUM, NAVY (pdf 1701–1702); SPIRITS IN THE MILITARY (pdf 1870) | primary | Reason given: ships grown technologically complex. |
| F11 | Navy rum was issued at 54.5% ABV after an 1866 regulation, strong and flavoured enough to stand up to the water. | Oxford, RUM, NAVY (pdf 1701) | primary | For Tomás if he wants "navy-strength". |

## Conflicts
- C1: Oxford GROG says "four parts water"; SPIRITS IN THE MILITARY says half a pint of rum to a quart of water. Same ratio (a quart is four half-pints). No conflict.
- C2: The lime-and-sugar clause: "good husbandmen" (Oxford) vs "good husbands" (common transcriptions). Paraphrase only.

## Legends
- L1: That sailors hated the watered rum and named it in mockery. No source in the library; don't say it.
