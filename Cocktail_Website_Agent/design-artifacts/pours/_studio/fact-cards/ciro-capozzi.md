# Ciro Capozzi and Ciro's, Monte Carlo

Verified 2026-10-01 by Hester for ruler-lover (The Magnetic Leader). Sources: Oxford Companion, CIRO'S (pdf 460–462, David Wondrich; primary), COCKTAIL (pdf 491–492), MANHATTAN (pdf 1224), MIXOLOGY, THE HISTORY OF (pdf 1331), PRINCE OF WALES (pdf 1562). Web: Gallica (BnF) tried for the 1890 *Herald* piece, 403 and a bad query; nothing read. Both web visits for the pour are spent.

- F1. Ciro Capozzi (1855–1938), a sea captain's son from Vico Equense on the Bay of Naples (pdf 460).
- F2. "In 1885 or thereabouts" the management of the Café de Paris, facing the Casino in Monte Carlo, opened a small American Bar with Capozzi as head bartender (pdf 460). The year is approximate: never "in 1885".
- F3. Unlike many in Europe's "American" bars, he had actually worked in America as a bartender, "and always prided himself on that experience" (pdf 460). Immigration records show a "C. Capozzi" arriving in New York in May 1883, from Napoli. Other sources put him in San Francisco, or at Delmonico's (pdf 460–461).
- F4. He served drinks "far simpler" than the fantasies at most European bars: while others offered "Heaps of Comfort" and "Flip Flaps", he was introducing Europe to the Manhattan and the Martini (pdf 460).
- F5. 1888: his own bar in the Galerie Charles III, just off the Casino square. 1889: the space next door, for breakfasts and lunches, ingredients imported from London's top purveyors (pdf 461).
- F6. His clientele was "very strongly English" and included the prince of Wales, "who would show him cocktails he had learned". With the prince and the press baron James Gordon Bennett as regulars, he drew "the cream of Monte Carlo's seasonal society" (pdf 461). Habitual ("would show"): no single scene, no date, no named drink. The prince then was Albert Edward (1841–1910), who held the title "for sixty years" (pdf 1562); so the visits fall before 1901 (our arithmetic).
- F7. Counterweight to F6: "With the exception of the Prince of Wales", the people with the most influence on British manners treated American drinks as curiosities (pdf 491). The prince already liked cocktails; Ciro didn't convert him. Never "won him over".
- F8. 1897 expansion; "a true moneymaker". His wife Clotilde was the cashier and his brother Salvatore the bar manager; "things ran smoothly" until 1911 (pdf 461). He didn't run it alone.
- F9. 1911: sold to a syndicate of London investors and retired to his villa nearby, "where he lived quietly for another twenty-seven years" (pdf 461).
- F10. 1912: the syndicate opened Ciro's in the Rue Daunou, Paris, "with the same formula"; instantly a top attraction (pdf 461). The elite sitting only in the bar room is **this** branch, after he'd sold: never his.
- F11. The Monte Carlo, Paris and London Ciro's "all closed in the immediate postwar years"; only Deauville survives (pdf 462). From the paragraph before (1939–40), the war is the Second (our reading). So the Monte Carlo house ran some three decades after he left it.
- F12. Oxford's verdict: "With his charm, skill, and hospitality, Ciro Capozzi had the leverage to move the culture when the time was right to move it." The hedge goes with it: "It might be an exaggeration to say that he made the cocktail fashionable in Europe, but it would not be much of one" (pdf 462).
- F13. pdf 1331: in the 1890s his "little bar" drew "perhaps the most exclusive clientele of any in the world", the nobility of Europe sipping "unpretentious, New York–style cocktails". "When Ciro's opened in Paris, London, and then Berlin, the American cocktail's work was done": those openings were the syndicate's (F10).
- F14. pdf 491: the tipping point for British society "seems to have come" at his little bar; he "made a specialty of the simple Cocktail", and those Cocktails "led to the emergence of Manhattans and Martinis" in Britain.
- F15. Recipe, "Ciro Capozzi's Cocktail, 1898" (pdf 491–492, Wondrich's modern measures): mixing glass of cracked ice, 2 dashes aromatic bitters, ½ barspoon (4 ml) orange curaçao, scant ½ barspoon (3 ml) rich simple syrup, 60 ml genever, cognac or straight American whiskey (rye or bourbon); stir, strain into a chilled cocktail glass, twist lemon peel over the top.
- F16. The Manhattan had reached "Vienna and London and Monte Carlo" by the end of the 1880s (pdf 1224).
- F17. The prince's own cocktail, two records. (a) Oxford PRINCE OF WALES (pdf 1562, Dinah Sanders): a July 1887 item in the London *Evening Telegraph* (21 July 1887, p. 2) describes "the drink the prince concocted" for a room named for him at the London American Exhibition: rye whisky, powdered sugar, ice, a small piece of pineapple, Angostura, a little lemon peel, a few drops of maraschino, a dash of champagne. (b) *Imbibe!* pdf 231 quotes an anonymous 1901 book on his private life: he "is also credited with having composed" it, and it was "often asked for at the clubs which he frequents". Oxford's verdict on it: "conspicuous consumption", "a demonstration of fiscal power". Pineapple was then "extraordinarily costly" (pdf 1562).
- F18. Nothing on any page says this drink was among the cocktails the prince showed Ciro (F6 says "cocktails he had learned", not ones he made). Any link is "I like to think".
- F19. Wondrich files the prince's drink as "a pretty fair variation on the Improved Whiskey Cocktail" (*Imbibe!* pdf 230): *Beside the First*'s drink. The champagne, he guesses, came from a magnum the prince "probably sabered" (pdf 230): his guess, not a record. The same page's mistresses stay out.
- F20. Oxford lists other "Prince of Wales" cocktails, among them one "from Monte Carlo with gin, pineapple juice, and egg white" (pdf 1562), undated and unlinked to Ciro. Not to be connected to him.
- C1. His Jerry Thomas boast (Paris *Herald*, 1898) drew letters disputing it; "not impossible" (pdf 460). His boast, or nothing.
- C2. Oxford's "before him … after him" (pdf 462) is undated and sits in a sentence about kings: it measures his influence, not his retirement. It can't carry "after he stepped away".
- C3. No face-to-face scene is on any page. The 1890 *Herald* piece ("Ciro at His Post on the Riviera", 30 Nov 1890) is unread.
- Guards: "one-percenters", "royalty", "sitting kings", the wildcat Ciro's, Ciro's London and its head bartender (*For Kicks*), Leo Engel (another card) stay out.
