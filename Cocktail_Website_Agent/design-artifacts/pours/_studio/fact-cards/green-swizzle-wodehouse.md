| W1 | Later at the same lunch Bertie tells a deaf-man joke about Wembley that he'd heard ("A fellow told me one"). Sir Roderick Glossop answers with a waggled eyebrow; Bertie: "back to the basket for Bertram", and Sir Roderick could make "a fellow feel like a waste-product". | src ll. 748–758 |
| W2 | Sir Roderick is taking Biffy to the British Empire Exhibition at Wembley because his daughter says it will "broaden your mind". Biffy asks Bertie to come; Bertie goes out of friendship ("A pal is a pal"). | src ll. 760–775 |
| W3 | Sir Roderick "took charge of the expedition" and headed for the Palace of Industry, the two young men trailing behind. | src ll. 909–911 |

| W5 | Sir Roderick had "whizzed us past" the Planters' Bar in the West Indian section; it touched no chord in him. Bertie had seen a man behind the counter mixing from bottles and **stirring them with a stick in long glasses that seemed to have ice in them**, and wanted "to see more of this man". | src ll. 922–931 |
| W6 | Biffy plucks his sleeve, "the air of one who has had about sufficient". They look at each other ("'?' '!'"); three minutes later they are at the bar. | src ll. 931–943 |
| W7 | The barman is "as kindly a bloke as I ever wish to meet" and seems to guess what they want; a planter, Bertie jokes, doesn't count it a drink without "at least seven ingredients" (**Bertie's joke, not a recipe**). The man tells them the drinks are called Green Swizzles. | src ll. 946–954 |
| W8 | **The vow:** if Bertie ever marries and has a son, "Green Swizzle Wooster" goes down "on the register", in memory of the day his father's life was saved at Wembley. (**"Life was saved" is Bertie's joke about the afternoon; Oxford's "lifesaving" paraphrases it.**) | src ll. 954–956 |

## The real drink
| ID | Fact | Source |
|---|---|---|
| F1 | The Green Swizzle is a traditional Caribbean drink "made famous" by Wodehouse's story. | Oxford pdf 945 |
| F2 | It "was long believed to be fictional" (passive: **no believer is named**); newspaper databases showed it was real and for a time quite popular, **"at least in its native region"**, and turned up "a handful of recipes". | Oxford pdf 945 |
| F3 | In 1924 the West Indies Pavilion at Wembley featured two drinks: Planter's Punch and the Green Swizzle. The pavilion where the fiction is set **really poured it that year.** | Oxford pdf 756 |
| F4 | Essentials across the recipes: gin or rum, falernum (in Trinidad, Angostura's Carypton), wormwood bitters (wormwood steeped in strong rum), and ice. Other ingredients in the pool: lime, sugar, Angostura, soda. | Oxford pdf 945; *Imbibe!* pdf 124 |
| F5 | 1908 *New York Herald* recipe, close to the Bridgetown Club's: rum, falernum, a dash of wormwood bitters, shaved (not cracked) ice, swizzle between the palms until the ice melts and the drink is almost frappé, strain into a cocktail glass. | *Imbibe!* pdf 124 |
| F6 | Already in 1908 a *Herald* correspondent in the West Indies found every swizzler had a different "real" swizzle (rum, Tom gin, Angostura and whiskey). Wondrich: "How many swizzlers, so many Swizzles." | *Imbibe!* pdf 124 |
| F7 | Wondrich: in its soul a long, slow sipper, best served in the glass it was swizzled in, tall and frosty; strained into a cocktail glass it's also "perfectly delicious". | *Imbibe!* pdf 125 |
| F8 | Swizzling: a stick rolled fast between the palms, raised and lowered, until the glass frosts. The traditional stick is a stripped branch of the swizzlestick tree (*Quararibea turbinata*), its twigs trimmed into spokes. | Oxford pdf 1963, 1965 |
| F9 | Oxford: "one well worth reviving"; Constante's later crème de menthe version at the Floridita is "one of the few drinks" he "did not improve". | Oxford pdf 945 |
| F10 | Falernum, Barbados: a prebottled punch base of limes, sugar, spices "(chiefly bitter almond)" and a little rum (Wondrich); Oxford: spices such as cloves, ginger and nutmeg; "Almonds are sometimes added". **Allergen flag for Tomás (nuts: nutmeg and almond).** | *Imbibe!* pdf 124; Oxford pdf 761 |

## Guards
- G1 Fiction, owned in one clause ("in a story", "Wodehouse has him…"). Bertie never "did" anything as a fact.
- G2 "Long believed to be fictional" has no subject: never "everyone", "the experts", "historians". (My own plan wrote "the experts didn't know either": **struck, it was mine.**)
- G3 "Life was saved" is Bertie's joke; health words stay out unless Wren rules the joke itself in, as his, signposted.
- G4 W10 (window, police, cells) stays out: chaos after drinking is not this guest's gift.
- G5 The barman is unnamed in the story. E. C. Pinnock (Oxford pdf 756) mixed the pavilion's Planter's Punches: never make him Bertie's barman. Planter's Punch stays out (Outlaw's Myrtle Bank row).
- G6 Bertie as a fool: Wren's guard. The mirror sits on boredom, the hush, the small thing, the seriousness; never his wits.
