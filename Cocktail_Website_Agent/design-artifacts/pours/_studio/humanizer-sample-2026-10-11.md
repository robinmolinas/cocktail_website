## Till Spring · The Healer

This person believes others can recover before they believe it themselves. They make help easy to accept, and keep wondering whether they could have done more for the people who did not get better.

The strongest material is already specific: "give it till spring", the second pair of hands, and the Sundays. The main edits simplify the story-to-person connection and remove repeated statements about the drink's construction.

### Original

Tagline: You believe in people before they believe in themselves.

**epigraph**
*Bitter orange and sweet orange, side by side. Neither one is hiding the other.*

**whoYouAre**
You can tell when someone is going to be all right, usually a good while before they can. A friend swears they'll never get over it, and you think: give it till spring. You don't say so, because it would sound like you weren't listening. You just keep turning up with something easy to say yes to: a walk, an errand, a Sunday that needs a second pair of hands. And one day they notice they've been getting better for a while, somewhere in all those Sundays.

You know that help can taste bitter. Being looked after can feel like being told you're not coping, so you make yours easy to take. But the few who didn't get better stay with you. You go back over them, wondering what you missed, as if their mending were yours to finish. That's the price of believing in people before they do, and you've never once thought it wasn't worth paying.

**yours**

1. In the 1830s, a young man from Marseille, still in his twenties, caught a fever in Algeria. He was treated the usual way then, with wine steeped with quinine and other bitters. He knew what that tasted like.
2. The way it's told, that fever is what set Gaëtan Picon trying, from 1837, to make a tonic that tasted better. By 1840 he had a recipe: bitter orange peel and sweet orange peel together, with bitter roots and a bark. He opened a small distillery, and his amer, a bitter orange liqueur, was soon drunk in quantity across Algeria. Later it reached France.
3. I like to think he never forgot being the one handed the glass nobody wanted to drink, and that's why he didn't take the bitter out, only put something sweet beside it, so people would come back for it on their own. I can't tell you whether it made anyone better. I can tell you people kept choosing it: by 1909 it took about three million oranges a year. That's how your help works too. It stays honest, and it still gets taken.
4. So I crafted your cocktail from his idea, not his bottle, and long, the way people came to drink his amer in the Basque communities of the American West: with soda, a little grenadine and brandy on top. The amer in your cocktail is a modern one, made with the old Amer Picon in mind, and the rest of the glass is built around it. The grenadine is my own pomegranate syrup, with a sweet orange's peel rubbed into the sugar, so a bitter orange and a sweet one sit side by side, the way they did in 1840. Then soda, and the cognac poured last over a spoon so it rests on top. The first sip is the gentle one. The bitter comes right after, and nothing covers it.
5. The ones who didn't get better: put them down. Their mending was never yours to finish. You were the sweet beside the bitter, the reason the hard part got taken at all, and that was always the job. Next time you catch yourself going back over one of them, remember the Sundays instead.

Closing line: *Pour the brandy last, over a spoon. How it lands was never all up to you.*

#### Your cocktail

1. In the 1830s, a young man from Marseille, still in his twenties, caught a fever in Algeria. He was treated the usual way then, with wine steeped with quinine and other bitters.

2. The way it's told, the fever led Gaëtan Picon to start trying in 1837 to make a tonic that tasted better. By 1840 he had a recipe: bitter orange peel and sweet orange peel together, with bitter roots and a bark. He opened a small distillery, and his amer, a bitter orange liqueur, was soon drunk in quantity across Algeria. Later it reached France.

3. I like to think he remembered being handed a glass nobody wanted to drink, and that he kept the bitter and added something sweet so people would come back of their own accord. I can't tell you whether it made anyone better. People did keep choosing it: by 1909, it took about three million oranges a year. You find ways to offer help that people can accept without hiding what's hard.

4. I've made you a long cocktail in the tradition of the Basque communities of the American West, where Picon's amer was drunk with soda, a little grenadine and brandy on top. I use a modern amer made with the old Amer Picon in mind. For the grenadine, I rub sweet orange peel into the sugar for my pomegranate syrup, so bitter orange and sweet orange sit together as they did in his 1840 recipe. Then I add soda and pour the cognac last, over a spoon, so it rests on top. The first sip is gentle. Then you taste the bitter, uncovered.

5. When you find yourself going back over someone who didn't get better, remember the Sundays instead. You made the hard part easier to take. Their mending was never yours to finish.

Closing line: Pour the brandy last, over a spoon. How it lands was never all up to you.

## With the Bite In · The Subverter

This person uses a deliberate joke to question power where a direct statement would be stopped. They fear hearing their words repeated with the point removed. Their gift is helping others feel less afraid of whoever or whatever is in charge.

The original's best detail is the person at the head of the table laughing a beat late. The edits keep it and trim the sentences that explain the joke afterwards, along with the aphorism about borrowing a joke but not its maker.

Source: [Original With the Bite In dossier](</Users/robin.molinas/Documents/GenAI Projects/Dionysus/Cocktail_Website_Agent/design-artifacts/pours/outlaw-jester.md>).

### Original

Tagline: Your jokes give people the nerve to question who's in charge.

**epigraph**
*A song made rum and Coca-Cola famous. The song was never really about the drink.*

**whoYouAre**
There's a meeting, or a family dinner, or a rule nobody's supposed to question, and everyone's nodding along. Then you say something, lightly, and the table laughs, the person at the head of it too, a beat late. Nobody can quite tell you off. It was only funny. That was the idea.

Nobody notices that you chose it. The joke isn't a reflex, and it never punches down. It's aimed at whoever's at the head of the table, because that's the one place a plain sentence would be stopped. So you keep quiet about how much of it you mean: the moment they know, they stop laughing and start stopping you. A telling-off you can live with; it means it landed. What you dread is hearing your line from somebody else, everyone laughing, with the point quietly taken out.

And what you give is more than a laugh. When a room laughs at whatever's in charge, it sees how ordinary the thing really is, and fears it less. That's how you hand people their nerve back, and it's a real gift, whatever you call it.

**yours**

1. In wartime Trinidad, with an American base on the island, a calypso singer called Lord Invader set new words to an old tune by Lionel Belasco. Where he sang, songs had to get past the police commissioner's board of censors. His got through. It was called "Rum and Coca-Cola", it laughed at what the soldiers' money was doing to the island, and soldiers and Trinidadians alike sang it.

2. In 1943 an American comedian, Morey Amsterdam, heard it while entertaining the troops and took it home. The Andrews Sisters made it a hit, and the first 200,000 records named Amsterdam as the writer. A New York court later found that Lord Invader had written the words. The money reached him seven years late, and even then he was often short.

3. While the case went on, he recorded "Yankee Dollar", a follow-up that turned the joke on himself: the man who'd sung the hit, still without the dollars. Years later came "Crisis in Arkansas", which laughed at the black bow tie of the governor who had kept nine Black students out of a Little Rock school. I like to think he'd worked out what you know: someone can take a line, but never the place it came from.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is rum and Coca-Cola. It's older than the song, and simple, but made properly it's a classic worth asking for by name, long and cold over plenty of ice. The rum I'd pick is from Trinidad, from the same house as the Angostura bitters that go on last, so the island reaches you before anything else. And the lime, squeezed and dropped into the glass, to cut through the cola's sweetness. I doubled the lime in the recipe I started from, as a nod to the song: sweet enough for everyone to sing, and still sharp.

5. One day you'll hear a line of yours from someone else, to a big laugh, with the point gone. Don't spend the evening proving it was yours. Come up with another, and aim it a little higher than the last. A joke can be borrowed; the person it came from can't, and every room that laughed with you is a little braver for it.

Closing line: *Squeeze the lime and drop it in. If they sing it back wrong, write a new one.*
