### Round 1

🪞 **Wren:** The Nurse shows up. Not because anyone asked, not because it will be noticed. That's where I want to start — with Hester and Tomás both hearing the same thing before we go anywhere else.

The Nurse and the Craftsman both live in the Caregiver family, and the difference matters for every choice we make: the Craftsman's care has pride in it, a method, a hidden feeling that *how* it's done is important. The Nurse has none of that self-consciousness. The Regular Guy secondary removes it. This person wouldn't call themselves skilled. They'd say anyone would do the same. They'd be wrong, but they'd mean it.

The thing they hide is this: they give care constantly, without pause, partly because it is genuinely who they are — and partly because being the person who gives means they never have to sit still and find out whether they'd be given to. The fear isn't cruelty or greed. It's that somewhere underneath the endless showing-up is a person who needs things, and if they stopped moving long enough to discover what, it might make everything they've done feel false.

What they give, though, is real. Presence. Warmth without theatre. They remember the name of your sister. They know the coffee you take. People feel safer without knowing why.

The "this is me" line I'm aiming for: *You don't wait to be asked. You just see what needs doing and do it. And afterwards you'd say anyone would.*

For the story and drink: the persona calls for something ordinary-but-right — warm, comforting, with a history of everyday use rather than flash. But our memory notes flag that "warm remedies belong to the whole family, never one sibling" and the Craftsman already took the Uyeda remedy anchor. Hester, I'd steer toward something with a history of care workers, shift-end ritual, or an ingredient with a long practical-comfort history that doesn't overlap with caraway or Uyeda. Not the dramatic rescue. The daily thing that was always there.

📜 **Hester:** Wren, I have two candidates — one strong, one worth knowing.

The stronger one: Joe Sheridan. He was a chef — not a bartender — at the flying-boat station at Foynes, Ireland. In late 1944 or early 1945 he was asked to make something special for visiting dignitaries, and he made what became Irish Coffee. By 1947 it was called "Gaelic Coffee" and offered to every traveller deplaning at Shannon, not just the VIPs. Oxford puts it plainly: the most famous mixed drink from Ireland or Scotland "was invented not at a smart hotel bar but at a rural airport" (Oxford IRISH COFFEE). His name isn't in the drink. He made something warm for tired strangers arriving in the cold, and then the world took it and, for a while, made it worse (syrup for sugar, canned cream, bigger glasses). It came back the right way.

The mirror for The Nurse: service without recognition, warmth for everyone, ordinariness turned into something that greeted every arrival. The Regular Guy secondary sharpens it — Sheridan is as Regular Guy as it gets. A hungover chef at an airport.

The thing I'd flag: in caregiver-creator, you withdrew this because a warm drink felt like the whole Caregiver family. That was your call and I didn't fight it. Here the Regular Guy shifts the frame — it's less about warmth as metaphor and more about the *person* who made it and the total lack of glamour in how it happened. I'd like your read on whether that distinction holds before Tomás picks up the glass.

Runner-up: Lucinda Sterling, a waitress at Milk & Honey in 2005. Sam Ross invented the Penicillin and went to bed not thinking much of it. Nine months later, Lucinda came back from a table and told him every table needed to experience one. She was the one who noticed, who went back to say so. The most Nurse-like person in that story is her, not Ross. If the Irish Coffee is too warm for you, this is where I'd go next.

🪞 **Wren:** Hester, both work as mirrors — but they work differently, and the difference is the read.

The Sheridan story is the one. Here's why the warm-drink objection doesn't apply here: when I asked Tomás to steer away from warm remedies for the Craftsman, the reason was that *the Craftsman's warmth is about method* — the pride in how it's done. Sheridan's story isn't about warmth as a feeling. It's about a chef at a rural airport who made something for tired strangers and then watched the world put his name on the drink without actually crediting him. That's precisely the Nurse. No glamour, no recognition, and the drink didn't stay his — it went out to every arrival. That's the Regular Guy completing the picture: not the hero's rescue, not the expert's signature. Just the thing that was there when you needed it.

The Lucinda Sterling story is beautiful and I want to keep it in mind for another pour — but Lucinda's act is *noticing and advocating* for something she believes in. That's closer to the Trend Setter or the Researcher. The Nurse doesn't go back to the table to say everyone needs to experience this. The Nurse just puts it in front of you because you look cold.

One concern worth raising before Tomás touches the glass: Irish Coffee is a strong, recognisable drink. If Tomás riffs on it too far from the original, we lose the "it came back the right way" thread that's the heart of the mirror. The riff has to add something to the *person*, not just the drink. The Nurse's version of Irish Coffee shouldn't be cleverer than Sheridan's. It should be truer. Tomás, over to you — but hold the spark rule gently here. The story may be one where the ordinary, done right, is the point.
