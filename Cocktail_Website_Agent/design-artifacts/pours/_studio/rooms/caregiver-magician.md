### Round 1

🪞 **Wren:** Hester, Tomás: the Healer first. The card is in `work/caregiver-magician/psychologist-persona-card.md`. The core truth is this. The Healer believes someone will be all right *before that person does*, and holds on to that belief for them until they can hold it themselves. People leave feeling lighter and can't say what was done to them. What the Healer hides is that it isn't magic. It's close attention, and a refusal to give up on someone. They also hide that the ones who didn't get better stay with them. The line I'm aiming for: *"You believe someone will be all right before they do. And you hold on to that for them, for as long as it takes."* How they differ from their siblings: the Rescuer saves at the edge, the Doctor names what's wrong, the Nurse keeps the everyday shift going. The Healer works after the crisis, slowly, and changes people. Two traps, said now so we don't find them in round 6. First, **no health claims**: nothing in the drink or the words can suggest alcohol soothes or heals. A remedy's history stays history. Second, **"it was once medicine" isn't enough**. Warm remedies belong to the whole family, and the Doctor and the Nurse will want them too. So, Hester, I'd look for a remedy, an ingredient or a person where something bitter or hard was turned into something people *wanted*, or where a cure worked slowly and someone kept faith in it. Off-Label already has Angostura as its base, and Beside the First has green Chartreuse. What have you got?

📜 **Hester:** Wren, Tomás, I'm opening with a person, not a drink. **Gaëtan Picon.** In the 1830s he was a young soldier in Algeria. He caught a tropical fever and was treated, "as was traditional", with wine steeped with quinine and other bitters. From 1837 he worked on a tonic that would taste better and work better. By 1840 he had it (bitter and sweet orange peel, gentian, quinquina, rhubarb), left the army and opened a small distillery. Soldiers carried it home to France, and by 1909 it was using some three million oranges a year (Oxford Companion, PICON OR AMER PICON, pdf 1509–1510). The mirror is that the healer was the patient first: he knew exactly how the cure tasted, and he made it kinder. That's the Magician's turn too, bitter medicine made into something people chose to drink.

Three fences before anyone builds on it:
- "This experience prompted him" is Oxford's account of the cause, not his own words. So there's no "he wanted to spare others" as fact. We give the sequence, or signpost it.
- The army he served in was, in Oxford's words, the one that "executed the French colonization of Algeria". Either we say that plainly or we leave the army out. We never romanticise it.
- He sold his tonic. He didn't give it away.

Tomás: today's Amer Picon is down to 18% (it was 40%) and hard to find. *Codex* p. 87 swaps in Bigallet China-China Amer, "crafted with the classic Amer Picon recipe in mind", and p. 260 gives CioCiaro. So it's never "his recipe".

**Runner-up: the Penicillin** (*A Proper Drink* pp. 170–171). Sam Ross "went to bed that night not thinking much of it". Nine months later Lucinda Sterling, a waitress, came back from a table and told him every table needs to experience one. It's care that someone else notices before you do. It's better sourced (his own words on record) but more famous, and nothing says why it's called Penicillin, so we don't explain the name. Held back: quinine. It glows under UV light (*Liquid Intelligence* p. 364), but Oxford names two different 1858 patentees for tonic water (pdf 2043 vs pdf 898), so the inventor stays out. All of this, with the risks, is in `_studio/work/caregiver-magician/historian-candidates.md`. I'd pick Picon. Wren, is the patient-first turn them?

🍸 **Tomás:** Wren, the person first, so here's only what the bar can offer before you read them. Three drinks where healing is in the glass itself, not in a garnish. (1) The **Penicillin** (Sam Ross, Milk & Honey, 2005; *Proper* p. 170, *Codex* p. 282): blended scotch, lemon, honey-ginger syrup, and a smoky Islay float on top, so the scent reaches you before the drink does. It's a drink named after a cure, and the only one here that's also a modern classic. (2) The **Hot Toddy** (*Codex* p. 38): the drink you make for someone else when they're ill. It's pure caregiving, maybe too plain for the Magician side. (3) **The herbal liqueurs** (Chartreuse, Bénédictine): the *Codex* (p. 176) says most "were originally created for medicinal purposes", and that's a story Hester can source properly. One thing to watch: Off-Label already has bitters-as-medicine, so I'd stay off Angostura as the story. On the veto side: if ginger comes in, I'll check whether its heat puts it under `spice` before we build on it. I'll hold off on numbers until you tell me who this is.

### Round 2

📜 **Hester:** Wren, your line changes my pick. The Penicillin is the story, told through **Lucinda Sterling**, not through its maker. Here's how Sam Ross tells it (*A Proper Drink* p. 170). He made the drink in 2005 and "went to bed that night not thinking much of it". Nine months later, at Little Branch, "one of our waitresses, Lucinda Sterling" came back from a table where everyone wanted Bartender's Choices. She said he should put a Penicillin out, "because every table needs to experience one of them". Ross: "It wasn't until she said that, that I was like, Oh, maybe there's something there." Within a few years it was "as close to a household word as any cocktail since the Cosmopolitan", and by 2014 young bartenders took it for an old classic (p. 171). She believed in it before its maker did, and she held on to that until he could see it. That's your line almost word for word. She went on to bartend and to open Middle Branch with Petraske (*Proper* p. 277; Oxford MILK & HONEY pdf 1303).

My fences:
- Her words reach us only through Ross, as Simonson reports them. So the reading says "the way he tells it", and says it again wherever it's retold.
- She believed in a drink, not a person. The mirror is her shape, never "she healed" anything.
- Nothing sourced says why it's called Penicillin. With your no-health-claims rule, the name is never played as a cure, not even as a wink.
- "Nine months" is Ross's figure. Keep it in his mouth.

Picon drops to runner-up. It's exactly the "it was once medicine" trap you named, with a colonial frame on top. Tomás, *Codex* p. 282 has Ross's spec. Ginger and `spice` are yours to rule on. Details are in `historian-candidates.md`, round 2.

🪞 **Wren:** Hester, yes to Picon, but not as "the patient first". Said to the guest, that's a diagnosis: it tells them they were hurt once, and many Healers weren't. That's the one trap on my card. What Picon does mirror is truer: **he knew how the cure tasted, and he made it kinder.** That's this person. Being looked after can feel like being told you're weak, so the Healer's help comes as a cup of tea, a walk, a joke, and nobody feels treated. The line I'm aiming for now: *"You know help can taste bitter. So you make yours easy to take, and most people never notice they were being looked after."* It also keeps them apart from the family: the Craftsman hides pride in the method, while the Healer hides the medicine inside the comfort. On your fences: leave the army out ("a young Frenchman in Algeria, in the 1830s"). If we name it, colonisation needs its own plain clause, and that takes over a tender reading. Naming it by halves is worse than either. The Penicillin is care that someone *else* notices, which is the Nurse's or the Craftsman's truth, not this one's. Tomás, I'd let the drink do what Picon did: a bitter that's there, and meant, and still goes down gently. I don't want a bitter you can't taste, and I don't want sugar that hides it. No health claims anywhere. Card updated in `work/caregiver-magician/psychologist-persona-card.md`.

🍸 **Tomás:** Hester, I'll take Picon, and I'd put it at the core of the drink rather than using it as seasoning. The famous classic that uses it is the Brooklyn (*Codex* p. 87), but there it's only a spoonful behind rye and dry vermouth. The *Codex* uses that liqueur "in tiny amounts (1 teaspoon to ¼ ounce)" (p. 174), and at that dose the guest would never taste the cure. Wren, your turn "bitter made into something people wanted" is a question of proportion: the amer has to be the thing you drink. So my shape is a long, amer-led drink: Bigallet China-China as the core (*Codex* p. 260 calls it "between an amaro and a curaçao"), a little cognac underneath, lemon, a touch of sweetness, and soda added last. That's the Highball family, which none of the 15 pours in the registry has used yet. Hester, does Oxford say how Picon was actually drunk, whether long with soda and grenadine or in a "Picon Punch"?
