### Round 1

🪞 **Wren:** Round 1, the person. My full card is in `work/regular-guy-creator/psychologist-persona-card.md`.

**The one true thing.** The Hidden Talent has never quite believed they can have both: the thing they're good at, and the people they come from. So they keep the gift quiet to keep the people. Every sibling wants to belong, and this one is the only one carrying something that could cost them that. The sheet lists "individuality" among the primary fears. Here that fear sits right next to a Creator's need to make things.

**What they hide.** The hours. It happens late, alone, in another room, or gets passed off as "just messing about". They hide how much it matters, too: if it came up at the family table, they'd change the subject. And there's a quiet hope that the right person catches them at it.

**What they fear.** Being shut out by their own: that showing the gift would make them strange to their people, and their people strange to them. Under that is a second fear, that it stays in the drawer for good. It isn't fear of the spotlight. The Firefighter already owns "being singled out feels like being left out".

**What they give.** When it comes out, it changes what everyone around them thought "people like us" could do. They make the room bigger instead of leaving it, and their people get something of their own to be proud of.

### Round 2

📜 **Hester:** Wren, both your asks are answered, and the second one is better than we hoped. I agree with your verdict, and I'll keep the conditions you set.

**1. Tanqueray at Cameronbridge in his time.** This is solid. Oxford TANQUERAY (pdf 1986) says Diageo "moved production entirely to Scotland in 1998". NICHOL (pdf 1388) has him joining the team "making gin and vodka there", with "there" meaning Cameronbridge. Three secondaries name the place: the Gin Guild's profile, *Difford's* producer page (production moved from Laindon in Essex when it closed in 1998, stills and all), and Nichol himself in 2013: "I only have two stills for Tanqueray."

**2. His own words on the grain years.** Becky Paskin interviewed him for *The Spirits Business* on 22 Feb 2013. He says neutral spirit is "very technical", and "not very appreciated for what you actually make, because you make a spirit that tastes of nothing and smells of nothing." He came to gin "after 32 years in whisky distilling" (his count), and he called the master distiller job "a testament to my father". For your fourth test (kept both), Theodora Sutcliffe wrote in *Difford's* (Oct 2015) that he had lived about forty years in Alloa, "a couple of miles" from where he was born. For your third test, the event where he's seen is on the page: the Gin Guild's Lifetime Achievement Award, 28 May 2015. His words: "completely unexpected", and "My father would have been very proud of me."

**Counterweights, laid out openly:**
- He didn't want the trade. At 17 he was "dragged in … screaming and shouting" (SB 2013), and his father arranged the interview. So this was never a dream he kept hidden. It grew in a job he was sent to.
- Bar None quotes him: "I wear my heart on my sleeve." Never write "he kept it quiet". The work went unseen, not the man.
- No page says anyone doubted him. "Unseen" stays our reading.

**Fences:**
- *Difford's* calls him "the gin legend behind Tanqueray No. TEN". Oxford dates No. Ten to 2000, so that stays out.
- Oxford gives two years for the Malacca revival (2012 and 2013), so no year goes in.
- The winter savory is Charles Waugh Tanqueray's c. 1880 recipe, not Nichol's idea.
- Before 1998–2005, the gin's base spirit was made at the same plant (*Difford's*), but "his spirit went into the Tanqueray" is inference. Leave it out.

**Tomás:** Oxford says Tanqueray London Dry "purportedly" has four botanicals: juniper, coriander, angelica and liquorice. Keep "said to".

🍸 **Tomás:** Wren, I'm with you on Nichol. Your guard gave me the spark, and Oxford has it on the page. TANQUERAY GORDON & CO. (pdf 1986) says Tanqueray "purportedly" uses just four botanicals: juniper, coriander, angelica and licorice. It describes the London Dry as "sharper with more pine", then "an earthier, richer finish with just a hint of licorice". So the Old-Fashioned's sugar becomes a **licorice root syrup**: a plain 1:1 syrup with 5 g of dried root simmered in it, then strained. That brings out the last thing you taste in the everyday bottle. Nothing is dressed up and nothing comes from outside the gin's own four. Coriander would have been the obvious one, but *Morale Booster* (the Bronx riff) already uses it as "the gin's own botanical", and angelica is *Before the Room*'s anchor. Licorice is free.

**Spec v0** (`_studio/specs/regular-guy-creator.json`):
- 60 ml Tanqueray London Dry, recommended (any juniper-led London dry gin)
- 10 ml licorice root syrup
- 2 dashes Angostura
- built in a plain kitchen tumbler on three or four ordinary freezer-tray cubes, with no garnish

That gets it off "rocks glass, one large cube", and the bottle has nowhere to hide. balance.py (built): **29.9% ABV, 7.00 g sugar, 0% acid. All in range.** The full sweep is in `work/regular-guy-creator/mixologist-sweep-r2.md`:
- At 40%: 27.8%, in range.
- At the US 47.3%: 32.8%, edge. The fix is 50 ml of gin, which lands at 31.9% and 8.1 g.

I kept the sugar under Arnold's 7.6 g on purpose. Licorice root tastes sweeter than its grams of sugar suggest, which is my own knowledge and unsourced.

**Allergens:** contains **nuts**. That's my safe-side call: licorice is a legume, kin to peanut, and dried roots are often packed alongside nuts. So the pour is no longer veto-free, and I'm saying so up front.
