### Round 1

🪞 **Wren:** Round 1, so this is the person first. Full card: `_studio/work/outlaw-creator/psychologist-persona-card.md`.

**The one true thing.** A no has never frightened them. What they don't trust is the yes. Being refused gives them fuel. Being invited in (bought, licensed, made official, "framed and hung on the establishment's wall") is the day they fear they'll stop being able to make anything. Under that is the doubt they hide: *is what I make good, or only good because it isn't allowed?* And quieter still, part of them would like the thing to last. That usually means letting someone in charge have it, and they're a little ashamed of wanting that.

**Fear:** losing power in one particular way: being absorbed. Once they say yes, the people in charge own the thing, and them. The second cost nobody says out loud is their own crowd calling them a sell-out. **Gift:** they make the better version before anyone allows it, so everyone gets to see what was possible. Much of what people are later allowed, someone like this made first. Per Robin's rule, the reading says that once, plainly. **Position, direction only:** it was never good because it was forbidden. Make one thing that isn't against anything and find out it stands on its own. That's in their interest because it frees them from needing a wall.

**Siblings.** The closest trap isn't in this family. It's the Trend Setter's *Still Yours*. That pour is taste and the crowd. This one is making and power: the fear is the people in charge taking the thing in, not other people liking it. So the reading never uses "keep it", "still yours", "letting them choose for you", or "permission" as the gift (that's *Still Yours*' gift line). The Adolescent is the mirror: Vogler refuses the deal, while this story takes it and the thing survives. The Leading Edge owns "sold to a giant", so it can't be our ending. The Gentle Giant's *In One Piece* turns on a licence **taken away**, and ours is a licence **taken up**. That's the same noun in two sibling pours, so we say why in one clause and never show a licence in the image. Against the Trickster, my recognition line mustn't use the "isn't getting caught, it's being ignored" shape.

**Traps.**
- Never make the guest a lawbreaker. The mirror is "made without anyone's say-so".
- No pistols and no fire. The threat is his old colleagues turning on him.
- Never "outlaw" or "creator".
- No 1884 court order (the son's fight, and it lands on "keep it").

- Hester, Oxford pdf 917 says the distillery is now owned by Chivas/Pernod Ricard and is the world's second-bestselling single malt. That is literally this guest's "defeat" and the Leading Edge's ending, so it stays out of the reading. Tomás, keeping the recommended Glenlivet 12 is fine, but no line may call today's bottle "his whisky" or "the same whisky". No page says Smith's illicit spirit was a twelve-year-old malt.

**My story test.** (1) Made without anyone's say-so, on ground that wasn't theirs. (2) Good, and the page proves it: the people in charge wanted it before it was allowed. (3) The way in is offered, and taking it costs them with their own side. (4) After the yes it's still good, on a page and in their lifetime.

**Verdict on the lead, George Smith of the Glenlivet: accept.** I read Oxford pdf 917–918 whole.
- **Point 1:** he distilled illegally "since at least 1815", as the Duke of Gordon's tenant.
- **Point 2:** George IV "reportedly 'drank nothing else'" in 1822, and Usher's were selling it by 1821. The people who make the rules were drinking what the rules forbade. That's the scene this guest will see themselves in. Keep "reportedly".
- **Point 3:** the Duke was "largely responsible" for the 1823 Act that "encouraged illicit distillers to 'go legal'". He urged Smith, Smith took the region's first licence, and his old colleagues turned on him.
- **Point 4 is the only weak one.** Usher's were selling "the Real Glenlivet" in London by 1844, and thirty distilleries had borrowed the name by the 1880s. That implies the licensed whisky stayed good, but no page says he kept making it the same way. Hester, can you find one? If not, the reading says only "he went on making it, in the open".

Tomás, nothing from you yet as a spec. As you think: the glass shouldn't be a protest or something smuggled. A spark that makes the whisky more itself fits this person. One that argues against something doesn't.

### Round 2

📜 **Hester:** Wren, I accept George Smith too. On your point 4 the answer is no: no page says he kept making it the same way. The nearest is Oxford SCOTLAND AND IRELAND pdf 1769, where the country distillers "who did things the small and old-fashioned way" won out, "Glenlivet, for instance". But that sentence is about country distillers in general, and what it says of Glenlivet is where it stands today. What the pages do give is demand during his lifetime. In the 1820s his Glenlivet had already attracted "free riders", people trading on the name (SPIRITS MEASURE pdf 1879). He opened a second distillery in 1840. By 1844 Usher's were selling "the Real Glenlivet Whisky" in London, the first scotch promoted there (GLENLIVET pdf 917). So your line stands, and it can carry dates: "he went on making it, in the open, and within twenty years London was being sold it as the real one."

A find for your point 2. Oxford's "reportedly" comes from Elizabeth Grant's *Memoirs of a Highland Lady* (1898, p. 373, I read the page on archive.org). The king's Chamberlain was "looking everywhere for pure Glenlivet whisky", and it couldn't be had outside the Highlands. Her father was about to be made a judge, and he had her empty her own store of it, whisky with "the true contraband goût in it". That's your hidden doubt, written down by someone who drank it: part of the charm was that it was forbidden. Two limits on it. Neither Grant nor Oxford names Smith here, because this is the glen's illicit whisky. So no line may say the king drank *his* whisky. Second, after 1823 the country distillers moved to all-malt whisky, and before that theirs often had oats and rye in it (WHISKY pdf 2132). So the illicit spirit is never called a single malt.

Three more fences. The page says his old colleagues threatened him, and has no word like "sell-out", so don't put one in their mouths. Usher's 1853 vatting is his whisky mixed with others, which reads as absorbed, so it stays out. Tomás, *Codex* p. 204 calls the 12 "massively produced" and "not terribly complex". Neither phrase goes in guest text. Use Oxford's "subtle, even delicate" (pdf 918).

My strongest point, now, so it isn't saved for later: Oxford calls the 12 "made without influence of peat or sherry, … subtle, even delicate" (GLENLIVET pdf 918).
