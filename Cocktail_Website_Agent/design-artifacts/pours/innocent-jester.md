# Pour — There It Goes · The Giggler (innocent-jester)

## Cocktail

- **name:** There It Goes
- **tagline:** You find it funny first. Then everyone does.
- **glassware:** a coupe (about 180–200 ml), chilled
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 22.5 ml (¾ oz) | bourbon | 43% or stronger |
| 22.5 ml (¾ oz) | Amaro Nonino Quintessentia, recommended | an Italian bitter herbal liqueur made on grappa (a brandy distilled from what's left after winemaking); or any other bitter herbal liqueur made on grappa |
| 22.5 ml (¾ oz) | Aperol, recommended | or any bright orange Italian bitter aperitif of about 11% |
| 20 ml | fresh lemon juice | a touch less than the printed recipe's ¾ oz, so it's bright, not sharp |
| 5 drops | salt water | 1 level teaspoon fine salt stirred into 4 teaspoons water; or a small pinch of fine salt |

**method**
1. Make the salt water once: stir 1 level teaspoon of fine salt into 4 teaspoons of warm water until it's clear. It keeps in the fridge for months.
2. Put the coupe in the freezer to chill.
3. Pour the bourbon, the Nonino, the Aperol and the lemon juice into a shaker. Add five drops of the salt water.
4. Fill the shaker three-quarters full with ice, close it and shake for about 12 seconds, until the shaker's too cold to hold comfortably.
5. Strain into the cold coupe. No garnish.

**closingLine:** *Five drops of salt water before you shake. And when something's funny, laugh out loud.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*Behind this drink is one named for whatever song was on. One city adopted it.*

**whoYouAre**
A friend is halfway through a story, and you've already gone. Not a polite laugh: the real one, eyes watering, trying to say "sorry, go on" and not managing it. You're hardly ever the one telling the joke. You're the one who can't stop laughing at it, and that's usually the moment it gets funny for everybody else.

What people don't see is how many you hold in: in meetings, at the serious dinner, anywhere someone might think less of you for it. A laugh held in sits in your chest all afternoon. You've heard "you'd laugh at anything" more times than you can count. You wouldn't. You just find more of it funny. Now and then there's the second when you're the only one laughing and the room doesn't follow, and it stays with you longer than you let on. And the fear underneath is being told off for it so often that one day you stop. Please don't. When you laugh at something someone said, they leave feeling funny.

**yours**

1. In 2001, Sam Ross's family opened a cocktail bar in Fitzroy, in Melbourne, and called it Ginger. Most people who went remember it as inventive and odd: "lots of orange plastic", one bartender remembered, like "a psychedelic spaceship". Sebastian Reaburn, who worked there, said the menu "read like a comedy script". And Jacob Briars said the thing I love most about it: at Ginger, "there was no such thing as the idea anything might be wrong".

2. Not everyone was charmed, Sydney's bartenders least of all. In Reaburn's words, the place was all "so tongue in cheek", and "there was a credibility gap". Some years later, Ross made a drink for the opening menu of a new bar in Chicago, one where he never worked. It's called the Paper Plane, after a song by M.I.A. he had on while he was working it out. It became, he said, the second most travelled of his drinks, and Toronto took to it so completely that years later he called it "essentially the official drink of Toronto". Why Toronto, he couldn't say.

3. I like to think that's the Ginger in it. A whole city's bars joined in, and not even the man who made it could explain why. That's how your laugh works too. Nobody in the room decides to follow it. They just do. And I like to think the name suits it for one more reason, though it came from the song: a paper plane is the thing that sails across a room where everyone's being very serious, and somebody always smiles.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is the Paper Plane, a modern classic, with two small changes of mine. It's bourbon, Aperol (a bright orange Italian aperitif, gently bitter), Amaro Nonino (an Italian bitter herbal liqueur made on grappa, a brandy distilled from the grape skins, stems and seeds left once the juice is pressed out for wine) and fresh lemon. It's shaken until the shaker's uncomfortable to hold, and poured off the ice into a coupe that's been waiting in the freezer. I've taken a little of the lemon out, so it's bright rather than sharp. Then I've added five drops of salted water. It isn't meant to taste salty. It brings nothing of its own; it makes everything around it come forward, and it takes the frown off the bitter. I put it there for you, because that's what your laugh does for other people's jokes.

5. Next time something's funny in a room that's gone stiff, don't hold it in. Look round first: there's nearly always someone else trying not to laugh. Let yours go, and you'll have given them theirs. Then enjoy it. It really was funny.
