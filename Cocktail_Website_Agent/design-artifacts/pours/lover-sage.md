# Pour — In Kind · The Romantic (lover-sage)

## Cocktail

- **name:** In Kind
- **tagline:** You take what people love seriously enough to ask the next question.
- **glassware:** julep cup (pewter or stainless steel; any metal tumbler of about 300 ml at home)
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 ml (2 oz) | bourbon, 45-50% (90-100 proof) | any straight bourbon at that strength; the story names no bottle |
| 7.5 ml (¼ oz) | rich sugar syrup (below) | the ode's sweetener: sugar mixed into cold water until it's thick as oil |
| a few leaves | fresh mint (spearmint) | pressed round the inside of the cup, then taken out, as the ode asks |
| about 2 cups | ice cubes, pounded in a canvas bag | the way Chris McMillian pounded the ice for his juleps; a clean tea towel works too |
| 1 bouquet (4-5 sprigs) | fresh mint (spearmint) | set in the ice beside the straw |

**Rich sugar syrup:** 100 g sugar and 50 g cold water in a jar. Close it and shake until the sugar has gone and the syrup runs thick and clear (a few minutes; fine sugar goes faster). Fridge, about a month.

**method**
1. Put the cup in the freezer for ten minutes.
2. Put the ice cubes in a clean canvas bag (or fold them into a tea towel) and pound them with a wooden mallet or a rolling pin until they're fine and snowy. The canvas soaks up the meltwater, so the ice goes in dry.
3. Take the cup out, holding it by the rim. Press a few mint leaves round the inside with the back of a spoon, every side, then take the leaves out.
4. Fill the cup halfway with pounded ice. Pour the bourbon over the ice, then pour the syrup over the top.
5. Still holding the cup by the rim, churn the ice with a long spoon for about ten seconds. Add ice to two-thirds full and keep churning until the outside of the cup is frosted all over. The ice gives the drink about 30 ml of water as you go: that's part of it.
6. Heap more ice on top in a mound above the rim. Push a short straw in at one side, and set the mint bouquet in the ice right beside it, so the mint is under your nose at every sip.

**closingLine:** *Pound the ice loud enough that someone asks. Then give them the long version.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*This drink has its own ode. A bartender used to say it while pounding the ice.*

**whoYouAre**
At dinner, a friend mentions the book that got them through a bad winter. The talk moves on. You don't. You ask what it was about that book, then about the one page they still think of, and half an hour later the two of you are somewhere the rest of the table never went. You take what people love seriously, and they remember who did.

You do the easy talk well enough: the trains, the weather. But you feel the evening sliding past on the surface, and later you write three pages to someone who wasn't there. At some point you started keeping the long version for the one person you're sure will follow it. Here's the thing you'd never tell the table: you've decided most rooms aren't the place for it. Sometimes you're right. But it means you can sit at a full table and still feel unmet. Not an empty room: a full one.

**yours**

1. Chris McMillian came to bartending in his thirties, in New Orleans, and was intrigued by the city's old drinking life. He researched its long-gone bars and early cocktails, piecing his trade's history together drink by drink, and he shared what he found freely, with other bartenders and with the people at his bar, before that kind of history was fashionable. Before Hurricane Katrina, he was one of the few in the city trying anything adventurous behind a bar.

2. Around 2000, he recalls, at the Ritz-Carlton's small library bar, he took to making Mint Juleps the old way, pounding the ice in a canvas bag with a big mallet, in rhythm. And as he pounded, he'd recite an ode to the julep: a long, lush piece of prose, usually credited to a writer called Soule Smith, and printed in a newspaper by 1907. It goes on far longer than any recipe needs to. It even calls bourbon and mint a pair made for each other, grown from the same land and the same stream.

3. It had been reprinted and quoted for decades before him. But I like to think that's what being answered looks like: someone, generations later, taking a long piece of writing seriously enough to give it a voice, in a hotel bar, where most talk is small. It's the reply you hope for whenever you send the long version. And it's the risk you rarely take, because I doubt he waited to find out who at the bar deserved it.

4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Mint Julep, the renowned classic, put together in the order the ode sets out. The sugar is mixed into cold water till it's as thick as oil, as the ode asks. Mint is pressed all round the inside of the cup, then taken out. The bourbon goes over the ice first, the syrup over that, and the ice is pounded in a canvas bag, McMillian's way, so it goes in dry. Churned, the cup frosts white all over. I broke one of the ode's rules: it says no stirring, and I churn it, so the sweetness doesn't sink to the bottom. Otherwise it follows the ode's steps, as a nod to you: you're the one who reads all of a thing. A bouquet of fresh mint stands beside the straw, so the scent and the taste arrive together, the way the ode wanted. It's ice-cold, fragrant and quietly strong, and it's built to last.

5. Next time the talk at a full table stays on the weather, don't save the long version for later, or for the one person you're sure of. Give it to the room. Say what a book did to you, or why one song still gets you every time. Not everyone will follow. Someone will, and it may be the person you'd least have picked. That's how a crowded evening stops being lonely: you go deep first, and let them come after you.
