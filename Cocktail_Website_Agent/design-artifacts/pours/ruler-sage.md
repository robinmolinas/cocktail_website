# Pour — Either Way · The Judge (ruler-sage)

## Cocktail

- **name:** Either Way
- **tagline:** Even the people you've ruled against trust you. Especially them.
- **glassware:** old-fashioned glass (rocks glass, about 300 ml), filled with ice cubes
- **contains:** `["gluten"]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 ml | blended Scotch whisky | any blend, 40-43%: malt and grain whisky together |
| 7.5 ml (1½ tsp) | malt syrup | 1 part barley malt syrup (from a jar, the baking shelf) to 2 parts rich sugar syrup, stirred smooth; up to 1¾ tsp if your malt syrup is dark |
| 2 dashes | aromatic bitters (Angostura) | |
| 1 | orange peel strip | squeezed over the top, then dropped in |

**method**
1. Make the malt syrup: stir 1 tablespoon of barley malt syrup into 2 tablespoons of rich sugar syrup (2 parts sugar to 1 part water by weight, dissolved warm) until no streaks are left. It keeps in the fridge for weeks.
2. Put the malt syrup and the bitters in the glass, add the whisky, and stir to mix.
3. Fill the glass with ice cubes and stir for about 20 seconds, until the outside of the glass is cold.
4. Squeeze the orange peel over the top so its oils fall on the drink, then drop it in.

**closingLine:** *Stir until you can't see the malt. Then say what you've decided, and let the argument go on without you.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*There's whisky in this glass that rival distillers once insisted wasn't whisky at all.*

**whoYouAre**
Two friends fall out, and sooner or later each of them tells you their side. You hear both out to the end, and you ask for the bits that don't flatter them. Then you tell them plainly what you think happened, and it's rarely what either of them hoped. And here's the odd part: when trouble comes round again, it's the one you disagreed with who calls first.

People assume plain speaking is easy for you. It isn't, not once. You want to be liked as much as anyone, and later, alone, you replay the conversation, half hoping someone will show you what you missed. What you won't put up with is the kind-sounding fudge, the "let's not make a fuss", because you know who pays for it, and it's never the person saying it. It's the quietest one there. With you, the quietest voice gets the same answer as the loudest, and it matters more than they ever tell you.

**yours**

1. For decades, distillers argued about a word. In the 1830s, whisky makers in the Lowlands took up a new kind of still, one that could run without stopping, and made a light, pure grain whisky cheap enough for city factory workers. The makers of malt whisky, from the old pot stills, said the new spirit wasn't whisky at all. They had age and tradition on their side. The grain side had the size: in 1877, six of the biggest grain distillers joined forces.

2. In 1908 a British commission sat and took evidence in person from across the spirits trade. Where it landed was plain. Whisky was a spirit distilled from cereal grain, its starch turned to sugar by malt, and nothing in that said which still. The new spirit was whisky. Blending stayed legal, and the commission set out what a label could claim.

3. That settled the law, not the argument. Over decades of marketing, the idea that the older whisky is the real one has largely won people over. The ruling stood anyway. That will be familiar to you: a call that went against the side with age and tradition, and then held, liked or not. I like to think the people it looked after most weren't in the room at all: whoever was buying the cheaper bottle.

4. The cocktail I've made for you starts from one of the most renowned classics of all, the Old-Fashioned. I make it with blended Scotch: malt and grain whisky together, the two whiskies that argument was about. Only the sugar is different. I sweeten it with a syrup made partly from barley malt, which the commission's definition asked of every whisky, whatever still it came from. That's as a nod to how you treat people: one rule, whichever side they're on. The malt makes it rounder and warmer than plain sugar would. Angostura goes in as well, the glass is a heavy one packed with ice, and last, orange peel is squeezed above it for its scent.

5. Once everyone's been heard and you've made the call, you've done the fair part. You don't also owe the room its agreement. Some of them will never come round, and that doesn't make the call any less fair. If the evidence ever changes, it'll find you, because you're the one who always asks for it. Until then, take the evening off from deciding. You've earned the nights when nobody needs an answer from you.
