# Pour — Show Your Working · The Genius (explorer-sage)

## Cocktail

- **name:** Show Your Working
- **tagline:** You'll wait years for the real answer. Then you give it away.
- **glassware:** a tall Zombie glass, filled with crushed ice
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 30 ml | gold Puerto Rican rum | the first of the three rums Berry printed |
| 30 ml | gold Jamaican rum | the second |
| 20 ml | Lemon Hart 151 Demerara rum (75.5%), recommended, or any Demerara (Guyanese) overproof rum of about 70-75% | the third, named in the recipe Berry printed; very strong |
| 27.5 ml | fresh lime juice | |
| 15 ml | fresh grapefruit juice | half of "Don's Mix", written out |
| 7.5 ml | cinnamon syrup, made at home | the other half of "Don's Mix" |
| 17.5 ml | falernum, made at home: a lime and clove syrup with rum in it, as they make it in Barbados, but with no almond, no nutmeg and no ginger | a bought falernum may hold almond or nutmeg, so this one is made where you can see what goes in |
| 1 teaspoon (5 ml) | grenadine, plain, bought | |
| 1 dash | Angostura bitters | |
| 6 drops | Pernod (the anise liqueur, not absinthe) | |
| 1 | mint sprig | |

**method**
1. **The falernum (10 minutes, then a day; makes about 375 ml, enough for about 20 drinks).** With a vegetable peeler, take the green zest off 4 limes, leaving the white pith behind. Put the zest and 12 whole cloves in a clean lidded container with 150 ml of the gold Jamaican rum, and leave it at room temperature for 24 hours. Strain it. In a small pan, warm 200 g of sugar with 100 ml of water, stirring, just until the sugar dissolves, then let it cool. Stir the syrup into the strained rum and pour it into a clean bottle. Write everything that went in on the bottle. It keeps in the fridge for a few months.
2. **The cinnamon syrup (5 minutes, then 30 minutes off the heat).** Warm 200 g of sugar in 200 ml of water with 3 cinnamon sticks, stirring until the sugar dissolves, then let it simmer for 2 minutes. Take it off the heat and leave it for 30 minutes, then strain. It keeps in the fridge for about two weeks.
3. Fill the tall glass with crushed ice so it chills while you mix.
4. Put everything except the mint in a shaker. The grapefruit juice and the cinnamon syrup go in separately: together they're "Don's Mix", two parts grapefruit to one of cinnamon.
5. Fill the shaker with ice cubes, close it and shake hard for about 10 seconds.
6. Strain over the crushed ice, and top the glass up with more crushed ice.
7. Slap the mint sprig once between your palms to wake its scent, then stand it in the ice.

**closingLine:** *Write everything that's in the falernum on its bottle. Then ask your oldest question out loud.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*Once, if you asked what was in drinks like this, you'd hear: rum and fruit juice.*

**whoYouAre**
Years ago, someone gave you an answer that was perfectly true and told you nothing. You nodded, and kept the question. It has come along ever since, at the back of an ordinary week, and now and then another piece falls into place. Nobody would guess that one of your questions is older than some of your friendships.

You've stopped mentioning it, because people expect a question to be settled or dropped by now. Some take you for a know-it-all. It's nearly the opposite: you can't rest inside something you aren't allowed to understand. What frightens you is being stuck for good with an answer you can't get behind. And once you do understand, you lay all of it out, plainly, so nobody else has to take it on trust. People get the whole answer from you, free, and every year it cost you was worth it.

**yours**

1. In 1979, at twenty-one, Jeff Berry started going to tiki bars on his own. At the Tiki-Ti on Sunset Boulevard he asked the old barman, Ray Buhen: "What's in this drink?" Whatever the drink, Ray gave the same reply: "Rum and fruit juice." Berry heard it in bar after bar. Ray wasn't being difficult: he had once worked for Donn Beach, who created the Zombie at his Hollywood bar, and Beach had told him never to give away the bar's trade secrets, in case rival bars copied them. So by the time Berry started asking, many of the old tiki recipes, the Zombie's among them, had become a mystery.
2. Berry had a day job in films. His off-hours went on the question. He read microfilm in libraries, tracked down retired barmen, and their children when the barmen were gone, and learned to read recipes written in code. In 1998 he put it in print that the Zombie's recipe was still unknown. Getting to the bottom of the Zombie took ten years and three books, he said. He found an early recipe in a self-published cookbook from 1950, and in a former headwaiter's notebook, what he calls the original. And he printed a Zombie with every part named.
3. The answer that kept Berry going, I like to think, wasn't a no. He could have lived with a no. It was "rum and fruit juice": polite, true, and empty. You've been handed that answer too, more than once. A shrug doesn't end a question for you. It starts one. And his narrowed the way yours do, from every drink on the menu down to one: the Zombie, and then he stayed with it for years, beside an ordinary job, until he could write the whole thing down.
4. The cocktail I've made for you is a Zombie, the first famous tiki drink and still one of the great ones. Every part Berry printed is in it, and your recipe names each one. Two of them, grapefruit and cinnamon, travel under one old name, Don's Mix, a classic tiki syrup. Here they go in one at a time. The balance is mine: more of the lime, the grapefruit, the cinnamon and the falernum, so it's brighter and fresher. And I make the falernum myself (a sweet syrup of lime, cloves and rum that goes into many tiki drinks), as a nod to all the years he spent asking what was in things: with a bought bottle, you'd have to take the maker's word for it. It's shaken, then poured into a tall Zombie glass full of crushed ice, and topped with mint. It's a big drink and a strong one, made to be taken slowly.
5. One more thing from Berry's story. On his own, he got "rum and fruit juice". When he went in with Ted Haigh, a loud, sociable fellow historian whom one writer called Berry's opposite, he left with a recipe. "The first Don the Beachcomber recipes I got were because of Ted," Berry said. So take the question you've carried longest, and ask it out loud to the person you know who's least like you, the one who'd ask a stranger anything. Ask before you're sure it's a good question. You may get there years sooner, and on the day it all falls into place, you won't be the only one who's glad.

- **The question's true order (Hester C2):** at twenty-one, in 1979, Berry asked "What's in this drink?" of every drink and heard "rum and fruit juice" (Proper p. 258).
