# Pour — In Your Own Hand · The Muse (lover-creator)

## Cocktail

- **name:** In Your Own Hand
- **tagline:** People make their best things around you. So do you.
- **glassware:** two nick-and-nora glasses (small, stemmed), chilled, each rim dipped in coarse sugar on the outside
- **contains:** `[]`

**recipe**

| amount | item | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 30 ml (1 oz) | London dry gin | any; the card names none |
| 30 ml (1 oz) | fresh orange juice | |
| 30 ml (1 oz) | fresh grapefruit juice | can interfere with some medicines: if you take any, check first |
| 15 ml (½ oz) | fresh lime juice | |
| 15 ml (½ oz) | fresh lemon juice | plus the cut lemon for the rims |
| 22.5 ml (¾ oz) | mint syrup (below) | the one thing I added: the mint from his Bailey |
| a saucer | coarse sugar | for the rims |

**glassware:** two nick-and-nora glasses (small, stemmed), chilled, each rim dipped in coarse sugar on the outside
**contains:** `[]` (veto-free)
**serves:** 2 (his measures fill two small glasses, about 97 ml each)

**Mint syrup (makes about 400 ml, keeps a few days in the fridge):** 250 g white sugar, 250 ml water and a big handful of fresh mint leaves (about 20 g, stalks off). Blend until the sugar has dissolved, then pour through a fine sieve.

**method**
1. Make the mint syrup: blend the sugar, water and mint leaves until no grains are left, then pour it through a fine sieve and chill it.
2. Put two small stemmed glasses in the freezer.
3. Cut a lemon in half. Wet the outside edge of each glass with the cut side, then dip that edge into a saucer of coarse sugar. Keep the sugar on the outside, so none falls into the drink.
4. Squeeze the orange, grapefruit, lime and lemon fresh, and measure them into a shaker with the gin and the mint syrup.
5. Fill the shaker right to the top with ice and shake hard for about ten seconds.
6. Pour it through a fine strainer, half into each glass.

**closingLine:** *Pour both glasses. Hand one over with something you've made, and put your name on it.*

## Reading

**epigraph**
*'Juice of A Few Flowers' sounds like a joke. The handwritten card says otherwise.*

**whoYouAre**
Friends show you things first: the song before it has words, the first chapter, the dish they've never cooked for anyone else. Something in the way you pay attention makes them want to finish it, and often they do. People are braver with their own work when you're around, and that's a rare thing to give. Sometimes you even turn up in it: a line in the song, a character who talks the way you talk.

You make things too, and there's usually something of yours, half done, that nobody has seen. It's lovely to be someone's reason, and it feels safer than being judged on your own work. But the you in their songs is their idea of you, and an idea is easy to love from a distance. People have made beautiful things out of you. Very few have asked what you're making.

**yours**

1. In the 1920s, an American couple, Gerald and Sara Murphy, had a favourite little beach near Antibes, and it became a gathering place for their friends: Picasso, Hemingway, the Fitzgeralds. When F. Scott Fitzgerald wrote *Tender Is the Night*, he drew its main couple partly from the Murphys. And Gerald had a line about his cocktails: they were "just the juice of a few flowers". It sounds like a charming man's joke. Maybe that's all it was.

2. But among the Murphys' papers at Yale there's a card in Gerald's handwriting, headed "Juice of A Few Flowers". An ounce of orange, an ounce of grapefruit, half an ounce each of lime and lemon, an ounce of gin, shaken, in a glass with its rim wet with lemon and dipped in coarse sugar. He was a painter in his own right, too. He showed in Paris from 1923, and seven of his fourteen paintings survive. In one of them, *Cocktail*, the lid of a cigar box alone took him four months.

3. The charming version is the one that travelled: the man in the novel, the lovely line. The exact part, the half-ounces and the four months, was his, and you have to go looking for it. I like to think that card is what he'd most have liked someone to ask him about. I suspect you have a card like that too.

4. The cocktail I've made for you keeps every one of his measures, and his sugar on the rim. But I keep that sugar on the outside of the glass, where it sweetens your lips and not the drink, so it needed a little more, inside. I made that a mint syrup, and the mint comes from a cocktail he called the Bailey. In a letter, he wrote that he'd invented it, "as were a great many other good things". So this drink comes from two of his recipes, and joining them is my idea: I put the mint in as a nod to that sentence, where he simply says he made it. It's light, bright and properly sharp, four fresh juices and a little gin, and it fills two small glasses.

5. So when someone shows you what they're working on, show them something of yours back. Not the polished version, and not so they'll praise it: the unfinished one, in your own hand. Let one person see the maker, not the reason. That's how you get known up close. And since hardly anyone asks, I will: what are you making?

**closingLine** (Tomás's; I sign it)
*Pour both glasses. Hand one over with something you've made, and put your name on it.*
