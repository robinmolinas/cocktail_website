# Dionysus: useful questions from the synthetic stress test

16 October 2026 · Discussion draft · No product decisions or implementation changes

## Judgment

The report supplies plausible questions about expectations, recognition, attention, preparation and sharing. It supplies no observed user behaviour or evidence of commercial viability. Keep the questions; validate them against the experience before redesigning it.

The most useful question is whether guests understand what Dionysus promises, then feel that promise has been fulfilled. The most useful improvement opportunities are clearer expectations about personalisation, a legible sensory payoff, confidence about sharing, and practical preparation information. Mobile interaction needs direct testing rather than the report's blanket conclusion that phones are unsuitable.

This assessment compares the supplied report with the current local application source, current product brief, selected imported recipes and earlier recorded mobile work. It does not establish what is deployed, reproduce a browser journey, or report new participant tests. October 8 delivery snapshots are older than the current source; the source supersedes their statements that matching remains fixed to the pilot.

## What the report can support

The participants discussed a short description, without experiencing the actual site or drinks. Their reactions can reveal what that description suggests and generate possible objections. They cannot establish the readability of a screen, measured loading time, emotional effect of a reading, taste, abandonment, sharing, payment, or venue operations.

The numbers of agents and interaction rounds do not establish prevalence among real guests. There is no raw conversation, original simulation seed or traceable dataset in this attachment with which to audit the summary. Some reactions may reflect the personas and scenarios supplied to the model. Repetition across the report is not independent corroboration.

Specific unsupported claims to discard as evidence:

- Threefold sharing, fourfold sharing and a 2.4× premium-purchase effect. The document provides no provenance for the supposed owner observations or “Mixology-Meets-Quiz” data.
- A 70% completion requirement implying that Dionysus currently falls below it. A proposed target cannot establish an actual result. The product brief's own completion/save aspirations are also explicitly unmeasured.
- $5–15 willingness to pay, a $10 keepsake purchase, and a universal additional-preparation limit of 30 seconds. These are simulated propositions, not pricing or operational research.
- “High rates” of abandonment, “uniform” rejection and other population-sounding findings without observations, denominators or a sampling basis.
- A sensory memory triggered by bergamot and smoked tea, followed by an immediate text to a friend. The simulation did not supply those experiences.
- “Chaos Elixir,” repeatedly used as if it were an evaluated Dionysus pour. I found no such drink in the current imported catalogue or the product/spec sources checked.

The report also changes emphasis: its opening predicts widespread failure, while later sections describe transformative recognition and strong sharing. Both are imagined scenarios. Neither establishes which outcome is likely. Two earlier lists cut off mid-sentence, and the attachment ends mid-sentence under venue failure; its promised validation plan is not present in a completed form.

## Judge success against the product we are making

The current brief defines a **non-commercial portfolio experience**: an authored cocktail and personal reading, craft, recognition, and an invitation to share or make the drink. A guest who enjoys the journey and feels seen can have a successful experience without buying ingredients. A single satisfying visit can also fulfil the purpose; a habitual return loop is not an established requirement.

Useful outcomes to investigate separately:

1. **Understanding:** what guests expect to receive and how long they expect to spend.
2. **Journey quality:** whether they can proceed comfortably, including on a phone.
3. **Recognition:** what specifically fits, misses or feels interchangeable in the reading.
4. **Sensory understanding:** whether they can imagine the drink and whether it sounds appealing.
5. **Keeping and sharing:** whether they choose an action, whether it works, and what the recipient understands.
6. **Preparation:** whether interested guests can realistically make it, if making is their goal.

These are related outcomes, not a compulsory chain. Preparation, payment and repeat visits should not become invented entry requirements for enjoying Dionysus.

## Changes worth exploring, in order

### 1. Align the personalisation promise with the delivered reading

Start with a short expectation test before changing the prose. “Made for you alone” may be read as theatrical language, or as a literal promise that every recipe and sentence is newly made. We do not know which.

If guests expect more than the current result delivers, revise the introduction and trace instruction to describe a creative reflection honestly, while retaining Dionysus's voice. A possible direction for discussion is a cocktail that reflects the guest's choices, rather than a claim that the recipe exists only for them. Do not introduce scores, model explanations or clinical labels into the journey.

For the reading itself, ask guests to identify a specific passage that fits and one that does not. Their answer is more useful than a general compliment. A later answer-linked passage is worth considering only if it adds a defensible connection to the choices actually used. Existing plans for bounded live additions are separate work, not evidence that this is currently delivered or that an LLM would fix generic writing.

### 2. Validate the sensory arrival we have already added

The three words directly address the distance between seeing a cocktail and imagining drinking it. Preserve their drink-led discipline: ingredients, aroma, texture, flavour and the aura of the served drink; no borrowed personality adjective or title echo.

Show a handful of actual reveals to people who are unfamiliar with the ingredients. Ask them to describe the expected sip in their own words. If “Velvety · Herbal · Bittersweet” communicates a smooth botanical drink with a bitter-sweet edge, it is doing useful work. If a particular word is repeatedly misunderstood, revise that word.

This checks language, not actual taste accuracy. Taste claims still need physical tasting; the simulation cannot validate them. Add another sensory sentence only where the trio and existing prose leave a demonstrated gap.

### 3. Make sharing understandable before adding more content

Two different questions need testing: does the owner know what a friend will see, and does the friend understand why they might want their own experience?

If the first fails, add a quiet explanation alongside the existing share choices: the cocktail and dedication travel; their answers and reading are not included in the normal gift. Keep the actions at the bottom, as already directed.

If the second fails, strengthen the short public invitation using the drink's sensory appeal and what the recipient can discover for themselves. Test the wording against the invitation already present. Do not reveal the owner's reading to solve a recruitment hypothesis. A personal note could be something the sender adds in their messaging app; a new note feature is not necessary to test that idea.

### 4. Retest concrete phone friction

The older mobile audit is valuable because it recorded actual interaction probes. It also predates subsequent fixes. Do not reopen every historical finding as if it remains current.

One concern still visible in the source is that the gravity slider starts a drag on pointer-down and commits on pointer-up. Check real-thumb use to see whether taps or attempted swipes submit unintended choices. If reproduced, consider an explicit confirmation while keeping the continuous 0–100 meaning. Also test the trace with a real phone keyboard, orientation changes and return from a notification. These are focused checks, not evidence of abandonment.

Preserve intentional pauses and native video speed unless observed use shows a specific beat is unclear or unnecessarily long. The appropriate response to a control problem is to improve the control.

### 5. Describe the work needed to make the drink

The current recipes demonstrate different kinds of effort:

- **Good for Years:** mezcal and marmalade, shaken in a jar. Simple execution; whether mezcal is available depends on the guest.
- **Quite Alive:** gin, sweet vermouth and fernet, stirred. Bottle access and taste preference matter more than elaborate preparation.
- **Down the Line:** a long ingredient list and a five-minute shake. Technique and effort are central to its authored story.
- **Hoping You'd Come:** apples roasted for 30–45 minutes, then cooled; the recipe asks for preparation an hour or more ahead.

A compact note can set an honest expectation, such as “Roast the apple ahead; assemble when your guest arrives.” These are explanatory additions for review, not instructions to simplify the recipes. Avoid applying a universal difficulty label based only on ingredient count. Recipe substitutions and alternate versions are outside the current launch contract and would need separate mixology review.

## A small real-user check

Run a formative round with around six people drawn from the intended audience, including guests unfamiliar with cocktails and someone mildly sceptical of personality experiences. Include quiet phone use as well as desktop use. This is a practical first round, not a sample from which to estimate conversion rates.

Use the actual current build. Let guests proceed without coaching; ask follow-up questions after the relevant moment so we do not teach them the intended interpretation.

| Moment | Look for | Question after observation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Landing | Understanding of time, output and personalisation | “What did you expect Dionysus to make or tell you?” |
| Journey | Hesitation, accidental choices, unclear waits, phone interruptions | “Where, if anywhere, were you unsure what to do?” |
| Trace | Whether it felt required, and what effect the guest expected | “What did you expect to happen with what you wrote?” |
| Reveal | Specific recognition versus broad approval | “Which passage fits you most, and which fits least?” |
| Cocktail | Sensory understanding and appeal, separately from self-recognition | “What do you imagine the first sip would be like?” |
| Keep/share | An unprompted action, or a clear reason to leave without one | Observe first. Then ask what they expected the action to preserve or send. |
| Gift recipient | What a new person understands without the owner's explanation | “What do you think happened for the person who sent this, and what would happen if you started?” |
| Recipe, if interested | The actual obstacle to making this specific drink | “How would you go about making it?” |

Test sharing with willing real recipients; do not send messages on anyone's behalf. Record observed actions separately from statements of intent. Opening a share sheet does not prove a message was sent, and opening print does not prove a file was saved. A recipient starting is different from completing; making a drink is a separate follow-up.

Use consented session notes for this first round rather than introducing production tracking. When more than one independent guest has the same concrete misunderstanding, make a targeted change and check it again. Report counts with the small denominator and the context; do not convert them into product-wide forecasts.
